## Introduction

Twelve questions from the BGP fundamentals module, first part. Answer out loud before the answer comes.

They run in the order of the lessons: what BGP is for, sessions and the state machine, updates, attributes and the routing tables, and iBGP loops and next hops. Each one has four options, A to D, then a few seconds for you to commit to one.

## Question 1

The gateway gw2.ams, in AS 64500, receives Meridian's own prefix, 203.0.113.0 slash 24, from ISP2, with the AS path 64502 64496 64501 64500. gw2.ams itself never announced this prefix to anyone. What does it do?

A, accepts it as a backup path in case the iBGP route is withdrawn. B, discards it, because its own AS number is already in the path. C, accepts it, because gw2.ams itself never sent this route out. D, discards it, because a four-AS path exceeds BGP's hop limit.

[think]

The answer is B: it discards it, because its own AS number is already in the path.

The loop check is per AS: 64500 in the path proves the route has already been through Meridian, whichever router sent it. Keeping it as a backup would invite exactly the loop the rule prevents. BGP has no hop limit on paths; the AS path itself is the loop detector.

## Question 2

Meridian prepends its AS three extra times on announcements to ISP2. ISP2 now has a four-AS path from its customer, Meridian, and a three-AS path through its transit provider. Which does ISP2 most likely use?

A, the customer path, because customer routes get a higher local preference. B, both equally, because BGP load-shares across the two paths. C, the transit path, because providers always rank above customers. D, the transit path, because the shorter AS path wins.

[think]

The answer is A: the customer path, because customer routes get a higher local preference.

ISP2 sets local preference by relationship, and local preference is compared before AS path length, so its paying customer's route wins regardless of prepending. Choosing the provider route would mean paying transit to reach a customer who pays ISP2. And BGP does not load-share across paths with different attributes by default.

## Question 3

In an eBGP-underlay fabric, leaf3.ams and leaf4.ams were both configured as AS 65103. All BGP sessions are Established. What do you expect to see?

A, the spines rejecting both leaves for having a duplicate router ID. B, every leaf losing its routes to every other leaf in the site. C, leaf3.ams and leaf4.ams unable to reach each other, and everything else fine. D, both leaves' sessions to the spines cycling with a Bad Peer AS notification.

[think]

The answer is C: leaf3.ams and leaf4.ams unable to reach each other, and everything else fine.

Each leaf receives the other's routes with the AS path 65100 65103, finds its own AS and discards them as a loop. Routes to and from every other leaf carry different numbers and are accepted. The spines are configured to expect AS 65103 on both sessions, so there is no Bad Peer AS error, and router IDs are a separate value.

## Question 4

A BGP session on a leaf has shown Active for twenty minutes. What does that tell you?

A, the session is exchanging routes and the table is still loading. B, TCP to port 179 is not completing, so the router keeps retrying. C, OPENs were exchanged and the router is waiting for a KEEPALIVE. D, an operator shut the session down and it is waiting to be enabled.

[think]

The answer is B: TCP to port 179 is not completing, so the router keeps retrying.

Active means there is no TCP connection: the attempt failed, and the router listens and retries when the ConnectRetry timer fires. Waiting for a KEEPALIVE after the OPENs is OpenConfirm, a deliberate shutdown is Idle, marked admin, and a loading table is Established. The cause is below BGP: reachability, source address, filters, keys or TTL.

## Question 5

Why does BGP need a hold timer when it already runs over TCP, which retransmits lost data?

A, TCP has no way of retransmitting data on long-lived sessions. B, hold timers are needed to rate-limit how fast UPDATEs are sent. C, TCP keepalives would add too much traffic on fabric links. D, the kernel acknowledges segments even when the BGP process has hung.

[think]

The answer is D: the kernel acknowledges segments even when the BGP process has hung.

TCP acknowledgements are generated by the kernel, so a router whose BGP daemon is wedged still looks alive to TCP, and Linux's own keepalive would not probe for two hours anyway. Only the absence of BGP messages shows the process is dead. TCP retransmits fine; the timer is about liveness, not loss.

## Question 6

A session to an ISP comes up, receives a few hundred prefixes, and is reset with "hold time expired" every three minutes. The negotiated hold time is 180 seconds. What is the most likely cause?

A, an MTU mismatch that drops only full-size segments. B, a duplicate BGP identifier between the two routers. C, a TCP MD5 key mismatch between the two routers. D, a wrong peer AS number configured on one side.

[think]

The answer is A: an MTU mismatch that drops only full-size segments.

Small packets, the handshake, the OPENs and the KEEPALIVEs, pass, so the session reaches Established. The full-table UPDATEs fill maximum-size segments that a lower-MTU hop drops silently, TCP keeps retransmitting them, keepalives queue behind them, and the hold timer expires one hold time later. A key mismatch would stop TCP entirely, and a wrong AS or a duplicate identifier would refuse the OPEN, so the session would never be Established.

## Question 7

A leaf shows a BGP route to 192.0.2.0 slash 24 as best, learned over iBGP, but the routing table forwards that prefix using a static route someone added. What explains it?

A, the static route has a lower administrative distance, and wins the RIB. B, the FIB always prefers static routes over any dynamic protocol's route. C, the static route was received more recently, so it wins by being newest. D, the BGP path loses because its MED is higher than the static route's metric.

[think]

The answer is A: the static route has a lower administrative distance, and wins the RIB.

BGP's best path is offered to the RIB manager, which compares routes for the same prefix from different sources by administrative distance: 1 for the static against 200 for iBGP. The BGP path is shown as best but not installed, a RIB-failure on some platforms. MED is compared only between BGP paths, and the FIB installs whatever the RIB selected, not a fixed protocol order.

## Question 8

gw2.ams sets a local preference of 300 on the routes it announces to ISP2, hoping ISP2 will prefer Meridian's link. What happens?

A, ISP2 copies it into the MED, since MED is the external version of local preference. B, ISP2 prefers the link, because local preference outranks every other attribute. C, ISP2 resets the session, because local preference on eBGP is a protocol error. D, nothing: local preference is not sent over eBGP, and is ignored if received.

[think]

The answer is D: nothing. Local preference is not sent over eBGP, and is ignored if received.

Local preference expresses an AS's own preference and travels on iBGP only. To influence how a neighbour sends traffic to you, you use MED towards one neighbour with several links, AS path prepending, more-specific prefixes, or the neighbour's own communities. It is not a session-reset error, and nothing converts it into MED.

## Question 9

A monitoring dashboard shows that ISP2 sent zero routes for 198.51.100.0 slash 24, and the team concludes ISP2 never announced it. What should you check before accepting that?

A, whether the session is Established, since routes only appear after that. B, whether the dashboard reads the router's FIB rather than its BGP table. C, whether the count is post-policy, since filtered prefixes would vanish from it. D, whether the hold time is short enough for the route to have arrived.

[think]

The answer is C: whether the count is post-policy, since filtered prefixes would vanish from it.

Many counters and show commands report the post-policy Adj-RIB-In, where a prefix rejected by import policy looks identical to one never sent. A pre-policy view, from soft reconfiguration, a hidden-route display or BMP's pre-policy feed, tells the two apart. Session state and hold time do not explain a single missing prefix.

## Question 10

Why does a router not re-advertise a route learned from one iBGP peer to another iBGP peer?

A, re-advertising would double the keepalive load on each session. B, the next hop would have to be rewritten at every iBGP hop. C, iBGP sessions cannot carry more than one copy of each prefix. D, the AS path does not change inside an AS, so loops would go unseen.

[think]

The answer is D: the AS path does not change inside an AS, so loops would go unseen.

iBGP does not prepend the local AS, so every copy of a route inside the AS has the same AS path, and a loop cannot be detected from it. Forbidding relay means a route crosses at most one iBGP session, and the side effect is the full mesh. Next hops are deliberately left unchanged across iBGP, and keepalives have nothing to do with route relay.

## Question 11

gw1.ams shows its iBGP session to gw2.ams as Established, with a million prefixes received from it, but none is used, and gw1.ams sends all traffic through ISP1. The paths' next hop is 198.51.100.0, ISP2's link address. What is the most likely fix?

A, a higher local preference on routes learned from gw2.ams. B, allowas-in on gw1.ams for the iBGP session. C, a shorter hold time on the session between gw1.ams and gw2.ams. D, next-hop-self on gw2.ams towards its iBGP peers.

[think]

The answer is D: next-hop-self on gw2.ams towards its iBGP peers.

iBGP leaves the external next hop unchanged, and IS-IS does not carry the ISP's link, so gw1.ams cannot resolve 198.51.100.0 and the paths are ineligible. Next-hop-self makes gw2.ams advertise its own loopback, which IS-IS carries. A higher local preference cannot make an ineligible path win, allowas-in is about AS loops, and the hold time has nothing to do with resolution.

## Question 12

gw2.ams loses power, and its loopback, 10.255.1.202 slash 32, disappears from IS-IS. gw1.ams also has an IS-IS route for 10.255.0.0 slash 16 through the spines. What happens to traffic for gw2.ams's prefixes?

A, it moves to ISP1 at once, because the slash 32 is gone. B, it is sent to the spines and dropped until the hold timer expires. C, it loops between gw1.ams and the spines until the TTL expires. D, it is dropped on gw1.ams, because the forwarding entry is deleted.

[think]

The answer is B: it is sent to the spines and dropped until the hold timer expires.

10.255.1.202 still matches the slash 16, so the next hop still resolves and the path stays best. gw1.ams keeps forwarding towards the spines and a gateway that is gone, and the packets are dropped there. Only when the iBGP hold timer expires are gw2.ams's paths removed. Resolving loopback next hops only through slash 32 routes would have made the path unusable at once and moved traffic to ISP1.

## Recap

Three ideas kept coming back. Loop prevention is built on the AS path: a router discards any route carrying its own AS, which is why duplicate AS numbers silently cut leaves off, and why iBGP, where the path never changes, must not relay routes at all. A session that looks healthy can still be wrong: Active means TCP never completed, a post-policy count hides filtered prefixes, and an Established iBGP session can deliver a million paths with an unresolvable next hop. And attributes have scopes: local preference stays inside the AS, while the next hop is left alone on iBGP until next-hop-self rewrites it.
