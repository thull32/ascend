## Introduction

Most engineers negotiate the wrong thing, at the wrong time, with the wrong tool. They push for a few percent more base salary, which is usually the least flexible part of an offer. They do it after saying "that sounds great" on the phone, when the company already believes the deal is done. And they use a vague "is there any flexibility?", which is easy to answer with a token increase. Meanwhile the choices that move a four-year total most, the level, the equity grant, the sign-on bonus, the vesting shape and the refresher practice, go unexamined.

Negotiating well is not about being aggressive. Recruiters negotiate every week and you do it a few times a decade. The way to close that gap is mechanism. Every number here is an illustrative round number, not market data, and the terms are American because most public compensation data uses them. Structures, taxes and norms differ by country.

Four ideas. Why level is the biggest lever. How an offer is approved, and why base is the hardest thing to move. How equity actually pays over the years. And the order and wording of the conversations.

## Level is the biggest lever

Compensation is organised by level. For each level, job family and location, a company keeps a base salary range, an equity guideline and a bonus target. Each range has a minimum, a midpoint and a maximum.

Picture two illustrative bands, in index points. The mid-level band runs from 80 to 120, with its midpoint at 100. The senior band runs from 105 to 155, midpoint 130. The company offers you mid-level at 100. A strong negotiation moves it to 112, a 12 percent gain, and going above 120 needs an exception that is rarely granted. Now suppose you are levelled senior instead, at that band's midpoint: 130. That is 30 percent more, and above the entire mid-level range.

The bands overlap, so a new senior at the bottom of their band can earn less than a mid-level engineer at the top of theirs. The senior is still ahead: future raises, refreshers and bonus targets are keyed to the senior level, and the next promotion is to staff. The rule: no in-band negotiation beats being in the right band.

So influence the level before numbers. Before the loop, tell the recruiter your target with scope evidence. During it, tell stories at that scope. After a down-level, ask which rounds drove it and whether one more round could revisit it. If not, ask about the promotion path and timeline, and decide deliberately. Sometimes a strong team at the lower level beats your alternatives.

## How an offer is approved

Between "you were approved" and the phone call, the level is set, the band is looked up by job family, level and location, and a package is drafted inside the guidelines. A package inside the guidelines is approved quickly. Anything outside them, base above the maximum, equity above the guideline, a large sign-on, is an exception that goes to a compensation team or senior leader with a written justification.

That justification is where your information goes. A competing offer with its level, components and deadline, unvested equity you would forfeit, a bonus you would lose: these are facts an approver can act on. "The candidate wants more" is not. Each counter goes back through the same chain, which is why a revised number takes days, and why a third round costs goodwill.

Why is base the least flexible? Before I tell you, think about what makes a base increase expensive for the company beyond its first year.

[pause]

Base recurs every year. Raises and the bonus are percentages of it. And it is compared with colleagues at the same level: a new hire paid above the team's existing seniors creates compression, which compensation teams guard against. An equity grant is paid once from a budget, and a sign-on is one-off cash protected by a clawback. So the same first-year cost is easier to approve as equity or sign-on, and specific asks for those reach yes faster.

The recruiter's job is to close the hire. That makes them the person who carries your evidence to the approvers, not your opponent. Give them something they can write down.

## The components

A few traps in the components. Target is not payout: a 15 percent bonus target paid at a 0.8 multiplier is 12 percent of base, so ask what recent payouts have been and whether year one is prorated. Sign-on bonuses come with clawbacks, commonly twelve months, and some agreements demand back the gross amount, which is more than you received after tax. And relocation support is generally taxable income in the US.

Now RSUs, restricted stock units. Each is a promise of one share, delivered when it vests. Trace one illustrative grant. The offer states 400 thousand dollars over four years. The company converts that to shares using its own price method, often an average over a window. At a conversion price of 200 dollars, that is 2 thousand shares. If the stock trades at 220 on your first day, your grant is worth 440 thousand; at 180, it is worth 360 thousand. The share count never changes; from conversion on, you are exposed to the price.

Then the cliff. Commonly, nothing vests for twelve months, then a quarter at once: 500 shares. The rest vests quarterly or monthly. Leave in month eleven and you keep nothing. Leave in month thirteen and you keep 500 shares. The even quarter-a-year shape is common, not universal: back-loaded schedules like 5, 15, 40, 40 percent and front-loaded ones have both been publicly reported at large companies.

## Refreshers and the four-year cliff

After you join, most large public companies grant refreshers: new grants, commonly yearly, each with its own four-year vesting. So your equity in any year is the sum of layers from several grants.

Trace it in thousands. An initial grant of 400 vests at 100 a year. A refresher of 100 each year adds a slice of 25 a year for four years. Year one: 100. Year two: 125. Year three: 150. Year four: 175. Year five: the initial grant ends, and equity drops to 100. This is the four-year cliff that surprises people who never modelled it. In steady state, annual equity equals the annual refresher, because four overlapping quarter-slices of equal grants add up to one grant. With refreshers of 50 instead, year four is 137 and a half, and year five is just 50.

So refresher practice matters as much as the initial grant. Ask each company what a typical refresher is for a solid performer at your level, and when the first one comes.

## Comparing offers year by year

Two illustrative offers. Offer A: 210 thousand base, a 15 percent bonus target, 400 thousand of stock vesting evenly, and a 30 thousand sign-on. Offer B: 230 thousand base, no bonus, 360 thousand of stock vesting back-loaded at 5, 15, 40 and 40 percent, and sign-ons of 75 thousand in year one and 50 thousand in year two.

The four-year totals are within 1 percent of each other: about 1 million 396 thousand for A, 1 million 405 thousand for B. But the offers are very different. A pays earlier: if you leave after two years, A is worth 56 thousand more. B pays more only if you stay, and its advantage sits in years three and four. And the ranking depends on the stock. If the stock falls 30 percent and A's bonus pays at 80 percent, B comes out 46 thousand ahead, because its cash protects it. If the stock rises 30 percent, A edges ahead by 3 thousand. Neither includes refreshers, which by year three can outweigh the difference. Compare offers year by year, never by headline totals.

Two warnings on what happens after vest. In the US, RSUs are generally taxed as ordinary income when they vest, and employers commonly withhold at a flat supplemental rate, federally 22 percent, which can be below your marginal rate. The gap arrives as a bill when you file. And startup options are different again. Investors' preferred shares usually carry a liquidation preference. In the illustrative example, investors hold a 150 million dollar preference, and the company sells for 200 million. Investors take their 150, common shareholders share the remaining 50, about 67 cents a share, below a one dollar strike. A sale that sounds like success leaves your options worth nothing. And when you leave, you typically have 90 days to find the cash to exercise.

## The order of conversations

The sequence matters. At the recruiter screen, state your target level and ask for the range; do not name a number first. When the level is decided, ask which level the offer is at and what drove it, before any numbers. On the verbal offer call, show enthusiasm, ask for everything in writing, and give a date. Do not accept, reject or counter on the call. With the written offer, model it year by year. Then counter once, by email, with one specific, evidenced ask. Expect one round, occasionally two; a third reads as re-trading. And never resign before the written offer and checks are cleared.

Mention other processes as soon as they exist, because timelines move packets. Share numbers when you counter, as evidence for a specific ask. Numbers go through the recruiter; with the hiring manager, talk scope, level and enthusiasm.

The counter email opens with enthusiasm, gives facts an approver can use, such as the competing offer's year-one and four-year average and the equity you would forfeit, asks for specific amounts on the flexible components, the stock grant and a sign-on, and makes a conditional commitment: if you can do this, I am ready to sign this week. Only make that commitment if it is true.

## In the interview

Here is a follow-up the lesson expects. The recruiter says: this is already at the top of the band.

[pause]

"Understood. Is there room on the equity grant or a sign-on, which sit outside the base range? And given the scope feedback, is a level review possible?" The wrong answer is pushing harder on base, which cannot move without an exception.

And another: what would it take for you to sign today? "I'm not in a position to sign today. I'll review the written offer and give you an answer by Friday." Naming a number on the spot, before you have the written details or have modelled the vesting, is the mistake.

## Recap

Five things to remember. Settle the level before the numbers; no in-band win beats the right band. Give the recruiter evidence an approver can act on, and aim your ask at equity and sign-on, because base recurs and is compared with peers. Your share count is fixed at conversion, and refreshers stack into layers, so model the year-five drop. Compare offers year by year, under leave-early and stock-down scenarios. And never accept on the phone; counter once, in writing, specifically.
