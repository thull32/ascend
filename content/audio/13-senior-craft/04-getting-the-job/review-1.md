## Introduction

Twelve questions from the getting-the-job module. Answer out loud before the answer comes.

Two from each lesson, in order: the FAANG loop, behavioural interviews, Netflix's culture, resumes and screens, negotiation, and the first 90 days. Each one has four options, A to D, then a few seconds for you to commit to one.

## Question 1

A candidate's packet shows strong coding in both rounds, a design round that followed a standard template, and behavioural stories confined to their own team. What is the most likely outcome for a senior role?

A, hire at senior, because two strong coding rounds set the level. B, hire, one level below, because level rests on scope evidence. C, an automatic extra coding round to confirm the strong signal. D, no hire, because coding is weighted least of all the rounds.

[think]

The answer is B: hire, one level below, because level rests on scope evidence.

At many large companies, coding is largely a bar check. It clears the bar without establishing senior scope. Level is argued from design depth and behavioural scope, and a template-shaped design with team-scoped stories gives the level case nothing to stand on. Coding does not count for nothing, though: it cleared the bar, which is why this is a hire and not a no.

## Question 2

What does an Amazon-style Bar Raiser add to a debrief, compared with a debrief led only by the hiring manager?

A, an outside interviewer who can block a hire below the bar. B, a senior leader who sets the level and approves the offer. C, a committee that decides from the packet without the team. D, a second hiring manager who breaks ties between the forms.

[think]

The answer is A: an outside interviewer who can block a hire below the bar.

Amazon describes the Bar Raiser as a trained interviewer from outside the hiring team who takes part in the loop and guides the debrief, and former Amazon executives describe a veto over the hire. It targets the bias a manager-led debrief is weakest on, the urgency to fill a seat, while keeping a debrief's speed. A committee that never met the candidate is the Google-style model, a different design.

## Question 3

A behavioural interviewer has three assigned competencies in 45 minutes. Why does a five-minute first telling hurt you, even if the story is strong?

A, it gives the interviewer more detail than the anchored rubric can use. B, it exceeds a fixed time limit that rubrics treat as a communication failure. C, it uses the probe time a competency needs, leaving it without evidence. D, it signals over-preparation, which rubrics score below spontaneity.

[think]

The answer is C: it uses the probe time a competency needs.

Each competency gets roughly ten to fifteen minutes, and most of the scoring evidence comes from the drill-down, not the first telling. A monologue spends that budget, so a competency can end the round with nothing written against it. Preparation is not penalised, and there is no fixed limit. The cost is lost probing time.

## Question 4

Asked for a costly mistake, you describe a cache outage, and the interviewer says: traffic beat the forecast, so why is this your mistake? Which answer scores highest on growth?

A, agree it was mainly the forecast, since that is honest. B, say nobody could have predicted it, so nobody is to blame. C, say you now over-provision everything, so it cannot recur. D, say the forecast was an input, and the sizing method was yours.

[think]

The answer is D: the forecast was an input; the sizing method was yours.

Separating the trigger from the cause, and owning the cause, is what the growth anchors reward, especially when the trigger was external. Deflecting to the forecast under a direct probe is the classic failure. And over-provisioning is a cost adopted without judgement, not a changed method.

## Question 5

Two tellings of the same API deprecation differ only in who decided and how objections were handled. Which is evidence for Netflix's idea of context, not control?

A, the candidate decided alone and told nobody, to avoid delay. B, the team voted, and the majority view overruled the objectors. C, the director approved it, and a complaint was escalated upwards. D, the candidate decided, sought dissent first, and changed the plan.

[think]

The answer is D: the candidate decided, sought dissent first, and changed the plan.

Context, not control puts the decision with the person who has the context, who then farms for dissent and owns the result. The manager is informed rather than asked. Approval chains are control. Acting without informing anyone is freedom without responsibility. And a vote is consensus, which the informed-captain model deliberately avoids.

## Question 6

Which feedback story best fits the four A guidelines described in No Rules Rules?

A, saving several concerns for the annual review, so they are complete. B, telling your manager the team felt demotivated, without examples. C, private, specific feedback aimed to help, then checking back. D, pointing out a peer's error in the design review, so everyone hears it.

[think]

The answer is C: private, specific feedback aimed to help, then checking back.

The four As are aim to assist and make it actionable when giving, and appreciate, then accept or discard, when receiving. Private, specific feedback with a follow-up meets both giving criteria. Public correction confuses candour with bluntness, vague feedback is not actionable, and saving it up defeats constant feedback.

## Question 7

What does an applicant tracking system mostly do with your resume?

A, stores and parses it so recruiters can search it. B, rejects it if it uses two columns or any tables. C, holds it unread until a referral is attached. D, scores its format and silently rejects low scores.

[think]

The answer is A: it stores and parses it so recruiters can search it.

An applicant tracking system is a database with search. It parses the resume into fields, records the source and tracks stages. Automatic rejection usually comes from knockout questions, such as work authorisation. Formatting matters because a garbled parse is what the recruiter sees and searches.

## Question 8

A former colleague offers to refer you. Why ask them to do it before you apply?

A, referrals are only valid for roles not yet posted. B, in many systems, the first recorded source sticks. C, applying first lowers the bar for the referrer. D, recruiters delete cold applications after a week.

[think]

The answer is B: in many systems, the first recorded source sticks.

If the cold application creates the record, it may stay tagged as cold and lose the referral's faster, more careful review. Referrals do not change the bar, and cold applications are not deleted; they are read in bulk.

## Question 9

A stock grant worth 400 thousand dollars is converted at a 30-day average price of 200 dollars. On your first day, the stock trades at 220. What do you hold?

A, about 1,818 shares, with the gap paid in cash. B, 2 thousand shares, worth 400 thousand dollars until they vest. C, 2 thousand shares, worth 440 thousand dollars at today's price. D, about 1,818 shares, worth exactly 400 thousand dollars.

[think]

The answer is C: 2 thousand shares, worth 440 thousand dollars at today's price.

The share count is fixed at conversion: 400 thousand divided by 200 is 2 thousand shares. From then on, their value moves with the price, so on day one they are worth 2 thousand times 220. Conversion does not happen at the first-day price, and no cash top-up covers price movements.

## Question 10

Why do compensation approvers usually agree to a sign-on bonus or a larger equity grant more readily than a higher base?

A, sign-on bonuses are not taxed, so they cost the company less. B, base increases need board approval at most public companies. C, equity is free to the company, since it is only newly issued shares. D, base recurs, drives raises and bonus, and is compared with peers.

[think]

The answer is D: base recurs, drives raises and bonus, and is compared with peers.

Base is paid every year, raises and the bonus are percentages of it, and paying a new hire above existing peers at the level creates compression. A sign-on is one-off and protected by a clawback, and an equity grant comes from a budget. Both have real costs and tax treatment, but not the recurring, internal-equity cost of base.

## Question 11

In your second week, you notice the deployment tooling is clumsy and could be replaced by a tool you used before. What is the best move?

A, replace it on a branch and present the finished result. B, log it, learn why it exists, and revisit it with context. C, drop it, since new hires should not suggest changes. D, propose replacing it this week, while your view is fresh.

[think]

The answer is B: log it, learn why it exists, and revisit it with context.

Early observations are valuable but often lack history, since many oddities come from past incidents or deliberate trade-offs. A fresh-eyes log turns them into credible proposals later. Proposing immediately, or rebuilding in secret, is the classic new-senior trap, and dropping them wastes a newcomer's main advantage.

## Question 12

Of reliability, competence and intent, which do new senior hires most often over-invest in first, and what works better?

A, competence; lead with reliability and intent instead. B, none; all three build at the same rate regardless. C, reliability; show competence first, then keep promises. D, intent; stop building relationships and show expertise.

[think]

The answer is A: competence; lead with reliability and intent instead.

New seniors often try to show expertise immediately, which reads as judgement without context. Small promises kept, and visible work for the team, come first, and they make later competence land as help rather than criticism.

## Recap

The questions kept returning to three ideas. Evidence beats impressions: level is argued from scope, a story needs your own decisions and an owned cause, and a resume number needs its derivation. Sequence matters: referral before application, level before numbers, learning before proposing. And the flexible, evidenced ask wins, whether it is equity and a sign-on instead of base, or a decision with dissent sought first instead of an approval chain.
