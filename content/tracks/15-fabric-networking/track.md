Network organisations hire software engineers to build the systems that run their networks: the source of truth, the automation that pushes configuration to thousands of devices, the telemetry pipeline, the tool that answers "is it the network?" in seconds. The interviews for those roles go well past coding. Expect a packet walk ("a laptop opens a website: what happens, hop by hop?"), a stack of BGP questions, a fabric design question, and, at any company near AI, RDMA and RoCE congestion control. The engineers who build the software do not need to configure a router. They need to know how the network works at the level of the engineers they build for, well enough to spot a wrong assumption in a design review and to know what a counter means at 3 a.m.

This track starts from zero. The first module builds the foundations a network engineer takes for granted: frames, MAC learning, subnets, the routing table, ARP and the hop-by-hop walk, ending with the host's own kernel path. Then it builds one fabric a layer at a time. The leaf-spine Clos and ECMP come first, then the underlay that makes every device reachable (IS-IS, or BGP used as an IGP). BGP gets two modules, one on how it works and one on how it behaves on the real internet. The overlay comes next: VXLAN, EVPN and multi-tenancy. The AI module covers why training traffic is different, NCCL and collectives, NVLink, RDMA, InfiniBand and RoCE, and lossless Ethernet with PFC, ECN and DCQCN. The operations module covers what a software engineer builds on top: automation and intent, configuration APIs, telemetry, active measurement, a troubleshooting method, safe change and incident case studies. The last module turns it into interview answers.

Every lesson uses the same reference fabric, so each new layer lands on a picture you already hold. **Meridian** runs three sites: Amsterdam (`ams`), Singapore (`sin`) and San Jose (`sjc`). Each site is a leaf-spine Clos: servers and customers attach at leaves (`leaf1.ams`), spines (`spine1.ams`) only forward, a pair of route reflectors (`rr1.ams`, `rr2.ams`) sit beside the spines, and a pair of border gateways (`gw1.ams`, `gw2.ams`) connect to the internet. Sites connect spine to spine over leased long-haul circuits. Every device has a loopback in `10.255.0.0/16` (`leaf1.ams` is `10.255.1.11`). The underlay is IS-IS, and the overlay is BGP EVPN in AS 65000 carrying two tenants, Acme and Globex, who both use `10.1.1.0/24` without colliding. To the internet Meridian is AS 64500, announcing `203.0.113.0/24` through two ISPs. In San Jose a 1,024-GPU training cluster (128 servers with 8 GPUs each, one 400 Gb/s NIC per GPU) runs on its own rail-optimised RoCE fabric. All addresses and AS numbers come from the ranges reserved for documentation and private use.

The bar is the networking panel at a network or AI-infrastructure company: you can walk any packet through the fabric with the right headers at every hop, explain BGP's decisions from first principles, say what breaks and which signal shows it, and design the software that watches it all.
