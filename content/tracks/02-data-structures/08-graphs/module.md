Trees are graphs with one path between any two nodes. Remove that restriction and you get the structure behind road maps, social networks, package managers, build systems, compilers, schedulers, network topologies, state machines and every "which of these depend on which" question you have ever answered by hand. Roughly a fifth of interview problems are graph problems, and half of those do not say the word "graph" anywhere in the statement.

The core of the module is small: the representations you must be able to build in your sleep (adjacency list, matrix, edge list and CSR, with the bytes per edge each costs in CPython, Java and a systems language, and what networkx, scipy and a graph database do with an edge), two traversals (BFS with a queue, DFS with a stack or recursion) traced step by step with their queue and stack states, and the handful of things each traversal gives you for free. Shortest paths in unweighted graphs, multi-source and bidirectional BFS, discovery and finish times with the four edge types, connected components, bipartite checks, cycle detection with three colours and topological ordering by Kahn's algorithm and by DFS are all one of the two traversals with a few extra lines, and each is worked on a drawn graph. The lessons also cover what goes wrong in production: marking visited on dequeue, `list.pop(0)` queues, recursion limits in CPython, the JVM and Node, a cycle that silently truncates a build order, and a deadlock detector that is a cycle check on a wait-for graph. The [graph algorithms module](/learn/algorithms/graph-algorithms/shortest-paths-dijkstra) in the algorithms track adds weights, heaps and the harder structural algorithms; this module makes sure the foundation is solid enough to hold them.

The last lesson is about modelling: recognising that a grid, a word list, a set of prerequisites or a state space is a graph, choosing the vertices and edges deliberately, estimating how many there are, and knowing when the graph is too large to build and must be explored implicitly. That is the skill senior interviewers are actually probing when they ask a graph question.
