Stacks and queues are the simplest abstract data types and the most frequently mis-implemented. A queue built on `list.pop(0)` or `array.shift()` is O(n) per operation and it ships to production every week: draining 100,000 elements that way takes over 200 ms on both CPython 3.14 and Node 24, against about a millisecond with a deque or a head index, and a million elements takes half a minute in JavaScript. A recursion that should have been an explicit stack overflows at depth 1,000. A "sliding window maximum" written with a heap is a log factor slower than it needs to be.

This module covers the mechanics first: array-backed stacks, ring-buffer queues traced through wrap-around and growth (including the full/empty ambiguity and the physical-copy bug), deques, the two-stack queue with its amortised proof, and what each language's standard library actually gives you (CPython's 64-slot deque blocks, Java's and Rust's ring buffers, Go's `hchan`, the kernel's `kfifo`, the Disruptor's padded sequence counters). It then spends two lessons on the monotonic stack and monotonic deque, a pair of techniques that look like tricks and are really one invariant applied twice, with hand traces of next-greater, stock span, sum of subarray minimums, the histogram, count-based and time-based windows, and a proof of why they are linear. The final lesson connects stacks to what they run: bracket matching, postfix and shunting-yard evaluation traced token by token, undo/redo, iterative DFS, the min-stack, and the call stack itself, with each runtime's frame cost and stack limit and what an overflow physically does.

By the end you should be able to implement any of these from scratch, say what the amortised cost of each operation is and why, diagnose the production failure modes each one has (stale-request FIFO under overload, `if`-instead-of-`while` expiry, unbounded queues, nesting-depth denial of service), and recognise the "next greater element" and "window extremum" shapes in a problem before the interviewer finishes describing it.
