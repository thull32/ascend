Every scheduler, every event simulator, every shortest-path search and every "top ten" dashboard has the same inner loop: repeatedly take the most urgent item from a set that keeps changing. A sorted list answers that in O(1) but costs O(n) to maintain; a hash table maintains cheaply but cannot answer "smallest" at all. The binary heap sits exactly between them, O(log n) for both insert and extract, with a memory layout so simple it is a flat array.

This module builds the heap from its array layout and the two repair operations that keep it valid, traced by hand and measured: why heapify is O(n), why CPython, Rust and libstdc++ sift a hole to the bottom before bubbling back up, what a heap entry costs in each runtime, and why heap sort loses to quicksort once the array outgrows the cache. The second lesson moves to how priority queues are used and misused in real code: what the standard libraries ship, why ties break unpredictably (traced), how to make a heap stable, how schedulers avoid starvation, and what libuv, Go, Tokio, Kafka and Linux each chose for their timers and why. The third lesson covers the interview pattern family that heaps own outright, top-k, k-way merge and the streaming median, together with how external sorts, LSM compaction and scatter-gather query merging size that same loop, and what breaks when you distribute any of them. The last lesson tackles the operation a plain binary heap cannot do, decrease-key, and the two ways to fake it (lazy deletion with a garbage bound, or an indexed heap with a position map), who ships which, what Fibonacci heaps promise and why they stay in textbooks, and the honest question of when you should be using a balanced tree instead.
