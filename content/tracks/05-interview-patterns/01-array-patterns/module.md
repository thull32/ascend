Arrays and strings are where most coding rounds start, and where most candidates lose the most time. The problems look different on the surface ("longest substring", "container with most water", "merge these meetings", "find the missing number") but they map onto nine mechanical patterns. An engineer who knows the patterns spends the first two minutes classifying the problem and the rest executing; one who does not spends thirty minutes rediscovering a sliding window, or writes one for a problem with negative numbers where it silently returns wrong answers.

Each lesson is built around the decisions an interviewer is listening for. It states the signal precisely, including the problems that hide the pattern (the complement window in "take `k` cards from the ends", the letter spans in Partition Labels, the ±1 transform that turns "equal 0s and 1s" into a prefix-sum lookup) and the near misses that look like the pattern and need something else. It gives a complete, annotated template in Python and JavaScript with the invariant that makes it correct, then runs three or more Ascend problems through it with a state-by-state trace you can reproduce on paper. Variants tables show how the template bends; failure modes are written as symptom, diagnosis and fix; and the interviewer follow-ups include the ones that change the pattern: "now it is a stream", "now values can be negative", "now `k` is 10⁹".

The lessons also measure what the runtime does, on CPython 3.14 and Node 24: how Timsort treats nearly sorted interval logs, what `bisect` calls per probe, why an `Int32Array` beats a `Map` tenfold as a window's state, why a float prefix difference loses precision late in the array, and how much a column-order walk costs a row-major grid. The underlying techniques and their proofs live in the [algorithms track](/learn/algorithms/technique-mastery/sliding-window-mastery); these lessons link there rather than repeating them, and concentrate on recognising and executing each pattern under interview conditions.

The order is deliberate. Two pointers and sliding windows are the foundation; prefix sums and binary search add precomputation and search on the answer; sorting-based patterns and intervals show how one `sort()` call unlocks a greedy sweep; cyclic sort, Kadane and matrix traversal are the specialised tools that appear less often but are unmistakable once you know their signals.
