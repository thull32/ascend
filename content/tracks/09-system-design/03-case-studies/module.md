Building blocks and distributed-systems theory are necessary but not sufficient. The senior design round asks you to take a vague product ("design Netflix", "design a rate limiter") and, in forty-five minutes, turn it into a system you could defend to the people who run it. That is a skill of composition and judgement, and the only way to build it is to do it many times against real constraints.

Every case study follows the same spine: requirements with explicit numbers; a back-of-envelope estimate worked to requests per second, storage, bandwidth and machine counts, with every assumption visible; the API; a data model whose keys are justified by the queries they serve; a high-level design you can draw in five minutes; and two or three deep dives, each of which follows one real request or event end to end with timings until something goes wrong. Then come the failure modes as symptom, diagnosis and fix, a table of the alternatives the design rejects and what would flip each choice, how the design changes at 10× and 100×, what real companies have publicly described doing, the interviewer's follow-up questions with the answer that scores and the one that does not, and the mistakes mid-level engineers make. Where a number matters it is computed, simulated or measured (several lessons reproduce a race or a bottleneck on Postgres), and where it cannot be, it is an order of magnitude with what it depends on.

The systems are chosen to cover the space. Some are read-heavy (URL shortener, news feed, autocomplete), some write-heavy (click aggregation, metrics, ride-sharing locations), some are about coordination (ticket booking, payments, collaborative editing), and some are about moving bytes at planetary scale (video streaming, video upload). Two lessons go deep on Netflix specifically, because Netflix is the reference bar for this curriculum and because its architecture is unusually well documented in public. Most lessons include an exercise that implements the mechanism at the heart of the design: geohash neighbours, a ledger balance check, seat holds with expiry, Bloom filter sizing, an operational transform, a windowed count.

Read each case study with a pen. Redo the estimates with different assumptions, draw the high-level design from memory, trace the deep dives yourself, and then argue with the choices in the trade-offs table. The point is not to memorise eighteen answers; it is to internalise one method well enough to design the nineteenth system live.
