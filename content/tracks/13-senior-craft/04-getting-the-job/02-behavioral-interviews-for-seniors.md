"Tell me about a time you disagreed with a technical decision." One candidate describes a library choice on their own team, says the team discussed it and went with their view, and finishes in ninety seconds. Another describes a cross-team decision, the data they gathered, how they took the disagreement to its proposer before the review rather than in it, what they gave up, what happened, and what they now do differently. Both stories are true. Only one of them is evidence of a senior engineer.

The behavioural round is where level is most often decided, and the round senior candidates most often under-prepare, because it feels like a conversation. It is a structured search for evidence that you have operated at a particular scope, run against a plan and scored against a rubric, by an interviewer trained to dig. This lesson shows that machinery, gives you STAR+, a worksheet and a story bank, and runs two stories weak and strong, with the interviewer's notes, the drill-down and the write-ups. All story details are illustrative.

## What the round measures

Behavioural interviewing rests on one premise: past behaviour in similar situations is the best available predictor of future behaviour. So the interviewer asks about specific past situations and scores the evidence against competencies whose names vary by company but cluster around the same things. Ascend's behavioural mock interviewer scores five:

| Dimension | What the interviewer is looking for |
|---|---|
| Ownership and impact | You owned an outcome, not only a task, and it mattered |
| Judgement and decision-making | You weighed real alternatives, decided with incomplete information, and can explain why |
| Collaboration and conflict | You worked through disagreement and kept the relationship intact |
| Growth and self-awareness | You describe your own mistakes honestly, and what changed as a result |
| Communication clarity | The story is structured, specific and easy to follow |

Some companies publish their competencies. Amazon's [Leadership Principles](https://www.amazon.jobs/content/en/our-workplace/leadership-principles) are the best-known example and worth reading wherever you interview: several ("Ownership", "Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit", "Earn Trust") map directly onto the dimensions above.

At senior level the same competencies are measured at a larger **scope**: more people and teams, longer horizons, more ambiguity at the start, higher stakes. The rubric below makes that explicit.

## Under the hood: how the round is run

**The plan.** As [The FAANG loop](/learn/senior-craft/getting-the-job/the-faang-loop) describes, each interviewer usually arrives with assigned competencies, commonly two or three for a 45-minute round (Amazon says its interviews are "rooted in behavioral-based questions" guided by its Leadership Principles). Each competency then gets ten to fifteen minutes: a prompt, your first telling and a drill-down. A five-minute monologue spends a third of that budget and leaves a competency with no probe time.

**Specific instances only.** Competency-based interviewing asks for one real situation. "What I usually do is…" and "I would…" get redirected ("can you give me a specific time?"), because a hypothetical shows what you know is right, not what you do. Google's [re:Work guide](https://rework.withgoogle.com/intl/en/guides/a-guide-to-structured-interviewing-for-better-hiring-practices) describes using "the same interview questions" graded "on an identical scale", and separates behavioural questions ("Tell me about a time…") from hypothetical ones ("What would you do if…?"); answering the first kind with the second is what gets redirected.

**Probing depth.** After the first telling, the interviewer drills several levels: what exactly you did, why, what else you considered, what happened, what you would change. They are looking for the level at which the detail runs out, because invented, inflated or borrowed stories run out early.

**Near-verbatim notes.** Interviewers write down what you say, close to your words, and tag each action with who did it. "We migrated the service" goes in the team's column; "I wrote the rollback plan" goes in yours. Only your column is scored for ownership, so a story told entirely in "we" leaves nothing to credit, and claiming the team's work is exposed by the next probe ("who wrote the prototype?"). The fix is "I" for your actions, "we" for outcomes, and others named for theirs: "I built the prototype; Priya ran the load test with me; the team shipped the migration."

## Under the hood: from notes to a level

After the round, the interviewer scores each assigned competency against an **anchored rubric**: every score has a written description of the behaviour that earns it, so two interviewers hearing the same story give the same score. Companies write their own; this illustrative set, on Ascend's five dimensions, is the one the walk-throughs apply.

| Dimension | 2: below the bar | 3: mid-level pass | 4: senior | 5: strong senior |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ownership and impact | No personal action identifiable, or tasks only | Owned an outcome within their own team | Owned an outcome across teams, with a business result | Set a direction others followed; changed how others work |
| Judgement | One option, no evidence | Alternatives named | Alternatives with costs; decided under uncertainty with evidence | Also said what would have changed the decision |
| Collaboration and conflict | Other side absent or a villain | Resolved within the team | Influenced people outside their authority; relationship intact | Turned the conflict into a mechanism others reuse |
| Growth and self-awareness | No mistake, or a disguised strength | A mistake named | Own mistake, its cost, a changed behaviour | Also changed the system against a repeat |
| Communication | Hard to follow; mostly context | Structured but generic | Structured, specific, with numbers, under three minutes | Also anticipates the follow-ups |

**Level is argued from scope evidence.** The write-up quotes the scope (people, teams, duration, stakes) and the ownership (which decisions were yours). A 4 in ownership with "three teams, two quarters, revenue path" in the evidence line reads senior; the same 4 with no scope in the line invites a committee to ask "at what scale?"

**The write-up is weighed with the design round.** In the illustrative packet in [The FAANG loop](/learn/senior-craft/getting-the-job/the-faang-loop), one behavioural line from a drill-down ("at the third probe could not say which decisions were theirs") moved a committee's decision one level down.

## STAR+

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) answers the interviewer's questions in the order they need them. The "+" adds what most distinguishes senior answers: reflection.

| Part | What it contains | Share of the answer |
|---|---|---|
| **Situation** | Enough context to follow: team, system, scale, stakes | 10–15% |
| **Task** | Your specific responsibility, and why it was hard or ambiguous | 10% |
| **Action** | What *you* did: decisions, rejected alternatives and why, whom you persuaded and how | 50–60% |
| **Result** | What happened, with numbers: business, technical and people outcomes | 10–15% |
| **+ Reflection** | What you learned, would change, and now do differently | 10% |

Aim for two to three minutes on the first telling, leaving the interviewer ten minutes to probe. The most common structural mistake is an answer that is 70% situation (architecture, org chart, history) with thirty seconds for what you did. Cut context to what the listener needs to follow the actions.

## The STAR+ worksheet

Fill this in once per story; writing it out is what makes the numbers available under pressure.

```text
STORY: <short memorable title, e.g. "Checkout stays synchronous">
Competencies it shows: <primary>, <secondary>, <secondary>
Scope: <people / teams involved>, <duration>, <users, requests, money at stake>

SITUATION (2 sentences)
  -
TASK: my responsibility, and what made it hard (1-2 sentences)
  -
ACTIONS (3-5 bullets; each is a decision or an influence move)
  - Decision:              Alternative rejected, and why:
  - Who I had to persuade: How:
  - What I did when it went wrong / someone disagreed:
RESULT (numbers; business outcome; what happened to people)
  -
+ REFLECTION
  - What I learned:
  - What I'd do differently:
  - What I do now because of this:

FOLLOW-UP PREP
  - My actions vs the team's (names):
  - Hardest question someone could ask about this:
  - The numbers (and how I know them):
  - Who disagreed, and what they would say about it:
```

The follow-up prep is the part people skip and the part that saves them: it is the material for probes three and four, where weak stories collapse.

## Building a story bank

Eight to ten stories, each showing two or three competencies, cover almost everything a behavioural round asks. Good sources: a project you led from an ambiguous start; a disagreement with a peer, manager or another team; a real failure; an incident you handled ([Incidents and postmortems](/learn/senior-craft/technical-leadership/incidents-and-postmortems)); a decision you changed without authority ([Leading without authority](/learn/senior-craft/technical-leadership/leading-without-authority)); mentoring; a time you cut scope under pressure. Prefer the last three years, and reuse a story at most once per loop, for a different competency. Then map them:

## What makes a story senior

Scope beyond your own tasks comes first: "I fixed the flaky test" is not a senior story; "I got three teams to agree on test ownership after flaky tests cost us two release days a month" might be. Then a fuzzy start you turned into a plan, trade-offs with named costs, influence on people you did not manage, a business outcome, someone else who got better because of it, and, for failures, your own mistake with a concrete change in how you work.

## Story 1, weak: "we didn't move checkout"

The question: "Tell me about a time you disagreed with a technical decision." The illustrative weak telling, in full:

> "So at my last company we were a pretty big platform, lots of microservices, mostly Java and some Go, and we'd been on RabbitMQ for years, and there was this big push from the architecture group to move everything onto Kafka. A lot of people thought it was the future. Our team owned checkout, which is really important, and I felt the move didn't make sense for us, because Kafka would add latency and checkout needs to be fast. We had a lot of discussions about it, in meetings and on Slack, and some people on the team agreed with me and some didn't. In the end we decided not to move checkout, and it worked out well: checkout stayed fast and the other teams moved over. I think the lesson is that you shouldn't follow the hype; you have to pick the right tool for the job."

| Line | Interviewer's note |
|---|---|
| "a pretty big platform… a big push from the architecture group" | "about 40% context; scale not quantified; decision owner unnamed" |
| "I felt… Kafka would add latency" | "a claim; latency never measured" |
| "We had a lot of discussions" | "no personal action identifiable; 'we'" |
| "some people agreed with me and some didn't" | "other side's view not described" |
| "In the end we decided not to move checkout" | "who decided, by what mechanism?" |
| "checkout stayed fast and the other teams moved over" | "result unquantified; no business outcome" |
| "don't follow the hype; right tool for the job" | "reflection is a platitude; no changed behaviour" |

Nothing in it is false, and nothing in it scores above the bar; what the probes find decides the round.

## Story 1, strong: checkout stays synchronous

The same disagreement, told by an illustrative candidate who did the work:

> **Situation.** "Two years ago our organisation decided to move service-to-service communication onto a new event bus. I was the tech lead for checkout, six engineers, handling about 40,000 orders a day."
>
> **Task.** "I agreed with the direction but thought it was wrong for payment authorisation, where the customer waits on the answer. My job was to find out whether that was real and, if so, to get an exception without undermining a migration the architecture group had already announced."
>
> **Actions.** "First, I checked whether I was right. I built a small prototype of authorisation over the bus and load-tested it: p99 went from 180 milliseconds to about 1.4 seconds, mostly from consumer batching, and we'd need idempotency keys and an outbox to avoid double charges. Second, I wrote a two-page document with those numbers, what would have to be true for async to work, and a proposal: authorisation stays synchronous, and everything after it (receipts, fulfilment, analytics) moves to the bus as planned. Third, before the review, I met the migration's lead architect one-to-one so the proposal wouldn't surprise him in public. He pushed back on having an inconsistent pattern, which was fair, so we agreed written criteria for when an exception is allowed, instead of a one-off carve-out for checkout."
>
> **Result.** "The exception and the criteria were accepted. The migration went ahead for about thirty other services on schedule, checkout latency and conversion were unaffected, and two quarters later another team used the criteria to justify their own exception in a day instead of a month."
>
> **Reflection.** "I raised it after the decision was announced, which cost two weeks and some goodwill. I now read organisation-level proposals during the comment period, and when I disagree, I bring numbers and talk to the author privately before any meeting."

| Line | Interviewer's note |
|---|---|
| "tech lead… six engineers… 40,000 orders a day" | "scope quantified; org-wide decision; revenue path" |
| "agreed with the direction… wrong for authorisation" | "precise disagreement, not blanket opposition" |
| "checked whether I was right… 180 ms to about 1.4 s" | "tested own hypothesis before arguing; knows the mechanism" |
| "idempotency keys and an outbox" | "sees the correctness cost as well as latency" |
| "what would have to be true for async to work" | "written proposal; states what would change their mind" |
| "met the architect one-to-one" | "influence without authority; no public surprise" |
| "which was fair… written criteria" | "conceded a fair point; exception became a mechanism" |
| "thirty other services on schedule… another team… in a day" | "business outcome plus a multiplier" |
| "raised it after the decision… now read proposals during the comment period" | "own mistake with its cost; changed behaviour" |

About two and a half minutes. Its moves (measure first, write it down, pre-wire the owner, concede the fair point) are the ones [Leading without authority](/learn/senior-craft/technical-leadership/leading-without-authority) and [Design docs and RFCs](/learn/senior-craft/technical-leadership/design-docs-and-rfcs) teach.

Apply the anchored rubric to each telling. For each dimension, list the lines that are evidence, then find the highest anchor those lines fully satisfy.

| Collaboration and conflict | "Some agreed and some didn't"; other side absent | 2 | Private conversation first; conceded consistency; criteria another team reused | 5 |

| Communication | 40% context, no numbers | 2 | STAR+ in order, numbers, about 2.5 minutes | 4 |
| **Total** | | **10 / 25** | | **22 / 25** |

## Story 1 under the drill-down

| Probe | Strong candidate | Weak candidate | Interviewer's note |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. "What exactly did you measure, and how?" | "I wrote a load generator replaying a day of authorisation calls at twice the rate. Priya set up the consumer config with me. p99 went from 180 ms to about 1.4 s; tuning batch settings got it to about 600 ms and no lower." | "We saw in testing that it was slower. I don't remember the exact number." | Strong: "specific, personal, knows the limit of the number". Weak: "no measurement was theirs" |
| 2. "What would you have done if the architect had refused?" | "Asked the review for a decision with a date, with the data. If overruled, built async with the outbox and idempotency keys, added an alarm on authorisation p99, and said so in writing." | "I guess we would have moved and seen what happened." | Strong: "real fallback; disagree then commit with mitigation". Weak: "no plan" |
| 3. "Didn't the exception create the inconsistency the migration was meant to remove?" | "Yes, one more pattern to learn. That's why we wrote criteria instead of a carve-out; two exceptions have been granted under them since, both on paths where a user waits." | "No, checkout is different. It needed to be fast." | Strong: "conceded, then bounded the cost". Weak: "defensive; could not state the trade-off" |
| 4. "Who disagreed with you most, and what would they say about this?" | "The architect. He'd say I raised it late, and that he was right to insist on criteria." | "Some people on the team who liked Kafka. They'd probably say I was right in the end." | Strong: "represents the other side fairly". Weak: "other side as the losing party" |

Probes 1 and 3 settle the weak candidate's round: the measurement was not theirs and the trade-off never considered.

## Story 2, weak: "the cache ran out of memory"

A different competency: "Tell me about a mistake you made that had real consequences." The illustrative weak telling:

> "We had an outage during a big sale event; the cache ran out of memory. Honestly, traffic was about twice what product had forecast, so I don't think anyone could have predicted it. When it happened I jumped on the call and helped get things stable again pretty quickly; we scaled up the cache and it was fine. The thing I learned is that I care a lot about reliability, maybe too much sometimes, so now I always make sure we over-provision everything. It made our team much more resilient."

| Line | Interviewer's note |
|---|---|
| "We had an outage… the cache ran out of memory" | "passive; whose cache, whose sizing?" |
| "traffic was about twice what product had forecast… no one could have predicted it" | "asked for their mistake; deflected to product" |
| "I jumped on the call and helped" | "role in the incident unclear" |
| "we scaled up the cache and it was fine" | "impact not stated: users, duration, money" |
| "I care a lot about reliability, maybe too much" | "disguised strength" |
| "always over-provision everything" | "lesson is a cost, not a judgement" |

## Story 2, strong: the cache I sized wrong

> **Situation.** "Last year I designed the caching layer for our product catalogue, which serves every product page, about 3,000 requests a second at peak. I owned the design and the capacity plan."
>
> **Task.** "The mistake was my sizing. I sized the Redis cluster from the average item size, about 2 KB, times the number of hot items, with 30% headroom."
>
> **Actions.** "During our biggest sale of the year, a merchandising change added bundle products whose cached entries were ten to forty times the average. The cache hit its memory limit, the hit rate fell from about 95% to about 60%, and the database behind it saturated. Product pages ran at a p99 of about three seconds for forty minutes, and conversion dropped. I was on call: I found the evictions within about ten minutes, shed the recommendations call that was doubling cache reads, and failed over to a larger node type, which took about twenty more. In the postmortem I put my sizing decision first among the causes: I had sized on the mean when the risk was in the tail and the key count, and I had never load-tested with production-shaped data."
>
> **Result.** "We resized from the measured p99 item size and key growth, alerted on eviction rate rather than memory alone, and added a load test at twice forecast peak with production-shaped payloads to the release checklist. The next sale ran with evictions flat."
>
> **Reflection.** "I size from distributions now, not averages. I write down the assumptions a capacity plan depends on and who would know if they changed, and before a peak I ask merchandising what they will launch. Two other teams now use the sizing template."

| Line | Interviewer's note |
|---|---|
| "I owned the design and the capacity plan" | "owns it in the first sentence" |
| "sized from the average item size… 30% headroom" | "names the decision that was wrong" |
| "bundle products… ten to forty times the average" | "mechanism of failure, not only the symptom" |
| "hit rate 95% to 60%… p99 about three seconds for forty minutes" | "impact quantified" |
| "found the evictions… shed recommendations… failed over" | "own actions under pressure, in order, timed" |
| "put my sizing decision first among the causes" | "owned publicly although the trigger was external" |
| "sized on the mean when the risk was in the tail" | "names the reasoning error" |
| "eviction-rate alert… production-shaped load test" | "fixed the system, not only this instance" |
| "Two other teams now use the sizing template" | "multiplier" |

A failure story done well is one of the strongest senior signals, because clean ownership of a real mistake is what makes someone safe to give autonomy. [Capacity planning and cost](/learn/system-design/senior-design-skills/capacity-planning-and-cost) covers the sizing mistake itself.

## Handling follow-ups

The probes in both drill-downs are the standard types: the **drill-down** tests whether the actions were yours, the **counterfactual** your judgement beyond the happy path, the **challenge** whether you can hold a position while hearing criticism, **scope** whether the numbers are real, **reflection** your self-awareness, and **other people** ("how did your manager see it?") whether you can state a view you did not share fairly. Four techniques carry most of the weight. **Answer the question asked**: a drill-down wants one level more detail, not a retelling. **Pause before hard ones**: three seconds of thought reads as care. **Be precise about what you do not know**: "about 1.4 s at p99, from the load-test dashboard" beats a confident invented figure. **Concede fair points**: probe 3 in story 1 is where defensiveness most often ends a round.

## Trade-offs: which story to tell

| Choice | Credibility under drill-down | Level evidence | Main risk | Prefer it when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Recent (last 2–3 years), smaller scope | High: details fresh | Moderate | Reads mid-level if own-team only | Its scope is at least cross-team |
| Older (4+ years), larger scope | Lower: numbers fade | High | "What have you done recently?" | Paired with a recent story |
| Technical depth | High on judgement | High for an IC senior | Collaboration unevidenced | Competencies are judgement or ownership |
| People and influence | Moderate | High for cross-team scope | Little technical substance to probe | Competencies are conflict or influence |
| One flagship for every question | High: rehearsed | High, once | Competencies go empty; a second interviewer hears it again | Only for the deep-dive |
| Scripted word for word | Low: brittle when interrupted | Unchanged | Sounds recited; collapses at probe 2 | Never |
| Outline: bullets and numbers | High | Unchanged | Rambling if under-rehearsed | Always, rehearsed aloud |

## Failure modes

**Symptom: stories collapse at the third follow-up.** Answers get vaguer as the questions sharpen ("I'd have to check"); the write-up says "could not go below the surface". Diagnosis: the story was prepared only to the depth of the first telling, or the key actions were someone else's. Fix: the worksheet's follow-up prep for every action, then have someone ask "how?" three times in a row.

**Symptom: after your failure story, "and what was your mistake?"** Diagnosis: a safe failure, a deflection or a humblebrag, like story 2's weak version. Fix: a failure with a real cost whose root cause includes your decision, told in four parts: what it cost, your part, how you contained it, what changed in you and in the system.

**Symptom: "do you have a different example?" or "what have you done in the last two years?"** Diagnosis: one flagship story for every question, a story the previous interviewer already heard, or strong stories only from a previous job. Fix: the mapped bank, with recent work reframed for scope (it is usually bigger than you describe it).

## Interviewer follow-ups

**"Can you give me a more recent example?"** Model answer: have one ready; if your most recent story at that scope is older, say so and offer a smaller recent one showing the same judgement. Common wrong answer: the same story with different emphasis, or an improvised one that falls apart at the next probe.

**"What was your part, specifically, versus the team's?"** Model answer: two or three decisions that were yours, one argument you lost, and teammates credited by name. Common wrong answer: "it was really a team effort", which reads as no contribution, or claiming everything, which the next probe exposes.

**"Tell me about a time you were wrong about something technical."** Model answer: a belief you held, the evidence that changed your mind, and what you changed as a result. Common wrong answer: a story in which you were right and everyone else was wrong.

## What mid-level engineers get wrong

- **Improvising.** Without a bank, the first story that comes to mind is usually small; the write-up says "limited scope".
- **Choosing stories by outcome, not evidence.** A smooth project often has no visible decisions; the score stays at 3.
- **Answering hypothetically.** "I would…" gets redirected, and a second redirect becomes "no example provided".
- **Treating reflection as optional.** The most predictable follow-up goes unanswered.
- **Inventing or merging stories.** Composites collapse at probe three, and a credibility doubt sinks the whole packet, not only the round.

## Practising

Rehearse aloud; rereading notes leaves the telling 70% situation.

1. Fill in the worksheet for eight stories and build both matrices.
2. Tell each aloud against a timer. Over three minutes, cut the situation, not the actions.
3. Score your own telling against the anchored rubric, line by line, as in story 1.
4. Run a 30-minute behavioural mock on `/interviews`. Its report scores the five dimensions with quoted evidence; add every probe that exposed a thin spot to that story's follow-up prep.
5. Record yourself once, and listen for the "we", the filler and the missing numbers.

If your honest stories feel too small, check first whether you are underselling them (usually the problem); if the scope really is small, gain it before the move, never inflate it.

## Senior signals

- Your stories have cross-team scope, a fuzzy start you turned into a plan, and results in business terms.
- The action section dominates: decisions, rejected alternatives, and how you influenced people you did not manage.
- You separate "I" from "we" precisely and credit others by name.
- Your failure stories own a real decision, separate trigger from cause, and show a changed habit and a changed system.
- You hold up at the third and fourth probe: numbers with sources, fair challenges conceded, unknowns stated plainly.
- You arrive with a mapped bank, so every interviewer hears a different story.

## Check yourself

```quiz
- q: >-
    A behavioural interviewer has three assigned competencies in 45 minutes. Why does a five-minute first telling hurt you even if the story is strong?
  options: ["It gives the interviewer more detail than the anchored rubric can use", "It exceeds a fixed time limit that rubrics treat as a communication failure", "It uses the probe time a competency needs, leaving it without evidence", "It signals over-preparation, which rubrics score below spontaneity"]
  answer: 2
  explanation: >-
    Each competency gets roughly ten to fifteen minutes, and most scoring evidence comes from the drill-down, not the first telling. A monologue spends that budget, so a competency can end the round with nothing written against it. Preparation is not penalised, and there is no fixed limit; the cost is lost probing time.
- q: >-
    You tell a strong project story entirely in "we". How is it most likely recorded?
  options: ["Scored higher on collaboration, which offsets ownership", "Logged in the team's column, so your part is unclear", "Credited to you, since interviewers assume you led it", "Treated as fabricated, since seniors always say I"]
  answer: 1
  explanation: >-
    Notes tag each action with who did it, and only your own column is scored for ownership, so an all-we story leaves nothing to credit and the interviewer spends probes asking what your part was. It is not treated as dishonest, and collaboration needs evidence of how you worked with others, not the pronoun.
- q: >-
    Asked for a costly mistake, you describe a cache outage and the interviewer says "traffic beat the forecast, so why is this your mistake?" Which answer scores highest on growth?
  options: ["Agree it was mainly the forecast, since that is honest", "Say nobody could have predicted it, so nobody is to blame", "Say you now over-provision everything, so it cannot recur", "Say the forecast was an input; the sizing method was yours"]
  answer: 3
  explanation: >-
    Separating the trigger from the cause and owning the cause is what the growth anchors reward, especially when the trigger was external. Deflecting to the forecast under a direct probe is the classic failure, and over-provisioning is a cost adopted without judgement rather than a changed method.
- q: >-
    Scoring story 1 against the judgement anchors, which line lifts the strong version to the top score?
  options: ["It described the platform and the architecture group's history", "It reported that the other teams moved over on schedule", "It mentioned discussions in several meetings and on Slack", "It stated what would have to be true for async to work"]
  answer: 3
  explanation: >-
    The top judgement anchor asks for alternatives with costs, a decision under uncertainty with evidence, and what would have changed the decision; stating the conditions for async is that last element. Context, results and discussion volume are evidence for other dimensions or for none.
- q: >-
    Your strongest story is five years old; your recent work is cross-team but less dramatic. What is the best plan?
  options: ["Present the older story as recent, since dates are not checked", "Tell only recent stories, since older ones are never scored", "Lead with recent work and pair the older story with it", "Tell only the old story, since scope is what sets level"]
  answer: 2
  explanation: >-
    Recent stories hold up under drill-down because details are fresh and answer "what have you done lately?"; an older, larger story still adds level evidence when paired with them. Old stories are scored, but fade under probing, and misdating a story is the kind of inflation that collapses.
- q: >-
    Why do behavioural interviewers redirect "what I would do is..." to "tell me about a specific time"?
  options: ["Hypotheticals are reserved for the system design round", "Real stories are easier for the candidate to keep short", "Anchors describe behaviour; hypotheticals show only intent", "Hypotheticals take longer to score than real stories do"]
  answer: 2
  explanation: >-
    Competency-based interviewing rests on past behaviour predicting future behaviour; a hypothetical shows what you know is right, not what you did, so it cannot be matched to anchors that describe observed actions. After a second redirect the note often reads no example provided.
```
