"Tell me about a time you disagreed with a technical decision." One candidate describes a disagreement about a library choice on their own team, says the team discussed it and went with their view, and finishes in ninety seconds. Another describes a cross-team decision, the data they gathered, how they took the disagreement to the person who proposed the change before the review rather than in it, what they gave up to reach agreement, what happened afterwards, and what they now do differently. Both stories are true. Only one of them is evidence of a senior engineer.

The behavioural round is where level is most often decided, and it is the round senior candidates most often under-prepare, because it feels like a conversation about things they already know. It is not a conversation. It is a structured search for evidence that you have operated at a particular scope, run against a plan and scored against a rubric, and the interviewer is trained to dig until they find out whether you have. This lesson shows the machinery, gives you a structure (STAR+), a worksheet and a story bank, and then runs two stories twice each, weak and strong, with the interviewer's notes beside every line, the follow-ups that separate them and the write-ups they produce. All story details are illustrative.

## What the round measures

Behavioural interviewing rests on one premise: past behaviour in similar situations is the best available predictor of future behaviour. So the interviewer asks about specific past situations, not hypotheticals, and scores the evidence against a set of competencies. Names vary by company, but they cluster around the same things. Ascend's behavioural mock interviewer scores five:

| Dimension | What the interviewer is looking for |
|---|---|
| Ownership and impact | You took responsibility for an outcome, not only a task, and the outcome mattered |
| Judgement and decision-making | You weighed real alternatives, decided with incomplete information, and can explain why |
| Collaboration and conflict | You worked through disagreement productively and kept the relationship intact |
| Growth and self-awareness | You can describe your own mistakes honestly and what changed as a result |
| Communication clarity | The story is structured, specific and easy to follow |

Some companies publish their competencies. Amazon's Leadership Principles are the best-known example, and reading them is useful preparation even if you are not interviewing there, because they are a concrete public statement of what one large company looks for, and several of them ("Ownership", "Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit", "Earn Trust") map directly onto the dimensions above.

At senior level the same competencies are measured at a larger **scope**: more people, more teams, longer time horizons, more ambiguity at the start and higher stakes. That is the difference in the opening example, and it is what the rubric below makes explicit.

## Under the hood: how the round is run

**The plan.** As [The FAANG loop](/learn/senior-craft/getting-the-job/the-faang-loop) describes, each interviewer usually arrives with assigned competencies, commonly two or three for a 45-minute behavioural round. Amazon's candidate preparation material describes behavioural questions tied to specific Leadership Principles. With two or three competencies in 45 minutes, each one gets roughly ten to fifteen minutes: a prompt, your first telling and a drill-down. A five-minute monologue spends a third of that budget and leaves a competency with no probe time.

**Specific instances only.** Competency-based interviewing asks for one real situation. "What I usually do is…" and "I would…" get redirected ("can you give me a specific time?"), because a hypothetical shows what you know is right, not what you do. Google's re:Work guidance on structured interviewing recommends the same discipline: the same questions for comparable candidates and a rubric to score them.

**Probing depth.** The first telling is the invitation. The interviewer then drills: what exactly you did, why, what else you considered, what happened, what you would change. Three to five levels is common. They are looking for the level at which the detail runs out, because invented, inflated or borrowed stories run out early.

**Near-verbatim notes.** Interviewers write down what you say, close to your words, with rough time marks, and tag each action with who did it. "We migrated the service" goes in the team's column. "I wrote the rollback plan" goes in yours. Only your column is scored for ownership, which is why a story told entirely in "we" leaves the interviewer nothing to credit, and why claiming the team's work is exposed by the next probe ("who wrote the prototype?").

## Under the hood: from notes to a level

After the round, the interviewer scores each assigned competency against an **anchored rubric**: every score has a written description of the behaviour that earns it, so two interviewers hearing the same story give the same score. Companies write their own anchors; this illustrative set uses Ascend's five dimensions and is the one the walk-throughs below apply.

| Dimension | 2: below the bar | 3: mid-level pass | 4: senior | 5: strong senior |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ownership and impact | No personal action identifiable, or tasks only | Owned an outcome within their own team | Owned an outcome across teams, with a business result | Set a direction others followed; changed how others work |
| Judgement | One option, no evidence | Alternatives named | Alternatives with costs; decided under uncertainty with evidence | Also said what would have changed the decision |
| Collaboration and conflict | Other side absent or a villain | Resolved within the team | Influenced people outside their authority; relationship intact | Turned the conflict into a mechanism others reuse |
| Growth and self-awareness | No mistake, or a disguised strength | A mistake named | Own mistake, its cost, a changed behaviour | Also changed the system so the mistake is harder to repeat |
| Communication | Hard to follow; mostly context | Structured but generic | Structured, specific, with numbers, under three minutes | Also anticipates the follow-ups |

**Level is argued from scope evidence.** The write-up quotes the scope (people, teams, duration, stakes) and the ownership (which decisions were yours). A 4 in ownership with "three teams, two quarters, revenue path" in the evidence line reads senior; the same 4 with no scope in the line invites a committee to ask "at what scale?"

**The write-up is weighed with the design round.** In a packet, the behavioural and design write-ups are where level is usually argued. In the illustrative packet in [The FAANG loop](/learn/senior-craft/getting-the-job/the-faang-loop), one behavioural line ("at the third probe could not say which decisions were theirs") moved a committee's decision one level down. That line came from a drill-down, not from the first telling.

## STAR+

STAR is the standard structure: Situation, Task, Action, Result. It works because it answers the interviewer's questions in the order they need them. The "+" adds the part that most distinguishes senior answers: reflection.

| Part | What it contains | Share of the answer |
|---|---|---|
| **Situation** | Enough context to follow: the team, the system, the scale, the stakes | 10–15% |
| **Task** | Your specific responsibility, and why it was hard or ambiguous | 10% |
| **Action** | What *you* did, step by step: decisions, rejected alternatives and why, who you had to persuade and how | 50–60% |
| **Result** | What happened, with numbers: business outcome, technical outcome, effect on people | 10–15% |
| **+ Reflection** | What you learned, what you would do differently, what you now do as a result | 10% |

Aim for two to three minutes on the first telling: long enough to show the decisions, short enough to leave the interviewer ten minutes to probe. The most common structural mistake is an answer that is 70% situation, with the architecture, the org chart and the history, and thirty seconds for what you did. Cut context to what the listener needs to follow the actions.

## The STAR+ worksheet

Fill this in once per story. Writing it out, rather than keeping it in your head, is what makes the numbers and details available under pressure.

```text
STORY: <short memorable title, e.g. "Checkout stays synchronous">
Competencies it shows: <primary>, <secondary>, <secondary>
Scope: <people / teams involved>, <duration>, <users, requests, money at stake>

SITUATION (2 sentences)
  -
TASK: my responsibility, and what made it hard (1-2 sentences)
  -
ACTIONS (3-5 bullets; each is a decision or an influence move)
  - Decision:              Alternative rejected, and why:
  - Who I had to persuade: How:
  - What I did when it went wrong / someone disagreed:
RESULT (numbers; business outcome; what happened to people)
  -
+ REFLECTION
  - What I learned:
  - What I'd do differently:
  - What I do now because of this:

FOLLOW-UP PREP
  - My actions vs the team's (names):
  - Hardest question someone could ask about this:
  - The numbers (and how I know them):
  - Who disagreed, and what they would say about it:
```

The follow-up prep section is the part people skip and the part that saves them: it is the material for probes three and four, where the walk-throughs below show weak stories collapsing.

## Building a story bank

Eight to ten well-prepared stories, each showing two or three competencies, cover almost everything a behavioural round asks. Good sources: a project you led from an ambiguous start; a disagreement with a peer, manager or another team; a real failure; an incident you handled ([Incidents and postmortems](/learn/senior-craft/technical-leadership/incidents-and-postmortems)); a decision you changed without authority ([Leading without authority](/learn/senior-craft/technical-leadership/leading-without-authority)); mentoring someone through a hard stretch; a time you cut scope under pressure. | B. Search migration, 3 teams | ● | | | | ● | | ● |

## What makes a story senior

- **Scope beyond your own tasks.** "I fixed the flaky test" is not a senior story; "I got three teams to agree on test ownership after flaky tests cost us two release days a month" might be.
- **You set the direction.** The problem was fuzzy at the start, and you turned it into a plan others followed.
- **Real trade-offs.** Every option had a cost, and you can say what you gave up.
- **Influence without authority.** You changed the minds of people you did not manage, with data, prototypes, documents and one-to-one conversations.
- **Business outcomes.** Users, revenue, cost, reliability or time, not only "the code was cleaner".
- **Multipliers.** Other people got better because of what you did: a runbook, a mentee, a pattern another team adopted.
- **Honest failure.** Real stakes, your own mistake, and a concrete change in how you work.

## Story 1, weak: "we didn't move checkout"

The question: "Tell me about a time you disagreed with a technical decision." The illustrative weak telling, in full:

> "So at my last company we were a pretty big platform, lots of microservices, mostly Java and some Go, and we'd been on RabbitMQ for years, and there was this big push from the architecture group to move everything onto Kafka. A lot of people thought it was the future. Our team owned checkout, which is really important, and I felt the move didn't make sense for us, because Kafka would add latency and checkout needs to be fast. We had a lot of discussions about it, in meetings and on Slack, and some people on the team agreed with me and some didn't. In the end we decided not to move checkout, and it worked out well: checkout stayed fast and the other teams moved over. I think the lesson is that you shouldn't follow the hype; you have to pick the right tool for the job."

| Line | Interviewer's note |
|---|---|
| "a pretty big platform… RabbitMQ for years… a big push from the architecture group" | "about 40% of the answer is context; scale not quantified; decision owner unnamed" |
| "I felt the move didn't make sense… Kafka would add latency" | "a claim, no evidence; latency never measured" |
| "We had a lot of discussions… in meetings and on Slack" | "no personal action identifiable; 'we'" |
| "some people agreed with me and some didn't" | "other side's view not described" |
| "In the end we decided not to move checkout" | "who decided, by what mechanism?" |
| "checkout stayed fast and the other teams moved over" | "result unquantified; no business outcome" |
| "don't follow the hype; right tool for the job" | "reflection is a platitude; no changed behaviour" |

Nothing in it is false, and nothing in it can be scored above the bar. The interviewer now has to extract everything by probing, and what they find decides the round.

## Story 1, strong: checkout stays synchronous

The same disagreement, told by an illustrative candidate who did the work:

> **Situation.** "Two years ago our organisation decided to move service-to-service communication onto a new event bus. I was the tech lead for checkout, six engineers, handling about 40,000 orders a day."
>
> **Task.** "I agreed with the direction in general but thought it was wrong for the payment-authorisation step, where the customer is waiting on the answer. My job was to find out whether that concern was real and, if it was, to get an exception without undermining a migration the architecture group had already announced."
>
> **Actions.** "First, I checked whether I was right. I built a small prototype of authorisation over the bus and load-tested it: p99 went from 180 milliseconds to about 1.4 seconds, mostly from consumer batching, and we'd need idempotency keys and an outbox to avoid double charges. Second, I wrote a two-page document with those numbers, what would have to be true for async to work, and a proposal: authorisation stays synchronous, and everything after it, such as receipts, fulfilment and analytics, moves to the bus as planned. Third, before the review, I met the architect who led the migration one-to-one so the proposal wouldn't surprise him in public. He pushed back on having an inconsistent pattern, which was fair, so we agreed written criteria for when an exception is allowed, instead of a one-off carve-out for checkout."
>
> **Result.** "The exception and the criteria were accepted. The migration went ahead for about thirty other services on schedule, checkout latency and conversion were unaffected, and two quarters later another team used the criteria to justify their own exception in a day instead of a month."
>
> **Reflection.** "I raised it after the decision was announced, which cost two weeks and some goodwill. I now read organisation-level proposals during the comment period, and when I disagree, I bring numbers and talk to the author privately before any meeting."

| Line | Interviewer's note |
|---|---|
| "tech lead for checkout, six engineers, about 40,000 orders a day" | "scope quantified in two sentences; org-wide decision; revenue path" |
| "agreed with the direction in general… wrong for authorisation" | "precise disagreement, not blanket opposition" |
| "First, I checked whether I was right… p99 180 ms to about 1.4 s" | "tested own hypothesis before arguing; measured; knows the mechanism" |
| "idempotency keys and an outbox to avoid double charges" | "sees the correctness cost as well as latency" |
| "two-page document… what would have to be true for async to work" | "written proposal; states what would change their mind" |
| "met the architect one-to-one" | "influence without authority; no public surprise" |
| "which was fair… written criteria… instead of a carve-out" | "conceded a fair point; turned the exception into a mechanism" |
| "thirty other services on schedule… another team… in a day" | "business outcome plus a multiplier" |
| "raised it after the decision was announced… now read proposals during the comment period" | "own mistake with its cost; changed behaviour" |

About two and a half minutes. The ideas behind it (measure first, write it down, pre-wire the owner, concede the fair point) are the ones [Leading without authority](/learn/senior-craft/technical-leadership/leading-without-authority) and [Design docs and RFCs](/learn/senior-craft/technical-leadership/design-docs-and-rfcs) teach.

## Scoring story 1, line by line

Apply the anchored rubric to each telling. For each dimension, list the lines that are evidence, then find the highest anchor those lines fully satisfy.

| Dimension | Weak: evidence | Weak score | Strong: evidence | Strong score |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ownership and impact | Only "I felt"; everything else is "we" | 2 | Prototype, document, one-to-one, criteria; org-wide migration kept on schedule | 4 (5 needs a direction others followed beyond the exception) |
| Judgement | One option, never measured | 2 | Measured; sync against async with outbox; costs of each; "what would have to be true for async" | 5 |
| Collaboration and conflict | "Some agreed and some didn't"; other side absent | 2 | Private conversation first; conceded consistency; criteria another team reused | 5 |
| Growth and self-awareness | "Don't follow the hype" | 2 | Timing mistake, its cost (two weeks, goodwill), the changed habit | 4 |
| Communication | 40% context, no numbers | 2 | STAR+ in order, numbers, about 2.5 minutes | 4 |
| **Total** | | **10 / 25** | | **22 / 25** |

The strong telling scores below 5 in three places, and each gap names what would be needed: a direction that others followed, a system change after the mistake, and the follow-ups anticipated. The weak telling can still recover, because the interviewer will probe; the next section shows what happens when they do.

## Story 1 under the drill-down

| Probe | Strong candidate | Weak candidate | Interviewer's note |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. "What exactly did you measure, and how?" | "I wrote a load generator replaying a day of authorisation calls at twice the rate. Priya set up the consumer config with me. p99 went from 180 ms to about 1.4 s; tuning batch settings got it to about 600 ms and no lower." | "We saw in testing that it was slower. I don't remember the exact number." | Strong: "specific, personal, knows the limit of the number". Weak: "no measurement was theirs" |
| 2. "What would you have done if the architect had refused?" | "Asked the review for a decision with a date, with the data. If overruled, built async with the outbox and idempotency keys, added an alarm on authorisation p99, and said so in writing." | "I guess we would have moved and seen what happened." | Strong: "real fallback; disagree then commit with mitigation". Weak: "no plan" |
| 3. "Didn't the exception create the inconsistency the migration was meant to remove?" | "Yes, one more pattern to learn. That's why we wrote criteria instead of a carve-out; two exceptions have been granted under them since, both on paths where a user waits." | "No, checkout is different. It needed to be fast." | Strong: "conceded, then bounded the cost". Weak: "defensive; could not state the trade-off" |
| 4. "Who disagreed with you most, and what would they say about this?" | "The architect. He'd say I raised it late, and that he was right to insist on criteria." | "Some people on the team who liked Kafka. They'd probably say I was right in the end." | Strong: "represents the other side fairly". Weak: "other side as the losing party" |

The weak candidate's answers at probes 1 and 3 settle the round: the measurement was not theirs and the trade-off was never considered.

## Story 2, weak: "the cache ran out of memory"

A different competency: "Tell me about a mistake you made that had real consequences." The illustrative weak telling:

> "We had an outage during a big sale event; the cache ran out of memory. Honestly, traffic was about twice what product had forecast, so I don't think anyone could have predicted it. When it happened I jumped on the call and helped get things stable again pretty quickly; we scaled up the cache and it was fine. The thing I learned is that I care a lot about reliability, maybe too much sometimes, so now I always make sure we over-provision everything. It made our team much more resilient."

| Line | Interviewer's note |
|---|---|
| "We had an outage… the cache ran out of memory" | "passive; whose cache, whose sizing?" |
| "traffic was about twice what product had forecast… no one could have predicted it" | "asked for their mistake; deflected to product" |
| "I jumped on the call and helped" | "role in the incident unclear" |
| "we scaled up the cache and it was fine" | "impact not stated: users, duration, money" |
| "I care a lot about reliability, maybe too much" | "disguised strength" |
| "always over-provision everything" | "lesson is a cost, not a judgement" |

## Story 2, strong: the cache I sized wrong

> **Situation.** "Last year I designed the caching layer for our product-catalogue service, which serves every product page, about 3,000 requests a second at peak. I owned the design and the capacity plan."
>
> **Task.** "The mistake was my sizing. I sized the Redis cluster from the average item size, about 2 KB, times the number of hot items, with 30% headroom."
>
> **Actions.** "During our biggest sale of the year, a merchandising change added bundle products whose cached entries were ten to forty times the average. The cache hit its memory limit, evictions spiked, the hit rate fell from about 95% to about 60%, and the database behind it saturated. Product pages ran at a p99 of about three seconds for forty minutes, and conversion dropped in that window. I was on call. I found the evictions on the cache dashboard within about ten minutes, shed the recommendations call that was doubling cache reads, and failed over to a larger node type, which took about twenty more. In the postmortem I put my sizing decision first among the causes: I had sized on the mean when the risk was in the tail and the key count, and I had never load-tested with production-shaped data."
>
> **Result.** "We resized from the measured p99 item size and key growth, alerted on eviction rate rather than memory alone, and added a load test at twice forecast peak with production-shaped payloads to the release checklist. The next sale ran with evictions flat."
>
> **Reflection.** "I size from distributions now, not averages. I write down the assumptions a capacity plan depends on and who would know if they changed, and before a peak I ask merchandising what they plan to launch. Two other teams now use the sizing template."

| Line | Interviewer's note |
|---|---|
| "I designed the caching layer… I owned the design and the capacity plan" | "owns it in the first sentence" |
| "sized from the average item size… 30% headroom" | "names the specific decision that was wrong" |
| "bundle products… ten to forty times the average" | "mechanism of failure, not only the symptom" |
| "hit rate 95% to 60%… p99 about three seconds for forty minutes" | "impact quantified for users and revenue" |
| "found the evictions… shed recommendations… failed over" | "personal actions under pressure, in order, with timings" |
| "put my sizing decision first among the causes" | "owned publicly although the trigger was external" |
| "sized on the mean when the risk was in the tail" | "names the reasoning error, not only the number" |
| "eviction-rate alert… production-shaped load test" | "fixed the system, not only this instance" |
| "Two other teams now use the sizing template" | "multiplier" |

A failure story done well is one of the strongest senior signals available, because clean ownership of a real mistake is what makes someone safe to give autonomy to. [Capacity planning and cost](/learn/system-design/senior-design-skills/capacity-planning-and-cost) covers the sizing mistake itself.

## Handling follow-ups

Interviewers probe in predictable ways:

| Follow-up type | Example | What it tests | How to handle it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Drill-down | "What exactly did you do in the load test?" | Whether the actions were yours | Specifics: tools, numbers, your part and others' by name |
| Counterfactual | "What if the architect had refused?" | Judgement beyond the happy path | Your real fallback, and why |
| Reflection | "What would you do differently?" | Self-awareness | A genuine mistake, not a disguised strength |
| Challenge | "Didn't that create inconsistency?" | Holding a position while hearing criticism | Concede what is fair, then explain the trade-off and how you bounded it |
| Scope | "How many teams were affected?" | Whether the scope is real | Exact numbers; if unsure, say roughly and why |
| Other people | "How did your manager see it?" | Collaboration, political awareness | Their view fairly, including where it differed from yours |

Four techniques carry most of the weight. **Answer the question asked**: a drill-down wants one level more detail on one part, not a retelling. **Pause before hard ones**: three seconds of thought reads as care. **Be precise about what you do not know**: "about 1.4 seconds at p99, from the load-test dashboard; I don't remember the decimal" beats a confident invented figure. **Concede fair points**: probe 3 in story 1 is where defensiveness most often ends a round.

## Trade-offs: which story to tell

| Choice | Credibility under drill-down | Level evidence | Competency coverage | Main risk | Prefer it when |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Recent story (last 2–3 years), smaller scope | High: details are fresh | Moderate | One or two | Reads mid-level if the scope is own-team only | Its scope is at least cross-team |
| Older story (4+ years), larger scope | Lower: numbers fade | High | One or two | "What have you done recently?" | Paired with a recent story in the same loop |
| Technical-depth story | High on judgement | High for an IC senior | Judgement, ownership | Collaboration left unevidenced | The interviewer's competencies are judgement or ownership |
| People-and-influence story | Moderate | High for cross-team scope | Conflict, influence | No technical substance to probe | The competencies are conflict or influence |
| One flagship story for every question | High, since it is rehearsed | High, once | Low | A second interviewer hears it again; competencies go empty | Never as the only story; use it for the deep-dive |
| Scripted word for word | Low: brittle when interrupted | Unchanged | Unchanged | Sounds recited; collapses at probe 2 | Never |
| Outline: bullets and numbers | High | Unchanged | Unchanged | Rambling if under-rehearsed | Always, rehearsed aloud |

## Failure modes

**Symptom: stories collapse at the third follow-up.** The questions get more specific while your answers get vaguer ("I'd have to check"), and the write-up says "could not go below the surface". Diagnosis: the story was prepared only to the depth of the first telling, or the key actions were someone else's. Fix: fill in the worksheet's follow-up prep for every action: your part, the number and its source, who disagreed; then have someone ask "how?" three times in a row.

**Symptom: the interviewer keeps asking "what was your part?"** Diagnosis: "we" everywhere, a good habit at work that leaves your column empty in the notes. Fix: "I" for your actions, "we" for outcomes, and others named for theirs: "I built the prototype; Priya ran the load test with me; the team shipped the migration."

**Symptom: after your failure story, the interviewer asks "and what was your mistake?"** Diagnosis: a safe failure, a deflection or a humblebrag, like story 2's weak version. Fix: choose a failure with a real cost whose root cause includes your decision, and tell it in four parts: what went wrong and what it cost, your part, how you contained it, what changed in you and in the system.

**Symptom: "do you have a different example?"** Diagnosis: one flagship story reused for every question, or a story the previous interviewer already heard, so the packet shows one story and several empty competencies. Fix: eight to ten stories mapped in the matrix; reuse at most once per loop, and then from a different angle for a different competency.

**Symptom: "what have you done in the last two years?"** Diagnosis: every strong story is from a previous job. Fix: reframe recent work for scope (it is usually bigger than you describe it); if an older story is your best, say so and pair it with a recent one.

## Interviewer follow-ups

**"Can you give me a more recent example?"** Model answer: have one ready; if the most recent at that scope is older, say so and offer a smaller recent story that shows the same judgement. Common wrong answer: retelling the same story with different emphasis, or improvising one that falls apart at the next probe.

**"What was your part, specifically, versus the team's?"** Model answer: two or three decisions or actions that were yours, one argument you lost, and teammates credited by name. Common wrong answer: "it was really a team effort", which reads as no contribution, or claiming everything, which the next probe exposes.

**"What would you do differently?"** Model answer: a specific change to an early decision, why, and the habit you have now (story 1's comment-period rule). Common wrong answer: "nothing, it went well", or "communicate more", which changes nothing.

**"Tell me about a time you were wrong about something technical."** Model answer: a belief you held, the evidence that changed your mind, and what you changed as a result. Common wrong answer: a story in which you were right and everyone else was wrong.

**"How did your manager see it?"** Model answer: their view, stated fairly, including where it differed from yours and what you took from it. Common wrong answer: "they agreed with me", which tells the interviewer you did not ask.

## What mid-level engineers get wrong

- **Improvising.** No bank means pauses and the first story that comes to mind, usually small; the write-up says "limited scope".
- **Choosing stories by outcome, not evidence.** A project that went smoothly often has no visible decisions; the score stays at 3.
- **Answering hypothetically.** "I would…" gets redirected, and a second redirect becomes "no example provided".
- **A five-minute first telling.** One competency gets covered and the interviewer's others go empty.
- **Treating reflection as optional.** The most predictable follow-up goes unanswered.
- **Inventing or merging stories.** Composites collapse at probe three, and a credibility doubt sinks the whole packet, not only the round.
- **Rereading notes instead of rehearsing aloud.** The worksheet is complete and the telling is still 70% situation.

## Practising

Rehearsal out loud improves behavioural answers far more than rereading notes.

1. Fill in the worksheet for eight stories and build both matrices.
2. Tell each aloud against a timer. Over three minutes, cut the situation, not the actions.
3. Score your own telling against the anchored rubric, line by line, as in story 1.
4. Run a behavioural mock on `/interviews`: 30 minutes of senior-level questions with follow-ups that probe ownership, judgement and conflict. The report scores the five dimensions with quoted evidence; add every probe that exposed a thin spot to that story's follow-up prep.
5. Record yourself once. You will hear the "we", the filler and the missing numbers immediately.

If your honest stories feel too small for senior, check first whether you are underselling them (usually the problem); if the scope is small, that is a reason to gain it before the move, never to inflate it.

## Senior signals

- Your stories have cross-team scope, a fuzzy start you turned into a plan, and results in business terms.
- The action section dominates and names decisions, rejected alternatives and how you influenced people you did not manage.
- You separate "I" from "we" precisely and credit others by name.
- Your failure stories own a real decision with a real cost, separate the trigger from the cause, and show a changed habit and a changed system.
- You hold up at the third and fourth probe: you know your numbers and their sources, concede fair challenges and say plainly what you do not know.
- You arrive with a bank mapped to competencies, so every interviewer in the loop hears a different story.
