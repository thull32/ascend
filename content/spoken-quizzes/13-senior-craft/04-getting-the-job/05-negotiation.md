## 1

In the lesson's illustrative bands, in index points, the mid-level band runs from 80 to 120, and the senior band from 105 to 155. A strong negotiation takes a mid-level offer from 100 to 112. Why does levelling still matter more?

A, titles are what future employers pay for, not the pay figure itself. B, base is fixed by band, so only a level change can move the offer. C, the overlap means the two levels pay the same in most cases anyway. D, the senior midpoint, 130, sits above the entire mid-level range.

[think]

The answer is D: the senior midpoint, 130, sits above the entire mid-level range.

Negotiation moves you within a range; levelling moves the range. The senior midpoint exceeds the mid-level maximum, and raises, refreshers and bonus targets are keyed to level, so the gap compounds. The overlap is real, but narrow at the edges, and base can move within its band.

## 2

A stock grant of 400 thousand dollars is converted to shares at a 30 day average price of 200 dollars. On your first day, the stock trades at 220. What do you hold?

A, about 1,818 shares, with the gap paid in cash. B, 2,000 shares, worth 400 thousand dollars until they vest. C, 2,000 shares, worth 440 thousand dollars at today's price. D, about 1,818 shares, worth exactly 400 thousand dollars.

[think]

The answer is C: 2,000 shares, worth 440 thousand dollars at today's price.

The share count is fixed at conversion: 400 thousand divided by 200 is 2,000 shares. From then on, their value moves with the price, so on day one they are worth 2,000 times 220. Conversion does not happen at the first-day price, and no cash top-up covers price movements.

## 3

An initial grant of 400 vests evenly over four years, and a refresher of 50 is granted each year, each vesting evenly over the following four. Why does annual equity fall sharply in year five?

A, the cliff restarts for each new refresher that is granted. B, the initial grant ends, and the steady state is one refresher a year. C, refreshers are forfeited at the four-year work anniversary. D, the refreshers only begin vesting after the initial grant ends.

[think]

The answer is B: the initial grant ends, and the steady state is one refresher a year.

Grants overlap: through year four, the initial 100 a year sits on top of slices of the refreshers, reaching 137.5. When it ends, only four slices of 12.5 remain, so equity drops to 50. In the steady state, annual equity equals the annual refresher, which is why refresher practice matters as much as the initial grant.

## 4

Your restricted stock units vest, and the employer withholds tax at the flat supplemental rate. Your marginal federal rate is higher. What happens?

A, the employer's withholding settles it, so nothing more is owed. B, the employer must refund the difference in the next payroll. C, the shortfall is due when you file, as a separate tax bill. D, tax is deferred until you sell the shares, so none is due yet.

[think]

The answer is C: the shortfall is due when you file.

In the US, vested stock units are ordinary income at vest, and withholding at a flat supplemental rate can fall short of your marginal rate, so the gap is owed when you file. Selling later creates a separate gain or loss, relative to the value on the vest day. Rules differ by country, so check with a tax professional.

## 5

Investors hold preferred shares with a 150 million dollar, one times, non-participating liquidation preference, and 40 percent of the company. The company sells for 200 million. What do common shareholders receive?

A, 60 percent of 200 million, since preferred converts at any sale. B, 200 million, since the preference applies only in failure. C, nothing, since all the proceeds go to the investors. D, 50 million, since preferred takes its 150 million first.

[think]

The answer is D: 50 million, since preferred takes its 150 million first.

Converting would give the preferred holders 40 percent of 200 million, which is 80 million, less than their 150 million preference. So they take the preference, and common shares the remaining 50 million. In the lesson's example, that is about 0.67 per common share, below a strike price of 1, so the options are worth nothing despite the sale.

## 6

Why do compensation approvers usually agree to a sign-on bonus or a larger equity grant more readily than to a higher base salary?

A, sign-on bonuses are not taxed, so they cost the company less. B, base increases need board approval at most public companies. C, equity is free to the company, since it is only newly issued shares. D, base recurs, drives raises and bonus, and is compared with peers.

[think]

The answer is D: base recurs, drives raises and bonus, and is compared with peers.

Base is paid every year, raises and bonus are percentages of it, and paying a new hire above existing peers at the level creates compression. A sign-on bonus is one-off and protected by a clawback, and an equity grant comes from a budget. Both have real costs and tax treatment, but not the recurring, internal-equity cost of base.
