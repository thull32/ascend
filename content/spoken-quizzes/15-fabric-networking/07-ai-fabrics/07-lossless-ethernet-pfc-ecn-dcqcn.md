## 1

On rail1-leaf1, the priority 3 buffer for traffic arriving from srv1 crosses XOFF, because srv1 and srv2 are both sending to srv3. Where does the PFC pause frame go, and what does it stop?

A, to srv1, stopping only its queue pair that sends to srv3. B, to srv1, stopping all of its priority 3 traffic to any receiver. C, to every port on the switch, stopping all priorities briefly. D, to srv3, telling it to stop accepting priority 3 traffic.

[think]

The answer is B: to srv1, stopping all of its priority 3 traffic to any receiver.

PFC acts on the ingress priority group, so the pause goes upstream, out of the port the bytes arrived on, to srv1. It names a priority, not a flow or a destination, so while paused srv1 cannot send priority 3 traffic to anyone, even an idle receiver. Slowing only the queue pair that sends to srv3 is what DCQCN does, end to end. PFC never pauses the receiver, and other priorities on the same link keep flowing.

## 2

A 400 gigabit lossless port's headroom was computed for the switch's default cable length of 100 metres, but the actual run is 300 metres. What happens under a heavy burst?

A, some priority 3 packets are dropped after the pause is sent. B, nothing, because headroom only matters for links of several kilometres. C, PFC fires earlier, because XOFF scales with the cable length. D, the NIC detects the length and lengthens its pause timer.

[think]

The answer is A: some priority 3 packets are dropped after the pause is sent.

Headroom must absorb the bytes still arriving after the decision to pause: the pause's trip up the cable, the bytes already on it, the NIC's reaction, and frames in progress. 200 extra metres of fibre add 2 microseconds of round trip, about 100 kilobytes at 400 gigabits, that the reservation does not cover, so the lossless class overflows exactly when it is needed. XOFF does not move with the cable, and nothing adapts on its own.

## 3

In the lesson's DCQCN trace, both senders cut to 200 gigabits at 29 microseconds, so together they send exactly the 400 gigabits the port drains. Why does the queue stay at 450 kilobytes until the second cut?

A, the first cut balanced the rates but did not remove the backlog. B, PFC is holding the queue full until it sends an XON frame. C, the switch keeps marked packets queued until their CNPs return. D, the receiving NIC stopped draining while it generated the CNPs.

[think]

The answer is A: the first cut balanced the rates but did not remove the backlog.

The 450 kilobytes built up during the feedback delay: the marked packet waited 4 microseconds in the queue, then the CNP took another microsecond. With input equal to output a queue neither grows nor shrinks, so only a second cut below the drain rate, allowed once the 50 microsecond CNP interval had passed, could empty it. No pause was sent in this run, and marking never holds packets back.

## 4

A RoCE cluster's switches report zero ECN-marked packets during jobs, while PFC pause counters on the same ports rise steadily. Which explanation fits?

A, pauses are expected to rise before the first ECN mark is ever set. B, ECN is working, and marks are only counted on the receiving NIC. C, the CNP queue is strict priority, so it hides the ECN marks. D, ECN's Kmin is above the dynamic PFC XOFF, or ECN is off for the class.

[think]

The answer is D: ECN's Kmin is above the dynamic PFC XOFF, or ECN is off for the class.

In a correct design ECN marking starts below PFC's XOFF, so marks appear before pauses. Rising pauses with zero switch marks mean marking never engages: thresholds above XOFF, ECN disabled on the lossless traffic class, or traffic sent as not ECN-capable. Switches count their own marks, and how the CNP queue is scheduled has nothing to do with whether data packets are marked.

## 5

Why does RoCE version 2 carry its congestion signal back to the sender in a separate CNP, rather than in its acknowledgements, as TCP does with its ECN echo flag?

A, RoCE has no per-packet acknowledgement stream to piggyback on, so the NIC sends a CNP. B, CNPs are encrypted, and RoCE acknowledgements cannot carry protected fields. C, a CNP is lossless on priority 3, so it is guaranteed to arrive. D, switches drop acknowledgements carrying ECN bits on the lossless priority.

[think]

The answer is A: RoCE has no per-packet acknowledgement stream to piggyback on, so the NIC sends a CNP.

Reliable connected acknowledgements are coalesced and generated by the transport, not one per data packet, so there is no steady reverse stream to piggyback on. The receiving NIC sends a dedicated, rate-limited CNP naming the sender's queue pair. CNPs go in a strict-priority, lossy queue, not the lossless class, so arrival is fast but not guaranteed. And nothing in RoCE is encrypted by default.
