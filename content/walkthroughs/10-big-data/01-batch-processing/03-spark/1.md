@0
A router and three partitions, S1, S2 and S3. The rule is one line: hash the key and take it mod 3. A shuffle assigns rows to partitions exactly this way. Nearby keys land in unrelated places, and any machine can compute the answer without a shared table.

@1-3
Alice hashes to a number that leaves 2 when divided by 3, so she goes to S3. Bob also lands on 2, S3. Hank, 2 again, S3.

@4
Judy's hash leaves 0, so she goes to S1.

@5
Four keys, and the partitions hold 1, 0 and 3. With this few keys the split is lumpy; hashing evens out across many distinct keys. What it does guarantee is that sequential keys, order 3,001, 3,002 and so on, scatter just like names do, so there is no hot tail.

@6
First cost. A range query, names from a to c, cannot be routed, because hashing destroyed the order. The router has to scatter it to every partition and merge the answers. The cost grows with the number of partitions, and the latency is the slowest one's.

@7
Second cost. Add a fourth partition and the rule becomes hash mod 4. Now Bob is on S1, Hank on S2, and Alice and Judy on S4. All four keys moved. In general about three quarters of the keys move, and in a shuffle that means changing the partition count reshuffles almost everything. Consistent hashing exists to shrink that to about a quarter.

@8
The third cost is the one in the title. Hashing spreads keys, not load. Every row for one key goes to the same partition, so a single hot key, like a new series with 18 percent of yesterday's plays, sends all of its rows to one task. In Spark that is the one task that never finishes. The fixes split the hot key: let adaptive query execution split the skewed partition, handle the hot keys separately, or salt them across several sub-keys.

@9
So hash partitioning gives you uniform spread of distinct keys and routing with no shared state, at the price of scatter-gather range queries and painful repartitioning. Range partitioning is the mirror image. When a stage's slowest task takes ten times the median, suspect one key, not the cluster.
