@0
Writers, a query, one partition of a MergeTree table, and the background merge threads. MergeTree has no memtable. Every insert is sorted by the table's sort key and written straight to disk as its own immutable part, and background merges combine parts later. It is the LSM idea, with parts as the sorted runs.

@1
A batched insert of 8,192 rows becomes the first part. Its name says what it is: partition 202405, blocks 1 to 1, level 0. Its columns, sparse index and marks are written once and never modified.

@2-3
A second insert becomes a second part, blocks 2 to 2, and a third becomes blocks 3 to 3. Parts are cheap to write, because nothing that already exists is touched.

@4
The price shows up on the read side. A query must consult every active part, because each has its own sparse index and its own files: three parts, three index lookups.

@5
A background merge reads the first two parts in sort order and writes one, blocks 1 to 2 at level 1. The inputs are deleted once the merge commits. Merges are the only way rows ever move.

@6
Another merge folds in the third part, giving blocks 1 to 3 at level 2. The partition is one part again, sorted end to end.

@7
The same query now opens one part. The merge paid a rewrite to make every later read cheaper, exactly the trade an LSM tree makes.

@8
Now the failure mode. Five hundred application servers each insert one row at a time. Every insert is a part, and parts arrive far faster than merges can combine them. Past 1,000 active parts in the partition, inserts are slowed down; past the next limit, 3,000 since release 23.6, they fail with a too many parts error.

@9
The fix is on the writer's side: batch thousands of rows per insert, about once a second, turn on asynchronous inserts so the server buffers the small ones, or write through Kafka and let a consumer batch.

@10
Druid and Pinot segments follow the same pattern: immutable sorted runs, written once and merged in the background. Write cost is deferred to the merges, and reads get cheaper as the runs get fewer and larger.
