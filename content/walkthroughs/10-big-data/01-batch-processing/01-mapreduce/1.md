@0
A word count, run the MapReduce way. On screen is a master at the top, three mappers in the middle, two reducers at the bottom, and three lines of input: the cat sat, the dog sat, and the the the the ran. Hold onto the contract, because it is the whole model. You write two functions, map and reduce. The framework splits the input, schedules the work, moves every pair with the same key to the same reducer, and re-runs anything that fails.

@1
The master hands out three map tasks, one split each. It prefers a worker that already holds that split on its own disk, because shipping a small program to the data is cheaper than shipping the data to the program.

@2
Each mapper calls map on its line and emits the word with a count of 1 for every word it sees. Mapper M1 emits the 1, cat 1, sat 1. M3, with its repeated word, emits the 1 four times and then ran 1. Eleven pairs in all. Map looks at one record at a time and remembers nothing, which is why the three mappers never need to talk to each other.

@3
Next, the combiner: the reduce function run locally on each mapper's output before anything crosses the network. M3's four pairs for the collapse into one, the 4, so eleven pairs become eight. M1 and M2 saw no word twice, so theirs are unchanged. The combiner is only allowed because adding is associative and commutative; it changes how many bytes carry the answer, never the answer.

@4
The shuffle begins with partitioning. Every pair goes to a reducer chosen by a hash of its word: here, the sum of its character codes, mod 2, the same function as the hand trace in the lesson. The sums to 321 and ran to 321, both odd, so they go to reducer 1. Cat, sat and dog have even sums and go to reducer 0. Each mapper writes one sorted segment per reducer to its own local disk.

@5
Reducer 0 fetches its segment from all three mappers, then merges and sorts by key. This all-to-all transfer is the expensive part of every job.

@6
Now the failure the design exists for. M2's worker dies after reducer 0 has fetched from it, but before reducer 1 has. M2's output lived on its local disk, so both of its segments are gone.

@7
The master notices the missed heartbeats, marks M2's output lost, and re-runs the same split on M1, which regenerates both segments with identical bytes. Map is deterministic, so a re-run is always safe. Reducer 0 does nothing: the master tracks fetches per map and reduce pair, and its copy is already merged.

@8
Reducer 1 is told the new location and fetches M2's segment from M1, along with the others. Nothing is lost, and nothing is counted twice.

@9
Each reducer calls reduce once per key and writes one output file. Reducer 0 writes cat 1, dog 1 and sat 2. Reducer 1 adds the partial counts for the, 1 plus 1 plus 4, and writes the 6 and ran 1. The combiner changed how many pairs carried those counts, not the answer.

@10
Two more ways a job goes slow. A straggler, one slow machine, holds up the end of the job, so the master runs backup copies of the last tasks and takes whichever finishes first. And key skew: one hot word sends most of the values to a single reducer, which finishes long after the rest.

@11
That is the trade. Writing every stage to disk gives simple, restartable fault tolerance on cheap hardware, but it makes iterative and multi-stage jobs slow. Spark keeps intermediate data in memory and recomputes a lost partition from its lineage instead. Faster, and it needs more care. Either way, the shuffle is where the time goes, so it is the first thing to look at in any slow job.
