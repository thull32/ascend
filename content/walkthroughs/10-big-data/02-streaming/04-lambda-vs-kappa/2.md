@0
A play service on the left, one retained log of plays in the middle, and three views on the right: a search index, a cache and a warehouse. Each view is its own consumer group reading the same records at its own pace. Hold onto one idea: reading consumes nothing. The log is the source of truth, and each view's progress is just an offset into it.

@1
The play service appends three events, at offsets 0 to 2, and moves on. It does not know which views exist.

@2
All three groups read offsets 0 to 2 independently and fold them into their own view: a document per title in the index, a key per title in the cache, a row per play in the warehouse. Each commits offset 3. One copy of the data, three readers.

@3
Now the warehouse loader goes down, and two more plays arrive, at offsets 3 and 4. Search and cache read them at once. The warehouse simply falls behind: its offset stays at 3 and its lag is 2. Nothing is queued per subscriber; there is only the log and an offset per group.

@4
The loader comes back, resumes from its committed offset, 3, and catches up to 5. Nothing was lost, and nobody else waited.

@5
Now add a view: recommendations, as a new consumer group starting at offset 0. The producer is not changed, and not even told.

@6
The new group replays the whole retained log, offsets 0 to 4, builds its view from history, and then keeps up with new events like the others. Fixing a buggy view works the same way: run the new code from offset 0 into a fresh table and switch readers over. That is Kappa's reprocessing, rebuilding a table from its changelog with a new function.

@7
The catch: a view can be rebuilt only from what the log still holds. Retention, or compaction down to the latest record per key, must cover the rebuild. And every view is eventually consistent with the log, each lagging by its own offset.

@8
So deriving every view from one log makes adding, fixing and rebuilding views cheap, and keeps them all consistent with one order of events. The price is keeping the log for as long as a rebuild needs it, and views that are only eventually up to date. It is the same shape everywhere: a database's tables materialised from its write-ahead log, a search index rebuilt from change data capture.
