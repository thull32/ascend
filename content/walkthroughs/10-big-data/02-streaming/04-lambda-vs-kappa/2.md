@0
An order service publishes to a topic called orders, and three subscribers listen: billing, email and analytics. This picture is drawn as a classic publish and subscribe broker, with services rather than derived tables, but the shape is the one the lesson cares about: every subscriber gets its own copy of every event, and does its own thing with it.

@1
Each subscriber registers interest. The order service never learns who is listening, so adding a fourth subscriber later needs no change to the publisher. In the lesson's terms, a new view is a new subscriber, not a change to the producer.

@2-3
The order service publishes order placed, number 1, and returns as soon as the broker has it. The broker delivers it to all three subscribers, and each acknowledges independently. One publish, three deliveries.

@4-5
Analytics goes offline. Order 2 is published while it is down.

@6-7
Billing and email get order 2 at once. Analytics cannot, so the broker parks order 2 in a backlog kept just for analytics. Order 3 follows, and the backlog grows to two. The broker is now holding state for each subscriber; that is what makes the outage survivable, and what costs it memory or disk.

@8
Analytics reconnects, and the broker replays orders 2 and 3 to it, in order. Nothing was lost, and the publisher never knew.

@9
The catch: a subscriber that is permanently slow grows an endless backlog, so every broker needs a retention policy. A log-based broker like Kafka sidesteps the per-subscriber copies entirely. There is one shared log, and each subscriber keeps only an offset into it. That is the one log of the title.

@10
And that is what makes derived views cheap. A search index, a cache and a warehouse table can each read the same log at their own pace. A new view is a new reader that starts at the beginning and replays, with no change to the producer. Delivery is at least once per reader, so each one must apply events idempotently.
