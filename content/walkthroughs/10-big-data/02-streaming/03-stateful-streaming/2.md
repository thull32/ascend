@0
A producer, a buffer that holds 8 messages, and a consumer that can process 2 per tick. Backpressure is the consumer's ability to slow the producer down. Without it, a fast producer and a slow consumer are separated only by a buffer, and every buffer is finite.

@1
First, no backpressure. The producer pushes a burst of 20 messages at once. The buffer keeps 8, and the other 12 have nowhere to go, so they are dropped. Had the buffer been unbounded, the process would instead have grown until it was killed for running out of memory.

@2
The consumer drains 2, its real capacity, leaving 6. The 12 dropped messages are gone, and nobody upstream was told: the producer believes it delivered 20.

@3
The fix is to flip the direction of control. The consumer tells the producer how many messages it may send, as credits, a window, or a request for n, and the producer may not exceed that.

@4-5
The buffer has 2 free slots, so the consumer grants exactly 2 credits. The producer sends 2 and stops. With zero credits it has to wait, so the buffer cannot overflow, by construction.

@6
Each tick the consumer processes 2 and hands back 2 credits. In steady state the producer runs at exactly the consumer's rate, and the buffer only absorbs jitter.

@7
And the pressure travels upstream. A blocked producer stops reading from its own source, whose callers then wait. That is the point: the slowest stage sets the pace for the whole pipeline, and overload shows up at the edge instead of as silent loss in the middle. In Flink, credit-based flow control carries it from operator to operator all the way back to the Kafka source, whose lag grows.

@8
Waiting is not always acceptable. For metrics and telemetry, shedding load by dropping or sampling is the right policy; for orders it is not. And watch for cycles: two stages each blocked waiting on the other is a deadlock.

@9
So backpressure guarantees bounded memory and an honest overload signal, at the price of tying the producer's speed to the consumer's. One more consequence for a stream processor: checkpoint barriers travel through these same full buffers, and an aligned barrier waits behind every buffered record. That is why checkpoints slow down, and can time out, under backpressure.
