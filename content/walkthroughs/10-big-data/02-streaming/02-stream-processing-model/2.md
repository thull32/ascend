@0
A source, one window covering event times 0 to 10 seconds, and a sink. Two clocks are in play: event time, when something happened, stamped by the device, and processing time, when it reaches us. Events arrive out of order, so the wall clock cannot tell us the window is complete. The watermark makes that call, and its rule is one line: the watermark is the largest event time seen so far, minus a bound of 2 seconds.

@1-3
Event 1 happened at 1 second; the watermark becomes minus 1. Event 2, stamped 4 seconds, moves it to 2. Event 3, stamped 9 seconds, arrives only 3 seconds into processing, and moves the watermark to 7. All three count: the window holds 3.

@4
Event 4 is stamped 3 seconds, after we have already seen 9. It is out of order, but the window still has not been declared complete: its end, 10, is beyond the watermark of 7. So it counts, and the window holds 4. The watermark stays at 7, because the largest event time is still 9.

@5
Event 5 is stamped 12 seconds. The watermark jumps to 10, which reaches the end of the window, so the operator now believes nothing more is coming and emits a count of 4. It fired 5 seconds into processing, long before the wall clock said 10: completeness is decided in event time.

@6
Event 6 is stamped 7 seconds, but the watermark is already 10 and the window has fired. It is late. By default it is dropped or sent to a side output; with allowed lateness the window would be kept and a corrected result emitted.

@7-8
Event 7, stamped 15 seconds, goes to the next window and moves the watermark to 13. Then event 8 arrives, stamped 2 seconds: late again, and dropped.

@9
So the sink was told 4, and the true count for the window was 6. The two late events are the price of deciding early. A bound of 6 seconds would have caught both, and every result would have arrived 4 seconds later.

@10
Three ways this goes wrong in production. The watermark is a guess, and late data is dropped silently, so measure it. One stalled source partition holds the watermark back for the whole job, and nothing fires; idle-source timeouts exist for exactly that. And a device with a skewed clock stamps events in the future, dragging the watermark past live data.

@11
The bound is a direct dial between latency and completeness. Small bound: fast results and more late data. Large bound: slower results and more buffered state. Flink and Beam let you have both: early speculative results, a final result at the watermark, and corrections for data within the allowed lateness.
