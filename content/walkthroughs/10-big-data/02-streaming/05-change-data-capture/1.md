@0
An app, Postgres, a change data capture connector, and a search index. The app writes only to Postgres. It never touches the index. Instead, the connector tails the database's write-ahead log and turns every committed change into an event, so the database's own commit order becomes the event stream.

@1
Setup comes first. The connector creates a replication slot, which makes Postgres keep the log from that point on, and takes a consistent snapshot of the rows that already exist, streaming them as if they were inserts. From then on it only needs the log.

@2-3
The app inserts user 1, Ada, and commits. Postgres writes it to the log at position 1 before acknowledging. The connector decodes position 1 into a change event carrying the row's before and after images, the index applies it as an upsert, and the connector's confirmed position moves to 1. The gap between the commit and the apply is the staleness a search user can see.

@4-5
The app updates user 1 to Ada L. That is position 2, decoded and applied the same way.

@6-7
An order, number 9 for user 1, is inserted at position 3, and applied.

@8-9
Then user 1 is deleted, at position 4. The connector emits a delete event, and the index deletes the document by its primary key. Notice that a delete arrives as an event of its own; a job that polled the table for changes would never see it.

@10
Now the connector crashes after emitting position 4 but before confirming it to the slot. On restart it resumes from its last confirmed position, 3, and emits position 4 again. Delivery is at least once, so the index must apply events idempotently: upsert by key, or deduplicate by log position.

@11
The second failure is quieter. The slot pins the log. If the connector stays down for three days, Postgres keeps every log segment since the confirmed position, and the primary's disk fills, here to 95 percent. Monitor slot lag, and cap how much log a slot may retain.

@12
Why go to all this trouble instead of having the app write to both? With a dual write, a crash between the two writes loses an update, and two concurrent writers can reach the index in the opposite order. Tailing one log makes the database the single arbiter of order. The index can lag, but it cannot disagree.

@13
So change data capture gives you the database's exact order with no application changes, and it sees deletes and every intermediate update. The costs: consumers are coupled to the table's schema, delivery is at least once, and you now operate slots, lag and connector restarts.
