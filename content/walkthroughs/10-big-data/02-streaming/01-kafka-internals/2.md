@0
A client, a leader, and two followers. This picture is drawn as a generic leader and follower database, with a value x and log positions, but the question it answers is Kafka's acks setting: when does the leader say OK? Everyone starts in step: x is 1, at log position 7.

@1-2
The client writes x equals 2. The leader appends it at position 8 and applies it. In asynchronous mode the leader acknowledges immediately, before either follower has the change. That is acks equals 1: the leader has it, nobody else does yet.

@3
Then the change goes out to the followers. On a good day that takes milliseconds; under load, or across regions, it can take seconds.

@4-5
Meanwhile this system serves a read from follower 1, which has not applied position 8. The client just wrote 2 and reads 1, so its own write seems to have vanished. Kafka sidesteps this particular trap: consumers read from the leader by default, and only below the high watermark, the point every in-sync replica has reached.

@6-7
Milliseconds later both followers apply position 8 and catch up. A database fixes the stale read by sending a recent writer's reads to the leader, or by making the follower wait until it has applied the writer's position.

@8
Now synchronous mode. The leader waits for follower 1 to confirm each write before acknowledging.

@9-10
The client writes x equals 3. The leader logs it at position 9, ships it, and waits.

@11-12
Follower 1 confirms position 9, and only then does the client hear OK. The write exists on two nodes before anyone is told it succeeded, and the price is one extra replication round trip on every write.

@13-14
The leader dies. The most up-to-date follower must be chosen and promoted, and clients pointed at it. Follower 1 has position 9, so it becomes leader and follower 2 follows it. Because the acknowledgement waited for follower 1, x equals 3 survives. Had the leader acknowledged asynchronously and died before the change went out, an acknowledged write would be gone. And the old leader must be fenced off before it can come back believing it still leads.

@15
That is the trade. Asynchronous acknowledgement is fast but can lose acknowledged writes in a failover; synchronous costs a round trip and stalls when the follower is slow. Kafka's answer is the configuration triple to remember: replication factor 3, minimum in-sync replicas 2, and acks equals all. One broker can die with no acknowledged record lost.
