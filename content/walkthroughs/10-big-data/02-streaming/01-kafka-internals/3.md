@0
A producer, a queue, two workers, and a dead-letter queue. This is a generic work queue, with per-message acknowledgements and a visibility timeout, rather than Kafka's offsets, but within one consumer group Kafka behaves the same way: each record is handled by one member, and an uncommitted record comes back after a crash. The queue decouples the producer from the workers in time and in rate.

@1
The producer publishes a burst of 12 messages. The broker stores each one and acknowledges straight away, so the producer never waits for processing. Depth: 12.

@2-3
Worker 1 receives message 1. The broker does not delete it; it hides it for 30 seconds so nobody else picks it up. Worker 2 takes message 2. Two workers in parallel, and each message goes to exactly one of them: competing consumers.

@4
Worker 1 finishes and acknowledges, and only now is message 1 deleted. Acknowledging after the work is what protects against a worker crash.

@5-6
And here is the crash: worker 2 dies halfway through message 2. It never acknowledged, so the message is still there, merely hidden. When the 30 seconds run out it becomes visible again, and worker 1 receives it, attempt 2. Nothing was lost, but a message can be delivered twice: at least once.

@7
Worker 1 finishes message 2 and acknowledges. If worker 2 had already charged a card before dying, the charge has now happened twice. The queue cannot know how far a crashed worker got, so consumers must be idempotent, deduplicating on the message id.

@8
Worker 2 restarts and the two workers drain messages 3 to 11. Adding workers adds throughput, but messages taken by different workers can finish in any order: the queue orders delivery, not completion.

@9-10
Message 12 is a poison message: its payload makes the handler throw every time. Worker 1 rejects it, and the broker makes it visible again with its receive count raised.

@11
After three receives, the maximum, the broker moves it to the dead-letter queue instead of retrying forever, which would tie up a worker and hide the bug. Someone inspects it and replays it by hand.

@12
This burst drained to zero. The backlog in the title is what happens when producers outpace the workers: depth climbs and keeps climbing. So watch the age of the oldest unprocessed message, not only the depth. In Kafka that is consumer lag, measured in time rather than records, because 100,000 records is nothing on one topic and an hour on another.
