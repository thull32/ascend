@0
The same three shards, cut differently. Range sharding splits the key space into contiguous ranges: shard 1 owns A to I, shard 2 owns J to Q, shard 3 owns R to Z. The router keeps that range table, and a lookup is a binary search over the boundaries. No hashing.

@1-3
Alice starts with A, so she goes to shard 1. Bob, B: shard 1. Hank, H: shard 1. Inside a shard, keys are stored in sorted order.

@4
Judy starts with J, which falls in J to Q, so she goes to shard 2.

@5
Now a range query, names between A and I. It touches exactly one shard and reads contiguous, sorted keys. That locality is the whole point of range sharding.

@6
The failure mode. Shard by a key that only ever increases, an order id or a timestamp. The ranges are now orders 1 to 1,000, 1,001 to 2,000, and 2,001 upwards. Every new key is the largest yet, so it always lands in the last range.

@7
Orders 3,001, 3,002, 3,003 all go to shard 3, which takes every write: the hot tail. Two shards sit idle while one saturates, and adding shards does not help, because they receive nothing until the tail moves.

@8
Two fixes. Split the hot range automatically and move half to another shard, as Bigtable, HBase and CockroachDB do. Or salt the key with a hash prefix, which spreads the writes but breaks cheap range scans.

@9
The trade: cheap range scans and sorted locality, but a range table to maintain and rebalance, and exposure to hot spots on skewed or sequential keys. Hash sharding is the mirror image. When you choose, the key also has to match how you query, and it must never change: shard by email, and a user who changes theirs moves every row.
