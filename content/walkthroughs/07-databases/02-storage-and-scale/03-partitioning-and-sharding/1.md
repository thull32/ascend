@0
A router and three shards. Hash sharding is one rule: hash the key and take it modulo the shard count. Any router computes the same answer, with no shared table to consult.

@1-3
Alice hashes to a number that leaves remainder 2 when divided by 3, so she goes to shard 3. Bob also gives remainder 2: shard 3. Hank, remainder 2 again: shard 3.

@4
Judy gives remainder 0, so she goes to shard 1.

@5
After four keys the shards hold 1, 0 and 3. Four keys are far too few to show the spreading, and here three of them happened to collide on one shard. What hashing does guarantee is that nearby keys land on unrelated shards, so sequential keys, like orders 3,001 and 3,002, scatter just the same. There is no hot tail.

@6
The first failure mode: a range query cannot be routed. The router must scatter it to every shard and merge the results. Cost grows with the number of shards, and tail latency is the slowest shard's.

@7
The second: add a fourth shard and the modulus changes from 3 to 4. All 4 of these keys now hash somewhere else and must be migrated while serving traffic. Consistent hashing exists to shrink that to about a quarter of the keys.

@8
The third: hashing spreads keys, not load. One celebrity key still lands on one shard. In the lesson's arithmetic, the largest of 10,000 tenants carries about 10 percent of all load, and hashed across 16 shards, the shard that gets it carries 2.5 times the average. Split a hot key with a suffix and read from all the pieces.

@9
The trade: uniform load and stateless routing, paid for with scatter-gather range queries and painful resharding. Range sharding is the mirror image, and many systems, Cassandra and DynamoDB among them, hash the partition key and keep rows sorted by range within a partition.
