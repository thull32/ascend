@0
Six request handlers, T1 to T6, and a pool of 2 database connections. A pool is a counting semaphore: acquire takes a permit, or blocks when the count is 0; release gives the permit back and wakes one waiter. Each handler needs a connection for 2 ticks.

@1
T1 acquires, and the count drops to 1. T2 acquires, and it drops to 0. T3, T4, T5 and T6 each find no permits and join the wait queue, in arrival order.

@2
T1 and T2 run their queries. The other four are waiting. This queue is where latency goes when the pool is too small or queries are too slow.

@3-4
T1 and T2 release, and the count goes back up to 2. The two longest waiters, T3 and T4, are woken and take both connections, and the count is back at 0 while they work.

@5-6
T3 and T4 release, and T5 and T6, the last in the queue, take their turn.

@7
T5 and T6 release, and both permits are free again.

@8
At most 2 handlers were ever inside at once, and the rest queued in first-in, first-out order. That is exactly what you want from a database pool. If 400 requests want the database at once, it is better for most of them to wait in a queue you control, with a timeout and a metric, than inside Postgres, where they slow each other down. The acquire timeout bounds how long anyone waits. And Little's law sizes it: connections in use equals the acquire rate times the hold time. At 1,800 acquires a second held for 1.3 milliseconds each, that is about 2.3.
