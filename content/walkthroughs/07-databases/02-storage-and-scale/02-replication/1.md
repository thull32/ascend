@0
One leader and two followers, all holding x equals 1 at log position 7. Replication here is the write-ahead log sent over a socket: the leader streams its log, and the followers replay it in order and serve reads. Every question in this picture reduces to one: which log position has each machine reached?

@1
A client writes x equals 2. All writes go to the leader, which appends the change to its log at position 8 and applies it locally.

@2
In asynchronous mode the leader acknowledges as soon as its own log is durable, before any follower has the change.

@3
Now it streams position 8 to both followers. On a good day that takes a few milliseconds; under load or across regions it can be seconds.

@4
The same client reads x, and the read is routed to follower 1, which is still at position 7, one record behind.

@5
It returns x equals 1. The client just wrote 2 and reads 1: its own write seems to have vanished. That is replication lag breaking read-your-writes, the profile page that shows the old name until you refresh.

@6
A few milliseconds later both followers apply position 8 and hold x equals 2. The system is eventually consistent: the lag window is real, but usually short.

@7
The fixes: route recent writers' reads to the leader for a while, or have the follower wait until its replayed position has reached the client's last write position before answering.

@8
Now synchronous mode. The leader waits for follower 1 to confirm each write before acknowledging. Usually only one follower is synchronous, so a slow follower cannot stall every write.

@9-10
A write of x equals 3 arrives, and the leader logs position 9. It ships the record to both followers and waits for the synchronous one.

@11
Follower 1 has position 9 durably on disk and acknowledges. Follower 2 is still at 8.

@12
Only now does the client hear OK, once the write exists on two nodes. The cost is the leader's fsync plus one round trip to follower 1.

@13
Then the leader dies. Someone, a coordinator or the nodes themselves through consensus, must pick the most up-to-date follower, promote it, and repoint the clients.

@14
Follower 1, at position 9, is promoted, and follower 2 now follows it. Because the last write was synchronous, nothing is lost. With asynchronous replication, writes the old leader acknowledged but had not shipped would be gone. And if the old leader comes back believing it still leads, two nodes accept writes: fence it first.

@15
The trade: asynchronous replication gives low write latency and tolerates slow followers, but can lose acknowledged writes. Synchronous replication guarantees a second durable copy, at the cost of latency, and of availability whenever that follower is slow. Either way, replicas scale reads. They do not scale writes.
