@0
A small social graph, and a breadth-first expansion from one user, me. A first-in, first-out queue explores people in order of distance, the number of hops. Level one is my friends; level two is friends of friends.

@1-4
Dequeue me, at distance 0. Its edges lead to Ana, Raj and Lee, each discovered at distance 1 and added to the queue.

@5-6
Dequeue Ana. Through her, Kim is discovered at distance 2.

@7-8
Dequeue Raj. Raj also knows Kim, but Kim is already discovered, so the only new person is Ivy, at distance 2. Kim is reachable twice, through Ana and through Raj, and that is exactly what a mutual-friend count measures.

@9-10
Dequeue Lee, and discover Bo at distance 2. The whole second level is in the queue: Kim, Ivy and Bo.

@11-14
Dequeue Kim, which reaches Zoe at distance 3. Dequeue Ivy, which reaches Max at distance 3.

@15-17
Bo, Zoe and Max are dequeued with nothing new to discover. The graph is exhausted.

@18
Every node was dequeued once and every edge examined once, so the cost is the number of nodes plus the number of edges, and each label is the shortest path length. That is the point for databases: a traversal costs the edges it touches. With 50 friends each, the frontier grows roughly 50-fold per hop. In the lesson's Postgres measurement on a random graph, depth 2 reached about 2,500 users in 1.9 milliseconds, depth 3 reached 71 percent of the graph in 43 milliseconds, and depth 4 reached everyone in 643. In SQL, every edge touched is a B-tree probe; a graph store follows a stored pointer instead.
