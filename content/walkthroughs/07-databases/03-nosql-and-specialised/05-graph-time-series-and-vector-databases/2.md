@0
Twelve vectors, drawn as points in two dimensions, and a query point at 7.2 and 3.1, shown as a ring. In HNSW, every vector is a node in the bottom layer, linked to its nearest neighbours. Some nodes are also promoted to sparser layers above, like the express lanes of a skip list: here 2 nodes on the top layer, 4 in the middle, and all 12 at the bottom.

@1
The search enters at the top layer's entry point, n0, which is 6.55 away from the query. The top layer is a coarse map, for long jumps across the whole space.

@2
n0's only neighbour up here is n9, at 6.91. That is no closer, so n0 is a local minimum for this layer.

@3
Descend to layer 1, at the same node. The graph is denser here, so the steps get shorter and more precise.

@4
n0's neighbours on this layer are n6 at 3.72, n9 at 6.91, and n3 at 0.63. Move greedily to the closest: n3.

@5
From n3, the neighbours are n6 and n0, both further away. A local minimum again.

@6-7
Descend to the bottom layer, still at n3. Its neighbours are n2 at 2.72, n7 at 1.84, n4 at 2.77, and n8 at 2.06. None is closer than 0.63.

@8
So the result is n3, at distance 0.63, and here it is the exact nearest neighbour. With 12 points the saving is tiny. The point is the shape: a few greedy hops per layer, so a search visits a few hundred to a few thousand nodes out of millions. Production indexes hold a hundred million vectors with recall around 95 to 99 percent. The search is approximate, which is why the bottom layer runs a beam search that keeps the best ef search candidates: raise it, and recall rises along with latency. More links per node, M, also raise recall, at the cost of memory and build time.
