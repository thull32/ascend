@0
The same three-key cache, now least frequently used. Each key carries a count of its accesses. When the cache is full, the key with the smallest count is evicted, and ties go to the least recently used. Popularity beats recency. The requests are A, A, B, C, A, D, B, E, A, D.

@1-2
A misses and is loaded with a frequency of 1. A again: a hit, and its count rises to 2. The more a key is read, the harder it is to evict.

@3-4
B misses and is loaded at 1. C misses and is loaded at 1. The cache is full.

@5
A hits a third time and its count is 3.

@6
D misses and something must go. B and C tie at the lowest count, 1, and B is the less recently used, so B is evicted. A was never in danger.

@7-8
B comes back and misses, evicting C. Then E misses and evicts D. Each newcomer enters at 1, at the bottom, and pushes out another key at 1.

@9-10
A hits a fourth time, and D returns, evicting B. Three hits in ten requests, every one of them on A. One-off keys churn through the bottom while the popular key stays put: exactly what an LRU cannot promise.

@11
The failure mode is no decay. A key that was hot last week keeps its high count and is never evicted, while every new key enters at the bottom and is the first to go. A shifting working set churns at the bottom and never displaces the stale top. So real frequency counters age.

@12
Redis does exactly that. Its counter is logarithmic: a new key starts at 5, so it is not evicted the instant it arrives, about 100 accesses reach 10, and about 1,000 reach 18. Every minute of idleness takes one off. So a nightly report that reads every product key once moves a cold key from 5 to 6, and the hot keys at 18 or more survive. Under LRU, the report's keys would be the most recent, and the hot keys would go.
