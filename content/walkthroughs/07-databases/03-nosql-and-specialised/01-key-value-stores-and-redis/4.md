@0
A circle of positions from 0 to 359 degrees, with three nodes spaced evenly around it: N1 at 0 degrees, N2 at 120, and N3 at 240. Consistent hashing puts nodes and keys on the same circle, and the rule is one sentence: a key belongs to the first node at or after it, walking clockwise.

@1-2
User 1 hashes to 159 degrees, past N2, so the walk continues to N3 at 240. User 7, at 124, has also just passed N2, so it walks on to N3 too.

@3-4
Video 9 lands at 35 degrees and walks to N2, at 120. Session 3, at 73, walks to N2 as well.

@5-6
Cart 5 lands at 322, past N3, the last node before the top. It wraps past zero and reaches N1. Feed 2, at 175, walks to N3.

@7
All six keys are placed: one on N1, two on N2, three on N3. Each node owns the arc that ends at its own position.

@8
Now add N4. It joins at 167 degrees, between N2 and N3, and claims only that arc. User 1 and user 7 sit on it, so they move from N3 to N4: two keys of six. No key moves between the old nodes. With hash modulo the node count, going from 3 nodes to 4 would have remapped 5 of these 6 keys.

@9
The ideal is that a newcomer takes its fair share, about a quarter of the keys, and nothing else moves. With one point per node, how much it takes depends on where it lands, so real rings put 100 to 200 virtual nodes per physical node: a newcomer takes a little from everyone, and a failed node's share spreads across many neighbours. Redis Cluster takes a different route to the same effect: 16,384 fixed hash slots, each owned by one primary. Every key has one owner, and moving a node moves only its slots, one key at a time, with clients redirected by ASK while a slot migrates and by MOVED once it has.
