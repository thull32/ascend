@0
A circle with three nodes on it: N2 at 161 degrees, N3 at 342, and N1 at 344. Consistent hashing puts nodes and keys on the same circle, and the rule is one sentence: a key belongs to the first node clockwise from it.

@1-2
User 1 hashes to 187 degrees. Walking clockwise, the first node is N3, at 342. User 7, at 181 degrees, also walks to N3.

@3
Video 9 hashes to 345 degrees, just past both N3 and N1. Walking clockwise, it wraps past zero and reaches N2, at 161.

@4-6
Session 3 at 164 degrees, cart 5 at 294, and feed 2 at 165 all walk clockwise to N3. With only three points on the circle the arcs are wildly uneven: N3 owns five of the six keys, and N1, two degrees past N3, owns none.

@7
Now add N4 at 159 degrees. Only the keys between its predecessor and the new node change owner. Its predecessor is N1, at 344, and the one key in that arc is video 9, which moves from N2 to N4. One key of six moves. With hash modulo the node count, nearly every key would move.

@8
Real systems put 100 to 200 virtual nodes per physical node on the ring, so load evens out and a failed node's share spreads across many neighbours. Redis Cluster takes a different route to the same effect: 16,384 fixed hash slots, each owned by one primary. Every key has one owner, and moving a node moves only its slots, one key at a time, with clients redirected by ASK while a slot migrates and by MOVED once it has.
