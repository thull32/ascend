@0
A replica set: one primary and two secondaries, all holding x equals 1 at log position 7. The primary applies each write and appends it to its log, the oplog, and the secondaries pull that log and apply it in order. They serve reads and stand by for failover.

@1-2
A client writes x equals 2. The primary applies it at position 8 and acknowledges straight away, before any secondary has it. That is write concern w 1: acknowledged once the primary has applied it.

@3
The secondaries fetch position 8 from the primary's oplog. Usually milliseconds, sometimes seconds under load or across regions.

@4-5
The client reads x from secondary 1, which has not applied position 8 yet, and gets x equals 1. Its own write seems to have vanished. Reading from secondaries means reading data that lags by an unbounded amount.

@6
A moment later both secondaries apply position 8 and catch up.

@7
The fixes: send a recent writer's reads to the primary, or have the secondary wait until it has reached the client's last write. In MongoDB, causally consistent sessions do this per client.

@8-10
Now the primary waits for secondary 1 to confirm before acknowledging. A write of x equals 3 is logged at position 9 and shipped, and the primary waits.

@11-12
Secondary 1 has position 9 durably, and only then does the client hear OK. The write exists on two of three nodes, a majority. That is write concern majority, the default since MongoDB 5.0, and it costs one replication round trip.

@13
The primary dies. An election picks a new one; MongoDB's election timeout is 10 seconds, and a new primary is expected within about 12.

@14
Secondary 1 is promoted, and secondary 2 follows it. Because position 9 reached a majority, the new primary has it. Had the old primary acknowledged position 9 with w 1 and then lost contact, a secondary would have been elected without it. When the old primary rejoined, it would roll back that write, saving it to a rollback file. The client was told it succeeded; the replica set no longer has it.

@15
So write concern is a choice of what survives. w 1 is fast, but does not survive a failover to a secondary that lacks the write. Majority survives any single failover, for one round trip. Reads scale out across secondaries; writes do not.
