@0
A replica set: one leader, which MongoDB calls the primary, and two followers, the secondaries. All three hold x equals 1 at log position 7. The primary applies each write and appends it to its log, the oplog, and the secondaries pull and apply it in order.

@1-2
A client writes x equals 2. The leader applies it at position 8, and acknowledges straight away, before any follower has it. That is write concern w 1: acknowledged once the primary has applied it.

@3
The leader streams position 8 to both followers. Usually milliseconds, sometimes seconds under load or across regions.

@4-5
The client reads x from follower 1, which has not applied position 8 yet, and gets x equals 1. Its own write seems to have vanished. Reading from secondaries means reading data that lags by an unbounded amount.

@6
A moment later both followers apply position 8 and catch up.

@7
The fixes: send a recent writer's reads to the leader, or have the follower wait until it has reached the client's last write. In MongoDB, causally consistent sessions do this per client.

@8-10
Now the leader waits for follower 1 to confirm before acknowledging. A write of x equals 3 is logged at position 9 and shipped, and the leader waits.

@11-12
Follower 1 has position 9 durably, and only then does the client hear OK. The write exists on two of three nodes, a majority. That is write concern majority, the default since MongoDB 5.0, and it costs one replication round trip.

@13
The leader dies. An election picks a new one; MongoDB's election timeout is 10 seconds, and a new primary is expected within about 12.

@14
Follower 1 is promoted, and follower 2 follows it. Because position 9 reached a majority, the new leader has it. Had the leader acknowledged position 9 with w 1 and then lost contact, a follower would have been elected without it. When the old primary rejoined, it would roll back that write, saving it to a rollback file. The client was told it succeeded; the replica set no longer has it.

@15
So write concern is a choice of what survives. w 1 is fast, but does not survive a failover to a secondary that lacks the write. Majority survives any single failover, for one round trip. Reads scale out across secondaries; writes do not.
