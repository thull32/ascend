@0
A cache that holds 3 keys, in front of a database. Most caches start from least recently used eviction: every access moves a key to the front, and when the cache is full, the key at the back, untouched for longest, is evicted. The requests are A, B, C, A, D, B, E, A.

@1-3
A, B and C each miss and are loaded from the database. The cache is full, with C at the front and A at the back.

@4
A is requested again: a hit. It moves from the back to the front. Reads update recency, not just writes.

@5
D misses. The cache is full, so B, the key at the back, last used at step 2, is evicted, and D goes in at the front.

@6-8
Now the working set is bigger than the cache. B returns and misses, evicting C. E misses and evicts A. And A, requested once more, misses and evicts D. Every one of these misses is a database read and an eviction: one hit in eight requests.

@9
The other failure mode is a one-off scan. A report or a crawler reading a handful of keys once each pushes every genuinely hot key out, and the next real requests all miss. Some variants admit a key to the main cache only on its second touch.

@10
LRU is constant time and adapts instantly to a shifting working set, but it tracks recency only, so it cannot tell a key read a thousand times from one read once a moment ago. Size the cache from the working set. A working set larger than the cache turns hits into a steady stream of evictions, exactly what the last few steps showed, and it appears as a falling hit ratio and rising database load. So watch hit ratio and eviction rate, and bound the number of distinct keys.
