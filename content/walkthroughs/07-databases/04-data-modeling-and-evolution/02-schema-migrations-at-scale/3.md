@0
A load balancer, nine stable instances, one canary, and a metrics panel that compares them. A canary release sends a small slice of real traffic to the new version, compares its metrics against the stable version serving the rest, and only then widens the slice. The gate here: no more errors than stable, and a 99th percentile latency no worse than 1.2 times stable's. For a schema migration, the new version is the read new flag: read from the new columns instead of the old one.

@1
Version 2 is deployed to the canary instance. Health checks pass, but it gets no traffic yet.

@2
Stage one: 10 percent of traffic goes to the canary, 1 of the next 10 requests, and 9 go to stable. If version 2 is broken, at most one user in ten sees it, and only briefly.

@3
The analysis compares both versions over the same window. The canary has no errors, like stable, and its 99th percentile of 120 milliseconds is within 1.2 times stable's 118. The gate passes and the share widens.

@4
Stage two: 50 percent, 5 requests each. Now the canary sees enough traffic to hit a code path that 10 percent never exercised: 2 of its 5 requests fail, and its 99th percentile jumps to 410 milliseconds.

@5
The gate fails: 2 errors in 5 canary requests against none in stable's 5, over the same window. Because stable is serving the other half cleanly at the same moment, the comparison isolates the release from ambient noise.

@6
Automatic rollback: the canary's weight goes to zero, and the load balancer sends everything to stable. The bad release reached only the canary's share of traffic for one window, not everyone until a human noticed.

@7-8
Version 2.1 starts again at 10 percent, then 50, and passes both times: no errors on either side, and 121 milliseconds against 118 at 50 percent. Every promotion repeats the gate. And because it compares like with like, a traffic spike that slows both versions does not fail the canary.

@9
Promotion: all ten instances run version 2.1, which becomes the stable version.

@10
The pitfalls. With only a handful of canary requests in a window there may be too few samples to see a 1 percent error rate, so the gate needs a minimum count, not just a rate. A canary that only gets easy traffic passes and then fails at 100 percent. And stateful sessions need sticky routing.

@11
The trade: a small blast radius, and problems only real traffic reveals, caught early, at the cost of slower rollouts and two versions live for hours, so the schema must stay compatible with both. For data, flags make the read switch instant and reversible. Each one is temporary, with an owner and a removal date.
