@0
Q6 is the comments on one lesson, newest first, and it is served by one B-tree, on target kind, target slug and created at. The picture uses small numbers for keys, but the mechanics are the same. A root page with separators 20 and 40, and three leaves: 5, 10 and 15; 20, 25 and 30; 40, 45 and 50. Every leaf sits at the same depth.

@1-2
A lookup for 45 reads the root, sees that 45 is at least 40, and follows the pointer to leaf 3, where it finds 45. Two page reads, and the root is always cached, so one disk read.

@3
This is the step that matters for Q6. A range scan from 25 to 45 descends once, to leaf 2, then walks sideways along the sibling pointer to leaf 3. For Q6, the two equalities, kind and slug, pick out one contiguous run of leaf entries, already sorted by created at. So the query needs no sort, and a limit can stop the walk early. Postgres scans it backwards for newest first.

@4
Every index is paid for on every write. Insert 42: it routes to leaf 3, which now holds four keys, its capacity.

@5-6
Insert 47: leaf 3 overflows and splits. 47 and 50 move to a new leaf 4, and 47 is promoted into the root as a separator.

@7
Insert 12 lands in leaf 1, which has room.

@8
The failure modes: a split is a multi-page write that relies on the log, random inserts leave pages around 70 percent full, and a hot rightmost leaf serialises inserts.

@9
That is the lesson's rule in one picture. Every hot query names its index, and every index names the query that pays for its writes. Q6 needs nothing more than one backward index scan. The query worth denormalising is the one no index can bound, like Q4, which aggregates over every user.
