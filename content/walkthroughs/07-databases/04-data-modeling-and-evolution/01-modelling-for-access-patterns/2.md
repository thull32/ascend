@0
Every denormalised copy needs a named mechanism that keeps it in sync. Inside one database, that is the same transaction. Once the copy lives where the transaction cannot reach, a Redis leaderboard, a cache, a warehouse, it is change data capture. On screen: the app, Postgres, a connector, and the read model on the right. The app writes only to Postgres. The connector tails the write-ahead log and turns every committed change into an event, in commit order.

@1
Setup: the connector creates a replication slot, so Postgres keeps the log from here on, and streams a consistent snapshot of the existing rows. After that, it needs only the log.

@2-3
The app inserts user 1, Ada, and commits at log position 1. The app knows nothing about the read model. The connector decodes the change, with the row's before and after images, a consumer applies it to the summary store, and the connector confirms position 1 to the slot. The gap between commit and apply is the staleness a reader can see.

@4-7
An update renames Ada to Ada L., at position 2, and an order is inserted for user 1, at position 3. Each is decoded, applied in the same order the database committed it, and confirmed.

@8-9
User 1 is deleted at position 4, and the consumer removes that row's contribution from the read model. Notice the connector: it has emitted position 4 but not yet confirmed it, so the slot still says 3.

@10
Now it crashes. On restart it resumes after the last confirmed position, 3, sends position 4 again, and this time confirms it. The read model receives the delete twice. Delivery is at least once, so applying an event must be idempotent: upsert by key, or skip anything at or below the last applied position.

@11
An abandoned slot pins the log. Down for 3 days, and the primary's disk is 95 percent full. Monitor slot lag.

@12
Why not write the copy from the request handler? A crash between the two writes loses an update, and two racing writers can apply their updates to the copy in the opposite order. One log makes the database the single arbiter of order.

@13
The rule from the lesson: one transaction for copies inside the database, change data capture for copies outside it, and triggers only with a written reason. The commit-to-apply lag, tens of milliseconds to seconds, is the copy's staleness window, so monitor it. And every read model must be droppable and rebuildable from the write model, or its first inconsistency becomes permanent.
