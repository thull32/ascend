@0
A B-tree index is a balanced tree of fixed-size pages. The root page holds two separator keys, 20 and 40, that route a lookup. Below it sit three leaves holding the keys in sorted order: 5, 10 and 15; then 20, 25 and 30; then 40, 45 and 50. Every leaf is at the same depth, and that is the property every operation here protects.

@1
Look up 45. Read the root and binary-search its separators: 45 is at least 20 and at least 40, so follow the pointer to child 3. One page read.

@2
Read leaf 3 and binary-search it: 45 is there. Two page reads for the whole lookup, and since the root is always cached, only one of them touches the disk. With a fan-out of around 300 keys per page, a tree four levels deep indexes billions of rows.

@3
Now a range scan, 25 to 45. Descend once, to leaf 2, then follow its sibling pointer sideways to leaf 3. The leaves are linked, so sorted queries and range conditions read in sequence without going back to the root.

@4
Now the inserts, which are ascending, like a sequence or a time-ordered key. Insert 52: it routes to leaf 3, which now holds 4 keys, its capacity. The key slides into place and nothing else changes. Like any page change, this write goes through the write-ahead log.

@5
Insert 55: also routed to leaf 3, which would now hold 5 keys, one over capacity. The page must split.

@6
Half the keys move to a new leaf: leaf 3 keeps 40, 45 and 50, and leaf 4 takes 52 and 55. Leaf 4's smallest key, 52, is promoted into the root as a third separator. If the root itself overflowed, it would split too and the tree would grow one level, from the top. That is why all the leaves stay at equal depth.

@7-8
Insert 58, then 61. Both route past the new separator to leaf 4, which fills to 4 keys. Ascending keys always land in the rightmost leaf. Random keys, like version 4 UUIDs, would touch a random leaf on every insert.

@9
Three failure modes. A split writes several pages, so a crash in the middle needs the log to keep the tree consistent. Random inserts leave pages around 70 percent full. And a hot rightmost leaf, fed by an auto-increment key, serialises every insert on one page latch.

@10
The trade: logarithmic reads with very few page fetches, in-order scans, and updates in place, paid for with write amplification, a whole page per change, and random writes for random keys. Log-structured merge trees make the opposite bet: sequential writes, and more work on every read.
