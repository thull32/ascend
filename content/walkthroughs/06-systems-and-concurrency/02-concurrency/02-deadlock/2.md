@0
When you cannot control the order locks are taken in, as in a database running arbitrary transactions, you let deadlocks happen and detect them. This is a wait-for graph: a node per thread, and an edge from T to U when T is blocked on something U holds. T4 waits for T1, T1 waits for T2, T2 waits for T3, and T3 waits for T2. A deadlock exists exactly when this graph has a cycle. The detector finds one with a depth-first search that remembers which nodes are on its current path.

@1
The search enters T1, which goes on the path.

@2
It follows T1's edge to T2, and T2 joins the path.

@3
It follows T2's edge to T3, and T3 joins the path.

@4
T3's edge points back to T2, which is still on the path. That back edge is a cycle, and the cycle is the deadlock: T2 and T3 are waiting for each other. Notice what is not in it. T1 and T4 are stuck, but they are only blocked behind the cycle. Abort T1 and T4 proceeds, but T2 and T3 stay stuck. A detector has to pick a victim on the cycle itself, which is what Postgres does when a lock wait passes its deadlock timeout of one second.
