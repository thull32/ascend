@0
A producer, a buffer that holds 8 messages, and a consumer that can handle 2 messages a tick. Backpressure is the consumer's ability to slow the producer down. Without it, the two are separated only by the buffer, and every buffer is finite.

@1
The producer pushes a burst of 20 messages at once. The buffer keeps 8. The other 12 have nowhere to go, so they are dropped. If the buffer were unbounded instead, the process would grow until it was killed for running out of memory.

@2
The consumer drains 2, its real capacity, and the buffer is down to 6. The 12 dropped messages are gone, and nobody upstream was told. The producer believes it delivered 20.

@3
The fix is to flip the direction of control. The consumer tells the producer how many messages it may send, as credits, a window, or a request for n, and the producer may never exceed that number. Sending becomes pull-driven. This is how Reactive Streams, the TCP window and gRPC flow control work.

@4
The consumer looks at its free space, 2 slots, and grants exactly 2 credits.

@5
The producer sends 2 and stops. With zero credits left it must wait, so the buffer cannot overflow, by construction.

@6
Each tick the consumer processes 2 and hands back 2 credits. In steady state the producer runs at exactly the consumer's rate, and the buffer only absorbs jitter.

@7
And the pressure propagates upstream. A blocked producer stops reading from its own source, whose handlers slow down, whose clients see latency or 429 responses. That is the point: the slowest stage sets the pace of the whole pipeline, and overload shows up at the edge instead of as silent data loss in the middle.

@8
Backpressure has its own failure mode. A user who gets a hung request is not always acceptable. For metrics or telemetry, dropping the oldest, the newest, or a sample is the right policy; for orders it is not. And beware cycles: a producer blocked on a consumer that is blocked on the producer is a deadlock.

@9
The trade-off: backpressure guarantees bounded memory and honest overload signals, at the cost of tying the producer's speed to the consumer's. For each kind of data, choose between slowing the source, buffering to durable storage such as a log, and shedding. Remember the lesson's arithmetic too: a buffer absorbs bursts, never a consumer that is slower on average.
