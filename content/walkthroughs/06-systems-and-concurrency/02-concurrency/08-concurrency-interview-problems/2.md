@0
A read-write lock. Any number of readers may hold it together, but a writer needs it alone. The invariant: if a writer is inside, the reader count is zero. Two readers arrive first, then a writer, then two more readers.

@1-2
R1 takes the read lock, and the reader count is 1. R2 takes it too, and the count is 2. Readers never exclude each other.

@3
W1 asks for the write lock, but there are 2 readers inside, so it blocks and registers as a waiting writer.

@4
R3 and R4 arrive wanting to read. Readers are active, so they could slip in. But this lock prefers writers: because a writer is waiting, new readers queue behind it. Otherwise a steady stream of readers could starve the writer forever.

@5-6
R1 releases, leaving 1 reader. W1 still waits for the last one. R2 releases, the count reaches 0, and the lock goes to W1.

@7
W1 holds the lock exclusively and writes. No reader can see a half-written state.

@8
W1 releases and broadcasts. Both waiting readers wake at once, and R3 and R4 read in parallel.

@9
R3 and R4 finish, and the count is back to 0.

@10
Reads share, writes exclude. Choose the preference deliberately. Reader preference starves writers under constant read load; writer preference, shown here, makes reads bursty. Read-copy-update and sequence locks go further and never block readers at all.
