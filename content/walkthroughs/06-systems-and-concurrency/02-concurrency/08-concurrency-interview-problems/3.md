@0
Five philosophers, P0 to P4, sit around a table with five forks, F0 to F4, one between each pair. To eat, a philosopher needs both neighbouring forks: P0 needs F0 on the left and F1 on the right, and so on round the table, until P4, who needs F4 and F0. The naive rule is: pick up your left fork, then your right.

@1
Everyone picks up their left fork at the same moment. P0 holds F0, P1 holds F1, and so on to P4, who holds F4.

@2
And nobody can move. P0 wants F1, which P1 holds. P1 wants F2, which P2 holds. Around the table it goes, until P4 wants F0, which P0 holds. The wait-for graph is a cycle through all five, and every philosopher is waiting for a fork that only a waiting philosopher can release.

@3
Every philosopher holds one fork and waits for the neighbour's: circular wait. Real programs hit exactly this with two mutexes taken in opposite orders.

@4
The fix is Dijkstra's resource hierarchy: number the forks, and always pick up the lower-numbered one first. For four philosophers nothing changes, because their left fork is already the lower one. Only P4 changes: its forks are F4 and F0, so it now reaches for F0 first. That one change is enough to break the cycle.

@5
Replay. P0 to P3 each take their first fork. P4 reaches for F0, finds P0 holding it, and blocks while holding nothing.

@6-8
So F4 is free, and P3 takes it. P3 holds F3 and F4, eats, and puts both down. P0, P1 and P2 are still waiting on their right-hand neighbours.

@9-11
F3 is free now, so P2 takes it, eats with F2 and F3, and puts them down.

@12-14
That frees F2 for P1, who eats and puts down F1 and F2.

@15-17
P0 finally takes F1, eats, and puts down F0 and F1. P4, which has been waiting for F0 the whole time, takes it.

@18-20
P4 takes F4, eats, and puts both forks down.

@21
Everyone ate, in 16 ticks of the replay. The waiting unwound like a chain, because the one philosopher who would have closed the loop was waiting while holding nothing. Two alternatives: a waiter, a semaphore with n minus one seats, so at most four philosophers try at once; or try-lock with backoff. All of them work by breaking one of the four Coffman conditions.
