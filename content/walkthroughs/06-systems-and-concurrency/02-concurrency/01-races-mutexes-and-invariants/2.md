@0
The same counter plus plus, now with three threads, and each increment wrapped in lock, then load, add, store, then unlock. Only the thread holding the mutex may touch the counter, so to every other thread the three instructions look like a single indivisible step.

@1
T1 acquires the mutex. T2 and T3 also call lock, find it taken, and block. The kernel parks them until the mutex is free.

@2-4
T1 loads 0, adds 1, and stores 1. Nobody else can load a stale value in the middle, because nobody else can get past lock.

@5-6
T1 unlocks, which wakes T2. T2 acquires the mutex, and T3 keeps waiting.

@7-10
T2 loads 1, adds 1, stores 2, and unlocks, waking T3. This is the difference from the race: T2's load happens after T1's store, never before it.

@11-15
T3 acquires the mutex, loads 2, adds 1, stores 3, and releases it.

@16
The counter is 3, and every increment survived, because load, add and store never interleaved. The price is serialisation: the three threads spent nine ticks in single file, with no parallelism at all inside the lock. That is why the work done while holding a lock should be as short as possible. A mutex also orders memory: everything the previous holder wrote before unlock is visible to the next holder after lock.
