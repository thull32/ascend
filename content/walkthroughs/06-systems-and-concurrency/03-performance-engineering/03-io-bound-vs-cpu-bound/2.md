@0
A producer, a buffer that holds 8 messages, and a consumer that can handle 2 messages a tick: the bottleneck. When producers outpace the bottleneck, the excess has to go somewhere, and every buffer is finite.

@1
The producer pushes a burst of 20 messages. The buffer keeps 8, and the other 12 have nowhere to go, so they are dropped. With an unbounded buffer instead, memory would grow, every item's wait would grow with it, and the process would eventually be killed for running out of memory, after minutes spent serving requests whose clients had already given up.

@2
The consumer drains 2, its real capacity, leaving 6. The 12 dropped messages are gone, and nobody upstream was told. The producer believes it delivered all 20.

@3
The fix is to flip the direction of control. The consumer tells the producer how many messages it may send, as credits or a window, and the producer may not exceed that. Sending becomes pull-driven, the way the TCP window and gRPC flow control work.

@4
The consumer has 2 free slots, so it grants exactly 2 credits.

@5
The producer sends 2, and with zero credits left, it waits. The buffer cannot overflow, by construction.

@6
Each tick the consumer processes 2 and hands back 2 credits. In steady state the producer runs at exactly the consumer's rate, and the buffer only absorbs jitter.

@7
The pressure travels upstream. A blocked producer stops reading from its own source, whose handlers slow down, whose clients see latency or 429 responses. That is the point: overload becomes visible at the edge, as a signal, instead of as silent data loss in the middle.

@8
But a hung request is not always acceptable. For metrics or telemetry, dropping the oldest, the newest or a sample is the right policy; for orders it is not. And watch for cycles: a producer blocked on a consumer that is blocked on the producer is a deadlock.

@9
So a bounded queue forces a decision at the point of overload: block the producer, reject fast, or drop low-value work, chosen per kind of data. Pair it with deadlines at every hop, so a request that has already blown its budget stops holding a connection.
