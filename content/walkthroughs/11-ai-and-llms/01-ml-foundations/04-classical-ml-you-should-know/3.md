@0
Nine points with no labels, in three visible groups: lower left, upper right and upper left. We ask k-means for 3 clusters. The three centroids, the crosses, start on chosen data points: C1 at 1 and 1, C2 at 8 and 8, C3 at 1 and 8. Real code uses k-means plus plus to spread them out. Then one loop, repeated: assign every point to its nearest centroid, then move every centroid to the mean of its points.

@1
Iteration 1, assign. The point at 1 and 1 is 0 from C1, 9.9 from C2 and 7 from C3, so it joins cluster 1. Each point joins the centroid in its own group, and all 9 count as changed, since none had a cluster before.

@2
Iteration 1, update. Each centroid moves to the middle of its points. C1 moves to 1.5 and 1.4, C2 to 8.5 and 8.4, and C3, the one that moves furthest, from 1 and 8 to 1.5 and about 8.47, a shift of 0.68.

@3
Iteration 2, assign again, with the moved centroids. The point at 1 and 1 is now 0.64 from C1, and much further from the other two, so it stays in cluster 1. In fact no point changes cluster at all.

@4
And that is the stopping rule. If no assignment changed, the means cannot change, so the centroids will not move. The algorithm has converged.

@5
The inertia, the total squared distance from each point to its centroid, is 3.13. Each iteration costs one pass over every point for every centroid, and the inertia never increases, so k-means always converges. But it converges to a local optimum that depends on where the centroids started. So run several starts and keep the lowest inertia, choose k with the elbow of the inertia curve or a silhouette score, and remember the trap from the lesson: one far outlier can steal a whole cluster.
