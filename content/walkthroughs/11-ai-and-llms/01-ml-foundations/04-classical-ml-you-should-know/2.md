@0
The same eight points, and a new query, the red ring at 4 and 3.5, sitting right between the two clusters. k nearest neighbours has no training step at all. It stores the data, and at query time the 3 closest points vote.

@1
So compute the distance from the query to all eight points, which costs a full scan for every query. The nearest is the class 0 point at 3 and 2.5, at a distance of 1.41. Next is the class 1 point at 5 and 5, at 1.80. And then look at the list: the third and fourth distances are both 2.06. That is a tie for third place.

@2
The three nearest are kept: the class 0 point at 1.41, the class 1 point at 1.80, and the class 0 point at 2 and 3, at 2.06. The other point at 2.06, the class 1 point at 6 and 4, is left out only because it appears later in the input.

@3
The vote: two for class 0, one for class 1, so the prediction is class 0. But that answer hangs on the tie. List the points in another order, keep the class 1 point instead, and the prediction flips. Weighting votes by one over the distance does not rescue it either. Production code breaks ties with a stable key, such as an ID, or includes every point tied at the third distance.

@4
Two more habits. Use an odd k to avoid tied votes between two classes, and scale the features first, because a feature that ranges up to a thousand would swamp one that ranges up to one. And brute force scans everything on every query, so at scale you use an index: a KD-tree at low dimension, or an approximate index such as HNSW at high dimension.
