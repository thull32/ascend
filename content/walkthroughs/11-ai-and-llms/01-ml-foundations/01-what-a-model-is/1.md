@0
Four past batch jobs sit on the plot: 1 million rows took 3 minutes, 2 million took 5, 3 million took 8, and 4 million took 9. We want the straight line, a slope w times x plus an intercept b, that makes the mean squared error as small as possible. For a line and this loss, calculus hands us the answer directly, with no searching at all. That is ordinary least squares.

@1
First, centre the data. The mean of x is 2.5 and the mean of y is 6.25, drawn as two dashed lines. Remember this crossing point, because the least-squares line always passes through it.

@2
Now two sums over the deviations from those means. The co-movement, each x deviation times its y deviation, added up, is 10.5: it measures how x and y move together. The spread, each x deviation squared, added up, is 5: it measures how much x varies at all.

@3
The slope is co-movement over spread: 10.5 divided by 5, which is 2.1 minutes per million rows. The intercept follows from the crossing point: 6.25 minus 2.1 times 2.5, which is 1. The fitted line is 2.1 x plus 1.

@4
The red dashes are the residuals, the gaps between each real runtime and the line. At 1 million rows the line says 3.1 against a real 3, a residual of minus 0.1. The largest gap is at 3 million rows, where the line says 7.3 against a real 8. The mean squared error comes to 0.175, and 97 percent of the variance is explained. No other line does better on these four points.

@5
Extend the line to 5 million rows and it predicts 11.5 minutes. Here the closed form is a single pass over the data. With many features the same idea becomes the normal equations, and once the model is not linear in its parameters, as with a neural network, there is no closed form at all. That is where gradient descent takes over. And remember the trap: the line is only the best fit for these four points, and a prediction far beyond them is a guess.
