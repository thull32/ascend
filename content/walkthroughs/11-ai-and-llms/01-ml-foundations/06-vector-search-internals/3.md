@0
The same graph of 12 vectors, with 2 nodes on layer 2, 4 on layer 1 and all 12 on layer 0. This time the query is at 4.1 and 2.6, in the lower middle. Watch how the walk goes wrong: a greedy search can never take a step that looks worse, even when that step leads to the answer.

@1
Enter at n0, the top layer's entry point, at a distance of 3.49.

@2
On layer 2, n0's only neighbour, n9, is 5.11 away: further. Nothing better here.

@3
Descend to layer 1, still at n0.

@4
n0's layer 1 neighbours are n6 at 2.40, n9 at 5.11 and n3 at 2.90. The closest is n6, so the search moves there.

@5
From n6, the neighbours are n0 at 3.49, n3 at 2.90 and n9 at 5.11. None is closer than 2.40.

@6
Descend to layer 0 at n6, the densest layer.

@7
Here is the trap. n6's layer 0 neighbours are n5 at 2.52, n7 at 2.69, n10 at 5.40 and n9 at 5.11. Every one is further than 2.40, so greedy search stops at n6. But the true nearest neighbour is n1, at 1.25, and the only path to it runs through n5, which is slightly worse than where we stand. A greedy walk cannot step uphill.

@8
The result is n6 at 2.40, after 11 neighbour distance checks, and it is wrong: the exact nearest is n1 at 1.25. This is why real HNSW search on layer 0 is a beam search. With a beam of 2, called ef, n5 enters the result set although it is worse than n6, expanding it reaches n1, and the search finds the right answer. Raising ef is the query-time dial: more recall for proportionally more work.
