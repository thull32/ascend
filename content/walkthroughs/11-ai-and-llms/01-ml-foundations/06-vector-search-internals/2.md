@0
Twelve vectors, n0 to n11, in a graph where each node links to its nearest neighbours. Some nodes are also promoted to higher, sparser layers: all 12 are on layer 0, 4 are on layer 1, and just 2, n0 and n9, are on layer 2. In a real index promotion is random, and each layer holds about one in M of the layer below; here a fixed rule picks them. The query, the ring, is at 7.2 and 3.1. Think of a skip list spread over a plane: long jumps at the top, short precise steps at the bottom.

@1
The search enters at the top layer's entry point, n0, down in the lower left, at a distance of 6.55 from the query.

@2
On layer 2, n0's only neighbour is n9, at 6.91. That is further away, so there is nothing better on this layer. A local minimum.

@3
Descend to layer 1, staying at n0. This layer is denser, with up to 2 links per node, so the steps can be more precise.

@4
On layer 1, n0's neighbours are n6 at 3.72, n9 at 6.91, and n3 at 0.63. Greedy search moves to the closest, n3. This is the express lane at work: one long jump across most of the space.

@5
From n3, the layer 1 neighbours are n6 at 3.72 and n0 at 6.55. Neither is closer than 0.63, so this is the best this layer can do.

@6
Descend to layer 0 at n3. Every node lives here, with up to 3 links each, the densest and most precise layer.

@7
n3's layer 0 neighbours are n2 at 2.72, n7 at 1.84, n4 at 2.77 and n8 at 2.06. None beats 0.63, so the search stops.

@8
The result is n3, at 0.63, and it is the exact nearest neighbour, found after 10 distance computations instead of 12 for brute force. At 12 nodes the graph barely pays, but the hops grow roughly with the logarithm of the number of vectors while a scan grows with the number itself. Production indexes tune two dials: M, the links per node, and ef, the width of the search beam.
