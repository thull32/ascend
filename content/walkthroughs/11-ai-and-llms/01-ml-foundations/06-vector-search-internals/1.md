@0
Six stored vectors, one per word, each with four numbers labelled royal, male, female and food so you can read them. Real embeddings have hundreds of unnamed dimensions, but the search problem is the same. Hold onto one idea: exact search is one row of a similarity table. Take the query, compare it with every stored vector, and keep the best few.

@1
The vectors are not all the same length: king and queen 1.21, banana 0.86. Cosine similarity divides the dot product by both lengths, so only direction counts. In a real index every vector is normalised once, when it is stored, and cosine becomes a plain dot product.

@2-7
Now the table fills row by row, and each row is what one query costs. Treat king as the query: compare it with all six vectors, and the best match is man, at 0.81, with queen at 0.67. Queen's row finds woman at 0.81. Man's row finds king, woman's finds queen, and the apple and banana rows find each other at 1. Six rows, six comparisons each. With 10 million stored vectors, one row is 10 million dot products of hundreds of numbers each, and the scan is limited by how fast memory can stream the vectors, not by arithmetic.

@8
That is the whole of exact search: embed the query, compare it with every stored vector, return the top k. It has perfect recall, and below a few hundred thousand vectors it is often the right answer. It is also the ground truth that every approximate index is graded against. Every index in this lesson, IVF, HNSW and product quantisation, is a way to skip most of the row.
