@0
Eight points in two clusters, centred on the origin: four of class 0 in the lower left, four of class 1 in the upper right. The model is logistic regression: a weighted sum of the two coordinates plus a bias, squashed by a sigmoid into a probability of class 1. The boundary is wherever that probability is exactly one half. With every weight at zero, every point gets one half, the cross-entropy loss is 0.69, and there is no boundary yet. Gradient descent runs with a learning rate of 0.5, and after the first step you see every fourth step.

@1
Step 1. Every class 1 point is under-predicted and every class 0 point over-predicted, so the gradient pushes both weights up, to 0.5 and 0.36, while the bias stays at zero. The boundary appears as a line between the clusters, and all eight points are already on the right side of it. The loss drops from 0.69 to 0.214. The classifier is finished, as far as accuracy can tell. Hold onto that, and watch the length of the weight vector: 0.62.

@2
Steps 2 to 4. Accuracy is still 8 out of 8, and the boundary has barely moved. Yet the weights have grown to 0.87 and 0.62, a length of 1.06, and the loss has fallen to 0.092. Nothing about the classification changed. What changed is confidence: points far from the line now get probabilities closer to 0 and 1.

@3-11
The same thing, every four steps, all the way to step 40. Accuracy stays 8 out of 8. The boundary stays where it is. The weight vector grows steadily, past 1.3, 1.5, 1.8, 2.0, to 2.09 at step 40, and the loss creeps down, 0.056, 0.041, 0.027, until it reaches 0.015. Look at the loss curve: still falling, more slowly each time, never flat.

@12
From step 1 to step 40 the boundary's direction turned by less than one degree, yet the weights more than tripled in length, and the loss fell from 0.214 to 0.015. On separable data, once every point is classified correctly, the only way left to lower the loss is to scale the weights up, making every prediction more confident, and gradient descent never stops doing that on its own: the loss reaches zero only at infinity. That unbounded growth is what L2 regularisation exists to stop, by charging for the size of the weights. It is also a warning about the probabilities an unregularised model reports: very confident is not the same as well calibrated.
