@0
Reinforcement learning from human feedback starts from a supervised fine-tuned model. It can follow instructions, but it has no sense of which of two acceptable answers people prefer. The insight behind the whole pipeline: people are far better at comparing two answers than at writing the ideal one.

@1
Sample two answers to the same prompt: explain recursion to a 10-year-old. Answer A says recursion is when a function calls itself, like Russian dolls. Answer B gives a formal definition.

@2
A labeller picks A, because it suits a 10-year-old. The data is a comparison, not a score out of ten, because comparisons are far more consistent across people.

@3
Many thousands of these comparisons, here about 100,000, train a reward model. Its loss is the Bradley-Terry loss: minus the log of the sigmoid of the preferred answer's reward minus the rejected one's. It scores A at 1.8 and B at 0.6, a gap of 1.2, so the loss is a small 0.26. Ranked the wrong way round, the loss would be much larger, a strong push to fix the order. But remember what this model is: a learned proxy for human judgement, and a proxy can be gamed.

@4
Now the policy itself is trained with PPO. It generates, the reward model scores, and the policy is nudged towards higher reward. A penalty on its distance from the supervised model, measured as a KL divergence, is subtracted. Here the reward is 1.8, the divergence 0.12 and the penalty weight 0.1, so the objective is 1.79. Without that penalty, the policy drifts towards whatever the reward model happens to like: reward hacking. Padding, flattery and agreeing with the user are its common forms.

@5
Sampling, scoring and updating then repeat. In this illustration, the aligned policy wins 68 percent of blind comparisons against the supervised model. Direct preference optimisation reaches a similar result with no separate reward model, by optimising the preference pairs directly, and some pipelines replace part of the human labelling with AI feedback against written principles.
