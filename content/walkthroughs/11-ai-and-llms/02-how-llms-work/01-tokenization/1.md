@0
The text is six words: log, logs, blog, cog, log, cog. Byte-pair encoding starts from single characters, with a special marker in front of every word to show where a word starts. That makes 26 symbols, from a vocabulary of just 7: the marker and the letters l, o, g, s, b and c. Training has one rule, repeated: find the most frequent adjacent pair, and glue it into a new token.

@1
Merge 1. The pair o, g appears 6 times, once in every word, so it becomes the token o g. The vocabulary grows to 8, and the sequence shrinks from 26 symbols to 20.

@2
Merge 2. Recount with the new token. The pair l followed by o g appears 4 times: in both copies of log, in logs, and inside blog. It becomes the token log. Vocabulary 9, sequence 16.

@3
Merge 3. Now the word-start marker followed by log appears 3 times: the two copies of log and the start of logs. It becomes a new token, log at the start of a word. Notice that the log inside blog does not get this merge, because it has no marker in front. That is why, in real vocabularies, log with a leading space and log without one are different tokens.

@4
Merge 4. Several pairs now appear twice, and the first one found wins: the marker followed by c, from the two cogs. Vocabulary 11, sequence 11.

@5
Merge 5. The new token marker c, followed by o g, appears twice, and becomes cog at the start of a word. Vocabulary 12, sequence 9.

@6
Now no adjacent pair occurs more than once, so training stops after 5 merges. Look at what is left. The frequent words, log and cog, are single tokens. Logs is log plus s. And the rare word blog is still in pieces: a lone marker, b, and log.

@7
To encode, each token becomes its number in the vocabulary: 26 characters have become 9 token IDs, and those integers are all the model ever sees. Production tokenizers learn tens or hundreds of thousands of merges, so common words become one token and rare words, names and IDs split into several. That is why cost, context limits, and a model miscounting the letters in a word are all about tokens, not words.
