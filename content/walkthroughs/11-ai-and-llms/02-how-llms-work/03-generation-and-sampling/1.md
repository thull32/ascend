@0
The context is "The capital of France is". The model's last layer gives one score, a logit, to every token in its vocabulary. These are the six highest: Paris 6.1, a 4.0, the 3.6, Lyon 3.1, not 2.5 and famous 2.2. Logits are not text, and not even probabilities. Turning them into one token is the decoding strategy, and it is a pipeline of four steps.

@1
Step one, the softmax: raise e to each logit and divide by the total, so the scores become probabilities that sum to 1. Paris gets 0.77, a 0.09, the 0.06, and the rest a few percent each. A logit gap of 2.1 between Paris and a became a probability ratio of about 8. Greedy decoding would stop here and always pick Paris.

@2
Step two, temperature. Divide every logit by 0.8 before the softmax. A temperature below 1 stretches the gaps and sharpens the distribution: Paris rises from 0.77 to 0.86, and famous falls from 0.02 to about 0.01. As the temperature approaches zero this becomes greedy; a large temperature flattens everything towards uniform. Temperature adds no knowledge. It only decides how often the model says something other than its first choice.

@3
Step three, top-p at 0.9. Sort by probability and keep the smallest set whose mass reaches 0.9. Paris alone is 0.86, not enough, so a joins it, and together they hold 0.93. Everything else is dropped, including Lyon, the plausible-sounding wrong answer, and the two survivors are renormalised to 0.93 and 0.07. Cutting this tail matters: a long answer that keeps drawing from a few percent of tail mass will eventually draw a token that derails it.

@4
Step four, the draw. Pick a uniform random number, here 0.74, and walk the cumulative distribution. Paris covers everything up to 0.93, so 0.74 lands on Paris. It is appended to the context, and the whole pipeline runs again for the next token.

@5
Same model, different knobs, different behaviour. Temperature 0 is deterministic on paper and prone to repetition loops in long text. A moderate temperature with top-p around 0.9 is the usual balance for open-ended answers. A high temperature without truncation lets plausible-looking low-probability tokens through. And remember that output tokens come strictly one at a time, each needing its own pass through the model, which is why they are slower and priced higher than input tokens.
