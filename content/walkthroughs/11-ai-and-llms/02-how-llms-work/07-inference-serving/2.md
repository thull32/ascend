@0
Normal decoding runs the big model once per token, and each run spends most of its time waiting on memory, with the arithmetic units idle. Speculative decoding borrows that idle arithmetic. A small draft model guesses 4 tokens cheaply, and the big target model checks all 4 in one pass, because verifying several tokens costs about the same as generating one: the weights are read once either way. The text so far is "The quick brown".

@1
Round 1. The draft model proposes four tokens, one at a time, in about 8 milliseconds: fox, jumps, over, a.

@2
The target scores all four positions in a single forward pass, about 40 milliseconds, and compares its own probabilities with the draft's. Fox, jumps and over are accepted. At a, the target preferred the, so a is rejected, and anything after a rejection would be discarded.

@3
Commit the three accepted tokens plus the target's own replacement, the. That is 4 tokens from one big-model pass instead of 1. And the output is not an approximation: the rejection-sampling rule guarantees the result has exactly the target model's distribution.

@4
Round 2. The draft proposes lazy, dog, a full stop, and It.

@5
The target verifies all four in one pass, and this time it accepts every one of them.

@6
When all four are accepted, the target's pass also yields its own next token, was, for free, so this round produces 5 tokens from one pass.

@7
Two target passes produced 9 tokens, four and then five, 4.5 per pass, against one token per pass normally. Same quality, since every kept token is one the target would have sampled. The speed-up depends on how often the draft agrees: two to three times on predictable text such as code, boilerplate and edits that copy the input, less on creative writing. And it fades at large batch sizes, because the arithmetic it borrows is no longer idle.
