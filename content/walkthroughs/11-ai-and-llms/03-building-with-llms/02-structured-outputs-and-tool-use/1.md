@0
This panel shows the machinery a schema grammar plugs into. The context is labelled "The candidate's verdict is", but the six candidate tokens on screen are the stock example's: Paris 6.1, a 4.0, the 3.6, Lyon 3.1, not 2.5 and famous 2.2. Ignore the words and watch the operation, because it is the one constrained decoding reuses: zero out some tokens, then renormalise the rest.

@1
The softmax turns the scores into probabilities: Paris 0.77, a 0.09, the 0.06, and a few percent for the others.

@2
A temperature of 0.7 sharpens the distribution: Paris rises to 0.91, and famous falls to almost nothing.

@3
Now the mask. Top-p at 0.9 keeps the smallest set of tokens whose mass reaches 0.9, which here is Paris alone, at 0.91. Every other token gets probability zero, and the survivor is renormalised to 1. A schema grammar does exactly this with a different rule: at every step, any token that would take the text outside the grammar gets probability zero, and the legal tokens share the mass in proportion to the model's own preference.

@4
The draw is 0.95, and since one token holds all the mass, it lands on Paris.

@5
The guarantee and the blind spot both come from this mask. Constrained decoding guarantees the output parses, so the retry-on-parse-error loop can go. But the mask makes the model no smarter. In the lesson's verdict example, the model put 44 percent on maybe, which the schema did not allow; after masking, hire came out 62 percent of the time, a confident-looking answer in a perfect shape. If the honest answer is not representable, the schema forces a guess, so give the enum an honest escape value.
