@0
A retrieval pipeline, seen through an attacker's eyes. The question is what is the refund window? Hold onto one sentence while it runs: whoever can edit an indexed document can put text in front of your model. A retrieval pipeline is an injection pipeline for anyone who can write to the corpus.

@1
The query is embedded with the same model that indexed the documents. Nothing here is attacker-controlled yet.

@2
The index is searched for the nearest chunks: policy section 3 at 0.91, returns section 1 at 0.63, warranty section 2 at 0.55, shipping section 4 at 0.42. Now ask where each of these came from: a shared drive, a wiki page, a public forum? Every source anyone else can write to is a way in.

@3
The top 2 are kept, policy section 3 and returns section 1. Ranking chooses text by topic, not by trustworthiness. A planted paragraph that talks about refunds would rank here just as happily as the real policy.

@4
This is the step that matters: assembling the prompt, with the instructions, the retrieved chunks and their ids, and the question, about 227 tokens. The chunks are data, and the system prompt should say so. That blunts casual attacks, but it is not a boundary. The model reads the chunks in the same token stream as your instructions, and an attacker who can try a hundred variants against a defence that stops 99 percent of attempts gets at least one through about 63 percent of the time.

@5
The model answers from the context it was given, including anything an attacker managed to place there.

@6
Here the answer is the honest one: refunds are accepted within 30 days of delivery, citing policy section 3. The design lesson is about what happens when it is not. The real control is not the prompt; it is what the model can do afterwards. Apply access control at retrieval time, never in the prompt, and make sure a session that has read untrusted text cannot send private data anywhere without a check that does not depend on the model.
