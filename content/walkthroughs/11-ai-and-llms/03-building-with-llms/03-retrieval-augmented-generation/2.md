@0
Retrieval ranks chunks by one score, and this panel shows where that score comes from. Six words as vectors of four numbers, labelled royal, male, female and food so you can read them. Real embeddings have hundreds or thousands of unnamed dimensions. King is royal and male, queen royal and female, man and woman mostly their gender, and apple and banana almost all food.

@1
Their lengths differ: king and queen 1.21, man and woman 0.93, apple 0.92, banana 0.86. Length is not meaning, so similarity ignores it. Cosine divides the dot product by both lengths and compares direction only.

@2-7
The table fills one row at a time, and each row is what a retrieval query computes against every stored vector. King's nearest neighbour is man, at 0.81, ahead of queen at 0.67, because they share the strong male dimension. Queen's nearest is woman, also 0.81. Unrelated pairs sit around a quarter, and apple against banana is 1, as close as two vectors get. Notice what the score measures: closeness of topic and usage, not truth or relevance to a question. That gap is what reranking closes.

@8
Directions can carry relationships too: king minus man plus woman lands nearest queen, with a cosine of 0.99.

@9
That is the whole of the retrieval step: embed the query, compare it with every stored vector, and return the top few by cosine. Normalise every vector to length one when you index it, and cosine becomes a plain dot product, which is what vector indexes compute fastest. And keep keyword search alongside: embeddings are weak on exact tokens such as error codes, product numbers and surnames.
