@0
Exact search compares the query with every stored vector. At ten million chunks, that streams tens of gigabytes through memory for every query. HNSW avoids it with a layered graph: 12 vectors here, n0 to n11, all on the bottom layer, 4 promoted to layer 1, and 2 to the sparse top layer, which acts as an express lane. The query, the ring, is at 7.2 and 3.1.

@1
The search enters at the top layer's entry point, n0, at a distance of 6.55.

@2
On layer 2, n0's only neighbour, n9, is 6.91 away: no closer. Nothing better on this layer.

@3
Descend to layer 1, still at n0. This layer is denser, with up to 2 links per node.

@4
From n0, the neighbours are n6 at 3.72, n9 at 6.91 and n3 at 0.63. Greedy search jumps to n3, crossing most of the space in one hop.

@5
From n3, the layer 1 neighbours, n6 and n0, are both further away. Time to descend.

@6
Down to layer 0 at n3, the full graph, with up to 3 links per node.

@7
n3's neighbours are n2 at 2.72, n7 at 1.84, n4 at 2.77 and n8 at 2.06. None beats 0.63, so the search stops.

@8
The result is n3, the exact nearest neighbour, after 10 distance computations instead of 12. At real scale the saving is enormous, because the hops grow with the logarithm of the number of vectors. But it is approximate: a greedy walk can stop at a node whose neighbours all look worse while the true nearest sits behind one of them. The search beam, ef, buys back recall at the price of latency, and more links per node, M, improve recall at the price of memory. And watch filters: retrieve the top 10 and then filter to a tenant that owns 1 percent of the corpus, and you expect about 0.1 surviving results.
