@0
When prompting is the wrong lever, the next one is fine-tuning, and this is what it looks like with LoRA. A full fine-tune would update all 6.7 billion weights of a 7-billion model and carry optimiser state for each. LoRA freezes them and trains small low-rank adapters of rank 8 on the attention matrices: 4.2 million trainable parameters, 0.06 percent of the model.

@1
The data is about 2,000 prompt and response pairs, such as a ticket and its ideal summary. The loss counts only the response tokens, so the model learns to answer, not to write prompts.

@2-5
Epoch 1. Each batch runs forward through the frozen base plus the adapters. The loss on the response tokens falls from 2.31 to 1.42, with the held-out eval loss at 1.51. Gradients flow back through the frozen weights but are stored only for the adapters, so memory stays small, and AdamW updates the adapters with a learning rate of 0.0002.

@6-9
Epoch 2, the same loop: training loss down to 0.98, eval loss down to 1.12.

@10-13
Epoch 3: training loss 0.81, eval loss 1.06. Both are still improving, but the gap between them is growing, and that gap is the thing to watch.

@14
Three epochs took the training loss from 2.31 to 0.81. The adapters merge into the original weights for serving, at no extra inference cost. So what does it buy? Behaviour: format, tone, policy, a narrow skill, at a scale where a small tuned model can be far cheaper than a long prompt. What does it cost? Labelled data, days rather than minutes to change, and upkeep. And it is a poor way to add facts that keep changing: a tuned model is slow to update, cannot cite, and still invents to fill gaps. For knowledge, use retrieval or tools.
