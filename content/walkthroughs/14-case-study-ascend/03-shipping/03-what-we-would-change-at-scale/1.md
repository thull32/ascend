@0
With one replica, rate limits can live in process memory. Add a second and each keeps its own buckets, so the real limit doubles. Ascend's fix was to move the security buckets into Postgres, and to see why that is cheap, look at how little each key needs. One bucket, one key: it holds at most 5 tokens and regains 1 a second. Capacity sets the burst, the refill rate sets the sustained rate.

@1-5
At time zero the client bursts. Requests 1 to 5 each take a token, and the bucket drains to empty.

@6-7
Requests 6 and 7, in the same instant, are refused with a 429 and a hint about when to retry.

@8-11
Two seconds in, 2 tokens have returned, and request 8 uses one. At three seconds there are 2 again: requests 9 and 10 use them, and request 11 is refused.

@12
By nine seconds the bucket is full again, at 5 and no more, so request 12 goes through with 4 left.

@13
Too big a capacity lets a client dump a full bucket, 5 requests, in one instant; too small rejects honest bursts.

@14
Here is the part that matters at scale. A token bucket needs two numbers per key: the tokens left and the time of the last refill. GCRA is the same meter kept as a single timestamp, the theoretical arrival time of the next allowed request. One timestamp per key is what lets one conditional upsert, on an unlogged table that every replica shares, check and advance the limit in a single statement. If Postgres is down, the shared limiter refuses with a 503, which costs nothing a database outage had not already cost. Only the loose general bucket stays in memory per replica, on purpose: it only stops floods of cheap reads.
