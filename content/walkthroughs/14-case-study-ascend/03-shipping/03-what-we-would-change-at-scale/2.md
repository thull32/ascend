@0
The lesson proposes a global spend breaker for the AI bill. The state machine is the classic circuit breaker, and this run shows it on the classic case, a checkout service calling a payments API that starts failing, not on spend. Watch the three states; the mapping to the bill comes at the end. Closed passes calls and counts failures. After 3 in a row it opens and fails every call instantly. After 5 seconds it goes half-open and lets one trial call decide.

@1
Closed. Call 1 succeeds in 40 milliseconds.

@2-3
The dependency starts timing out. Calls 2 and 3 each hold a caller's thread for the full 2 second timeout: failures 1 and 2 of 3. Slow failures are how one sick dependency exhausts a whole thread pool.

@4
Call 4 is the third failure, and the breaker trips open.

@5-6
Now call 5 is refused with a 503 in under a millisecond, and over the next 5 seconds 16 more are shed the same way. The dependency gets quiet time, and the caller can show a fallback instead of waiting.

@7-8
Five seconds later the breaker goes half-open and lets one trial through. It times out, so the breaker opens again for another 5 seconds.

@9-10
The dependency recovers. The next trial, call 7, succeeds in 45 milliseconds, the breaker closes and resets its count. One failed probe instead of 16 more timeouts.

@11
The usual pitfalls: count a failure rate over a window with a minimum volume rather than a raw count, do not share one breaker across unrelated endpoints, and always have a fallback, or the breaker only fails the user faster.

@12
Now apply the same machine to money, which is the lesson's proposal. Closed while today's priced usage, summed across all users, stays under a daily threshold. Open when it is crossed: AI features switch to the existing disabled path with a graceful message, and someone is paged. Half-open the next day. The per-user budgets bound one learner's day; a spend breaker bounds the whole bill, which at a hundred thousand daily users could otherwise reach about a million dollars on a worst-case day.
