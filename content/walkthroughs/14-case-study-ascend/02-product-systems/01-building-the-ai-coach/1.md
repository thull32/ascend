@0
The coach's reply arrives a few tokens at a time, and all of them flow one way: server to browser. Two hosts here, a browser and a server, and two ways to deliver that stream. First long polling, where the browser asks and the server holds the request until there is news. Then server-sent events, one long-lived response that keeps sending.

@1-2
Long polling first. The browser asks for updates, and the server parks the request, waiting up to 30 seconds for something to say.

@3-4
An event arrives, so the server responds and the exchange is over. The browser immediately asks again. Every single event costs a full request and response, headers and all. For a reply made of hundreds of small token deltas, that overhead is the whole cost.

@5-6
Now the same traffic as server-sent events. One request, asking for an event stream.

@7
And a response that never ends. Events are lines of text: a delta, another delta, and finally done. The browser parses them as they arrive. The built-in EventSource client can only send a GET with no body, so Ascend posts the learner's message with fetch and parses the stream itself, and while the model is thinking it sends a keep-alive comment every 15 seconds.

@8
Pick the simplest transport that fits the direction of the data. Long polling works everywhere but pays a round trip per event. WebSockets are two-way, with their own framing. Server-sent events are simple, one-way and plain HTTP, which is exactly the shape of a streamed model reply, and why the coach uses them.
