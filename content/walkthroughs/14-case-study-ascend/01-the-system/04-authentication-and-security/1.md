@0
Every limiter in Ascend runs GCRA, the generic cell rate algorithm, which admits exactly the same requests as a token bucket, so the bucket is the picture to hold in your head. This demo bucket holds at most 5 tokens and gains 1 a second. A request takes a token, and with none left it is rejected. Capacity sets the burst; the refill rate sets the sustained rate.

@1-5
At time zero, a burst. Requests 1 through 5 each take a token and go through, and the bucket drains from 5 to 0.

@6-7
Requests 6 and 7 arrive in the same instant and find it empty. Each is refused with a 429 and a hint saying when to retry. The client pays for the overload, not the API.

@8
Two seconds later, 2 tokens have dripped back in. Request 8 takes one and goes through.

@9-11
At three seconds there are 2 again. Requests 9 and 10 take them, and request 11, in the same second, is refused.

@12
Then a quiet stretch. By nine seconds the bucket has refilled to its capacity of 5, and it never holds more than that, so request 12 goes through with 4 to spare. Waiting longer does not earn a bigger burst.

@13
Sizing is the trap. Too large a capacity lets a client dump 5 times the sustained rate on the API in one instant. Too small rejects legitimate bursts, like a page that fires several calls at once.

@14
A token bucket needs only two numbers per key, the tokens and the last refill time; GCRA gets it down to one, the theoretical arrival time of the next request, which is why Ascend can keep the shared limits in a single Postgres table that every replica checks. Ascend's password bucket uses the same mechanism with different numbers: a capacity of 10, regaining one token every 6 seconds, per account for unknown devices and per known device otherwise.
