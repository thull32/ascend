@0
Your error contract matters on the other side of the wire too. Here a checkout service calls a payments API through a circuit breaker: a proxy with three states. Closed passes calls through and counts failures. After 3 consecutive failures it opens and fails every call instantly. After 5 seconds it goes half-open and lets a single trial call decide what happens next.

@1
Normal operation. The breaker is closed, call 1 returns 200 OK in 40 milliseconds, and the success keeps the failure count at zero.

@2-3
Then the payments API starts timing out. Call 2 holds a checkout thread for the full 2 second timeout before failing: failure 1 of 3. Call 3 does the same: failure 2 of 3. Every slow failure costs the caller a thread and 2 seconds, and under load that is how one slow dependency exhausts the whole thread pool.

@4
Call 4 times out too. That is the third failure, the threshold is reached, and the breaker trips open.

@5
Now call 5 is rejected with a 503 in under a millisecond, without touching the dependency. A slow failure has become a fast one, and the checkout thread is free again.

@6
Over the next 5 seconds the breaker sheds 15 more calls the same way. The payments API gets quiet time to recover instead of a queue of retries, and checkout can serve a fallback: try again later, a cached quote, or "payment pending".

@7-8
The 5 seconds elapse, and the breaker goes half-open, allowing exactly one trial. The trial, call 6, times out: the dependency is still sick. Back to open for another 5 seconds. Many implementations back that timeout off exponentially.

@9-10
Meanwhile the dependency recovers. Five seconds later the breaker is half-open again, and this time the trial, call 7, returns 200 OK in 45 milliseconds. The breaker closes and resets its count. The whole episode cost one failed probe instead of 15 more timeouts.

@11
Three ways breakers go wrong. A raw count lets one flaky call trip it too often, so use a failure rate over a sliding window with a minimum volume. One breaker shared across unrelated endpoints lets a broken refund endpoint take down charges. And a breaker with no fallback still fails the user, just faster.

@12
This is where the error taxonomy comes in, although the run on screen only ever shows timeouts. A breaker should count timeouts and server errors, the 500s, as failures, and must not count client errors in the 400s: a buggy client sending invalid requests should not open the breaker for every other caller. The breaker gives up the few calls that might have succeeded while it is open, and in exchange makes failure explicit and fast, which is what keeps the rest of the system up.
