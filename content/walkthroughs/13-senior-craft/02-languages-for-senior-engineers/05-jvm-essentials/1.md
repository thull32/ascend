@0
Every JVM collector starts the same way: find what is reachable. On the heap are seven objects, A to G. On the stack, two variables, a and d, are the roots; in a real JVM the roots are thread stacks, static fields and native handles. A points to B, B to C. D points nowhere. E and F point at each other, but nothing on the stack reaches them, and G is orphaned. The rule: reachable from a root means live, and everything else is garbage.

@1
The mark phase starts a worklist with everything the roots point at: A and D.

@2-3
Take A, mark it, and push B, which A points at. Take D, mark it; it points at nothing, so nothing is added.

@4-5
Take B, mark it, and push C. Take C, mark it. The worklist is empty, and four objects are marked: A, D, B and C.

@6
The sweep phase walks the heap in address order and frees everything without a mark.

@7-10
A, B, C and D are marked, so each is kept, with its mark bit cleared for the next collection.

@11-12
E is unmarked, so it is freed, even though F still points at it. Then F goes too. A cycle nobody can reach is garbage, and a tracing collector reclaims it with no extra effort.

@13
G, the orphan, is freed.

@14
Four objects live, three reclaimed. Marking costs time in proportion to what is live, and sweeping in proportion to the heap. Here is where the JVM differs from this picture: its collectors copy or compact the live objects instead of only sweeping, which is what keeps allocation a simple pointer bump. And because most objects die young, a young collection copies the few survivors out and discards the rest wholesale, so its cost follows the survivors, not the garbage.
