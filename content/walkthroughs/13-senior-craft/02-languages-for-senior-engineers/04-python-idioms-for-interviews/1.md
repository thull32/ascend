@0
We want the three largest values from a stream: 3, 2, 1, 5, 6, 4, 9 and 7. The tool is a min-heap that never holds more than three. The idea that makes it work: the root is the smallest of the three best seen so far, so it is the only value a newcomer has to beat.

@1-3
The first values just go in. 3 is pushed at the root. 2 is appended in the next free slot, and since it is smaller than its parent, 3, they swap: 2 is the root.

@4-5
1 is appended at index 2. It is smaller than its parent, 2, so they swap. The heap is full: 1 at the root, then 3 and 2.

@6-8
Now 5 arrives and is compared with the root, 1. Five beats it, so 1 leaves the top three. 5 overwrites the root and sifts down: its smaller child is 2, so they swap, and 5 lands on a leaf. The heap is 2, 3, 5.

@9-11
6 beats the root, 2. It replaces it and sifts down past 3, the smaller child. The heap is 3, 6, 5.

@12-13
4 beats the root, 3, and replaces it. But 4 is already smaller than both children, 6 and 5, so it stays at the top.

@14-16
9 beats 4, replaces it, and sifts down past 5. The heap is 5, 6, 9.

@17-19
Last, 7 beats 5, replaces it, and sifts down past 6. The heap is 6, 7, 9.

@20
The heap holds the three largest: 9, 7 and 6, and the root, 6, is the third largest. Each of the n values costs at most a logarithm of k, so the whole pass is n log k, with memory for only k values. That is why it works on a stream, and it is what heapq's nlargest and a Counter's most common method do internally.
