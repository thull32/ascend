@0
Seven objects on the heap, A through G, and a stack frame with two variables, a and d. Those two are the roots. A points to B and B points to C. D points nowhere. E and F point at each other, but nothing on the stack reaches them, and G is orphaned. The rule a tracing collector lives by: reachable from a root means live, and everything else is garbage.

@1
Mark phase. The worklist starts with every object a root points at: A and D. Marking is a graph traversal from the roots.

@2-3
Take A: mark it live, and push what it points at, B, onto the worklist. Take D: mark it live; it points at nothing, so nothing is added.

@4-5
Take B: mark it, push C. Take C: mark it, and the worklist is empty. Four objects are marked: A, D, B and C.

@6
Sweep phase. The collector walks the whole heap in address order and frees everything without a mark.

@7-10
A, B, C and D are marked, so each is kept, and its mark bit is cleared, ready for the next collection.

@11-12
E is unmarked, so it is garbage and its memory goes back to the free list, even though F still points at it. Then F goes the same way. A cycle that reference counting would leak forever costs a tracing collector nothing extra.

@13
G, the orphan, is freed too.

@14
Collection complete: 4 objects live, 3 reclaimed. Marking costs time in proportion to the live objects, sweeping in proportion to the heap, and unreachable cycles are collected for free. Go runs these two phases concurrently with your program, with two short stop-the-world phases per cycle, and in the lesson's measurements pauses stayed under a millisecond at every setting. What its knobs move is how often the collector runs and how much memory the process holds.
