@0
Two goroutines, a sender and a receiver, and a channel between them. Under the hood a channel is a small ring buffer, a lock, and two queues of parked goroutines. Phase 1 uses an unbuffered channel, where a send completes only when a receiver takes the value. Phase 2 uses a channel with a buffer of 2.

@1
The sender sends 1. Nobody is receiving, so the sender blocks. An unbuffered send is a rendezvous, not a drop-off.

@2
The receiver arrives, and 1 is handed straight across; both goroutines continue. This handoff is a synchronisation point: the send happens before the receive completes, so everything the sender wrote before sending is visible to the receiver afterwards.

@3-4
The receiver is still working on 1, so the sender blocks again on 2, until the receiver comes back and takes it. Two sends, two waits: the sender ran exactly at the receiver's pace. That is backpressure, and you got it for free.

@5-7
Phase 2. With a buffer of 2, the sender's 3 goes straight into the buffer and the sender carries on, even though the receiver is busy. Then 4, and the buffer is full.

@8
The sender tries 5 and blocks. A buffer lets the sender run ahead by its capacity, then stops it. It delays backpressure; it does not remove it.

@9
The receiver takes 3, a slot frees up, and the waiting 5 drops in. The buffer holds 4 and 5.

@10
The sender closes the channel. The receiver can still drain 4 and 5, and after that, a receive returns the zero value with ok set to false at once, so a range loop ends cleanly. Only the sender closes, and only when no more sends can happen: sending on a closed channel panics.

@11
The rule of thumb: unbuffered to hand work over with a happens-before guarantee, small buffers to smooth bursts, and never an unbounded queue, because the bound is what makes overload visible. And channels are for handing over ownership and signalling, not for guarding a counter: with eight goroutines, a mutex cost 32 nanoseconds per increment and an atomic 5.
