@0
A sender and a receiver, joined by a channel, drawn Go-style. There are two phases: first an unbuffered channel, where a send completes only when a receiver takes the value, then a buffered channel with room for 2. The idea to watch for is backpressure: a slow receiver slowing the sender down, instead of letting work pile up in memory.

@1
The sender sends 1. No receiver is waiting, so the sender blocks. An unbuffered send is a rendezvous, not a drop-off.

@2
The receiver arrives and takes the value. 1 is handed straight across, and both continue. That handoff is a synchronisation point: everything the sender did before the send happens before everything the receiver does after it.

@3-4
The receiver is busy processing 1, so the sender blocks again on 2, until the receiver comes back and takes it. Two sends, two waits: the sender ran exactly at the receiver's pace, and that is backpressure for free.

@5-7
Phase 2: a channel with a buffer of 2. The receiver is still busy, but now the sender's 3 drops into the buffer and the sender returns at once. Then 4: the buffer holds 3 and 4, and it is full.

@8
The sender tries to send 5 and blocks. A buffer only delays backpressure by its capacity; it does not remove it.

@9
The receiver takes 3, which frees a slot, and the pending 5 lands in the buffer, which now holds 4 and 5.

@10
The sender closes the channel. The receiver can still drain 4 and 5, and the receive after that reports that the channel is closed, so a loop over the channel ends cleanly.

@11
The rule of thumb: unbuffered to hand work over with a happens-before guarantee, small buffers to smooth bursts, and never an unbounded queue, because the bound is what makes overload visible. Tokio's bounded channel behaves like the buffered phase. Ascend's coach streams through one with a capacity of 64: a slow browser fills it, the send waits, the pump stops pulling from the model, and the slowdown travels back to the model connection instead of into memory.
