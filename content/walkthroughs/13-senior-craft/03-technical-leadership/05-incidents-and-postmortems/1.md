@0
Most incidents follow a change, so the cheapest incident is the one a rollout stops on its own. This is a canary release: a small slice of real traffic goes to the new version, its metrics are compared with the stable version serving everyone else, and only then does the slice widen. On screen: a load balancer, nine stable instances on version 1 erroring at 0.1 percent, one canary slot, and the metrics that judge it. The gate: error rate and 99th percentile latency within 1 percent of stable's, with automatic rollback on.

@1
Version 2 is deployed to the canary. Health checks pass; it takes no traffic yet.

@2-3
Stage 1 sends it one request in ten. If version 2 is broken, at most one user in ten sees it, briefly. Stable answers in 118 milliseconds at the 99th percentile, the canary in 120, with no errors on either. The comparison runs on the same window, so the gate passes and the share widens.

@4
At half the traffic, the canary finally reaches a code path the 10 percent slice never touched. It starts erroring, and its 99th percentile jumps to 410 milliseconds. Stable is still clean.

@5
The gate fails. Stable serving the same traffic with zero errors is what makes the verdict trustworthy: it is the release, not the weather.

@6
And here is the mitigation, with no human in the loop: the canary's weight drops to zero, and stable serves everything again. The bad release reached part of the traffic for one analysis window, instead of everyone for as long as it took someone to notice. That is why rollback is the default mitigation: it takes minutes or less, its risk is low as long as the deploy had no irreversible migration, and you do not need to understand the bug first.

@7-9
The fix ships as version 2.1 and starts again at 10 percent, because nothing is trusted for having passed last time. At half the traffic it is clean, 121 milliseconds against 118, and it is promoted to become the new stable version.

@10
The caveats matter in an incident review. Five canary requests a window are too few to see a 1 percent error rate, so the gate needs a minimum count. A canary that only sees easy traffic, cache hits or one region, passes and then fails at full rollout. And sessions need sticky routing, or a user bounces between versions.

@11
A canary limits the blast radius of a bad release to the canary share, at the cost of slower rollouts, two versions live at once, and analysis that must be statistically honest. It only protects the code paths its traffic actually exercises, which is exactly what fails in the lesson's incident.
