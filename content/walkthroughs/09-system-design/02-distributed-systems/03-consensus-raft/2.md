@0
Five servers: a leader, A, in term 2, and four followers, B, C, D and E. The table shows each log as index and term. Four of the logs agree on three entries, the third from term 2. C's third entry is from term 1: it came from an old leader that was deposed before it committed, and it is about to be overwritten. Hold onto one rule: an entry is committed when a majority holds it, three of these five, and the leader decides that, not the followers.

@1
A client sends the command SET y equals 7 to the leader. Followers redirect clients here.

@2
A appends it to its own log first, as entry 4 in term 2. It is not committed yet: one copy, on the leader alone, is not enough.

@3
A sends AppendEntries to all four followers. The message carries the new entry, plus the index and term of the entry just before it: index 3, term 2. A follower accepts only if its own log matches at that position.

@4
B, D and E all have an entry 3 from term 2. The check passes on each of them, they append entry 4, and they report success.

@5
C's entry 3 is from term 1, not term 2. The check fails and C rejects. One mismatch is enough to refuse, because of the log matching property: if two logs agree at one index and term, the whole prefix before it agrees, so a mismatch at index 3 means nothing from there on can be trusted.

@6
Entry 4 is now on four of the five servers, A, B, D and E, and a majority needs only three. The leader advances its commit index to 4 and applies the command to its state machine. C's refusal did not hold anything up. A committed entry can never be lost by any future leader, because a future leader must win votes from a majority, and that majority includes a server holding it.

@7
Only now is the client told OK. The price of every write: one round trip to a majority, plus a disk flush on each of those servers.

@8
Now the leader repairs C. It steps C's next index back to 3 and resends from there, with both entries 3 and 4. The previous entry is now index 2, term 1, and C has exactly that, so the check passes.

@9
C deletes its conflicting entry from term 1 and appends the leader's entries 3 and 4. The leader's log is the truth, and followers converge to it.

@10
The next heartbeat goes to all four followers and carries commit index 4, and they apply the entries up to 4 to their own state machines. Followers learn of a commit one round trip after the leader does.

@11
One rule this picture does not trip, but the Raft paper spends a whole figure on: a leader may commit by counting replicas only for entries from its own current term. Older entries commit indirectly, once an entry of the current term after them commits. Skip this rule and committed data can be lost.

@12
The trade-off. You get strong consistency and a single ordered log. But the leader is a throughput bottleneck, every write pays a majority round trip, and a minority partition cannot make progress at all. This is what etcd, Consul, CockroachDB and TiKV run.
