@0
Six nodes, N1 to N6, each holding a view of who is alive, and that view travels piggybacked on gossip. The one idea to hold onto: a detector should only suspect. Before anyone acts on a suspicion, other nodes check it, so a single bad link cannot eject a healthy node.

@1
N1 probes N4 directly and hears nothing back. The link between those two is bad, but N4 itself is fine.

@2
Before suspecting anything, N1 asks two other members, N2 and N3, to probe N4 on its behalf.

@3
N2 and N3 each ping N4 over their own links, which have nothing to do with the broken one.

@4
N4 answers both, and they relay the acknowledgements back to N1. N4 is alive. One bad link did not eject a healthy node.

@5
Later, N4 really crashes.

@6
This time the direct probe and both indirect probes fail. N1 marks N4 as suspect, not dead. Suspicion is cheap and local: N1 just stops sending it requests.

@7
The suspicion spreads by gossip like any other update, to N2, N3, N5 and N6, tagged with N4's incarnation number, 3.

@8
Had N4 been alive, it would have heard this rumour about itself and refuted it, by gossiping "alive, incarnation 4". A higher incarnation overrides any suspicion of a lower one, so a node that was only slow or paused clears its own name.

@9
No refutation arrives before the suspicion timeout, so N4 is declared dead, and that too is gossiped to everyone. Only now does one decision-maker start the irreversible work: reassigning N4's partitions, or re-replicating its data.

@10
Suspect quickly, convict slowly. Indirect probes and a refutation window trade a few seconds of detection time for not ejecting healthy nodes over one bad link or a pause. This is SWIM, the protocol behind Consul, Nomad and Serf.
