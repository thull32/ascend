@0
A lease store, two nodes, A and B, and clients. Leadership here is a lease: 5 seconds long, renewed every 2 seconds, with a clock drift bound of 500 milliseconds. Leader election is a lock whose holder does the work while the others wait to take over. Watch the two clocks: the holder's, telling it when to stop, and the store's, telling it when to hand over.

@1-2
A asks for the lease and gets it until t plus 5 seconds. A measures the expiry from when it sent the request, which is the conservative end.

@3-4
A client reads, and A answers from local state, with no round trip, because while the lease holds, nobody else leads.

@5
A renews at t plus 2, and the lease now runs to t plus 7.

@6
Then A is cut off from the store. Its renewal at t plus 4 is lost, and clients keep sending it reads.

@7
At t plus 7 by its own clock, A steps down, without any message telling it to.

@8
The store sees the lease lapse, and waits another 500 milliseconds, the drift bound, before granting it again.

@9-10
B takes the lease, until t plus 12 and a half, and clients are redirected to it. Nobody served in between. That gap is the deliberate margin between the holder stopping and the store reassigning. Kubernetes leader election has the same shape: its controllers stop at a 10 second renew deadline, and a candidate takes over only after a 15 second lease duration.

@11
Now the case this picture does not draw. A was cut off, so its own clock could tell it to stop. A holder that is paused, rather than cut off, cannot step down, because it is not running to notice. It wakes up after B has taken over and acts as leader for a moment. That is a stale read or a split-brain write, and no lease length prevents it. It is the case fencing exists for.

@12
So a leader lease buys local reads and bounded failover, at the cost of an unavailability window after a crash, and a safety argument that rests on clocks and pauses behaving. When a wrong leader would corrupt something, the resource must also check a token.
