@0
A trip booking: an orchestrator and three services, each with its own database, for the flight, the hotel and the car. The table under them is the saga log, which the orchestrator persists. A saga runs one business transaction as a sequence of local transactions, each committed straight away. If a later step fails, the earlier steps are not rolled back. They are undone, by compensating transactions.

@1-2
Step 1, the flight service books the flight and commits. Step 2, the hotel service books the room and commits. Each change is visible to everyone the moment it commits: there is no isolation across a saga.

@3
Step 3 fails: the car cannot be reserved. The flight and the hotel are already committed in other services, and no database rollback can reach them.

@4
So the orchestrator switches to compensation. It runs the undo action for every completed step, in reverse order. Each compensation is itself a local transaction, retried until it succeeds.

@5
First, cancel the hotel, undoing step 2. The room goes back on sale.

@6
Then cancel the flight, undoing step 1. Notice what kind of undo this is: the booking happened, and so does its cancellation. Both stay on record; the compensation reverses the effect, not the history.

@7
The saga ends in the aborted state. Every transition was written to the saga log before the next call, so if the orchestrator itself crashes halfway, its replacement reads the log and resumes from the last recorded step, instead of leaving a half-booked trip behind.

@8
Three failure modes. While the saga runs, other transactions can see the intermediate state: a flight booked and no car. A compensation might arrive twice, so it must be idempotent. And some actions have no undo, like an email already sent, so put the irreversible step last.

@9
The trade-off. Sagas keep services independent and available, with no locks held across services, but you give up atomicity and isolation, and you design every compensation by hand. Choreography, with events and no orchestrator, removes the central coordinator, at the cost of a flow nobody can read in one place.
