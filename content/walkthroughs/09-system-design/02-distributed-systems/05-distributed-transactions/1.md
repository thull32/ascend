@0
A coordinator and three independent databases: Orders, Payments and Inventory. Two-phase commit makes one transaction atomic across all three: either every one commits, or none does. The coordinator drives two rounds, prepare and then commit or abort. Watch for one moment above all: the single log write where the outcome is decided.

@1
Phase 1 for transaction T1. The coordinator asks every participant whether it can commit.

@2
Each participant force-writes a prepare record to its own log, keeps its locks, and votes yes. A yes is a promise: from now on it must be able to commit T1, even after a crash.

@3
The coordinator writes COMMIT to its own log. That one write is the commit point. Once it is on disk, the outcome is decided, whatever fails next.

@4-5
Phase 2. The decision goes out, each participant commits, releases its locks and acknowledges, and the coordinator can forget T1. Two round trips and several disk flushes, for one atomic transaction.

@6-7
Now T2. Another prepare round, and Orders and Payments vote yes.

@8
But Inventory cannot: a constraint is violated, or it timed out. One no is enough.

@9
The coordinator logs ABORT and tells everyone to roll back. Atomicity holds: nobody committed T2.

@10-11
Now T3, the dangerous one. Everyone votes yes and holds their locks, waiting for the decision.

@12
And the coordinator crashes, after collecting the votes but before sending a decision. The participants are in doubt. They cannot commit, because the coordinator may have decided abort. They cannot abort, because it may have decided commit.

@13
This is the blocking problem. The locks stay held, other transactions on those rows queue behind them, and no participant can resolve it alone. A timeout does not help, because voting yes gave up the right to abort.

@14
Eventually the coordinator restarts, replays its log, finds T3 undecided, decides, and completes phase 2. The participants were blocked for the whole outage.

@15
The trade-off. True atomic commit across systems, at the cost of two round trips, several disk flushes per transaction, and availability tied to one coordinator. Real systems either make the coordinator durable by replicating it, as Spanner does with Paxos groups, or avoid two-phase commit altogether with sagas and idempotent messaging.
