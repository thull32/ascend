@0
Two replicas, A and B, that should hold the same keys but do not quite. A holds k1, k2, k3 and k7. B holds all of those, plus k9 and k105. Anti-entropy has to find the difference, and instead of sending its whole key list, A sends a Bloom filter of it: 16 bits, with 3 hash functions. The filter answers only "definitely not" or "probably yes", and that one-sided answer is exactly what this job needs.

@1
A adds k1. Its three hashes point at bits 7, 14 and 9, and those bits are set to 1.

@2-4
A adds k2, setting bits 10, 11 and 0. Then k3, setting 13, 8 and 3. Then k7, setting 1, 4 and 15. Bits only ever get set, so the filter never forgets a key it was given.

@5
A sends the finished filter to B: 16 bits, instead of a list of every key.

@6
B tests each of its own keys against A's filter. K1, k2, k3 and k7 all find their three bits set: A probably has them, so B sends nothing for those.

@7
K9 hashes to bits 15, 6 and 1, and bit 6 is 0. So A definitely lacks k9: a Bloom filter never says no wrongly. B sends k9 across.

@8
K105 also exists only on B. But its bits, 0, 13 and 10, were all set by A's other keys: a false positive. B wrongly concludes that A has it, and sends nothing. That difference survives this round. The next round, with a different filter, or a Merkle tree comparison, will find it.

@9
So the digest cost 16 bits rather than a list of keys, and every key it said was missing really was. Its error only runs one way: it can hide a difference for a round, never invent one. At real sizes, a million keys at a 1 percent false positive rate take about 1.2 megabytes, under 10 bits per key, in a single message.
