@0
A router and three shards, each owning a contiguous range of the key space: A to I, J to Q, and R to Z. The router keeps the range table, so finding a key's shard is a binary search over the boundaries, with no hashing at all.

@1-3
Alice starts with A, which falls in A to I, so she goes to shard 1. Bob and hank fall in the same range, and join her there. Inside a shard, keys are stored in sorted order.

@4
Judy starts with J, which falls in J to Q, so she goes to shard 2.

@5
Now a range query: names between A and I. It touches one shard and reads keys that sit next to each other in sorted order. That locality is the whole point of range partitioning.

@6
Here is the failure mode. The table is now keyed by order id: shard 1 holds orders 1 to 1000, shard 2 holds 1001 to 2000, and shard 3 holds everything from 2001 up. Order ids only increase, so every new key is the largest yet, and it always lands in the last range.

@7
Orders 3001, 3002, 3003 all go to shard 3, which takes every single write. That is the hot tail. Two shards sit idle while one saturates, and adding shards does not help, because they receive nothing until the tail moves to them.

@8
Two fixes. Split the hot range automatically and move half of it to another shard, as Bigtable, HBase and CockroachDB do, although with keys that only increase, a split just creates a new last range. Or salt the key with a hash prefix, which spreads the writes but breaks cheap range scans.

@9
So range partitioning gives cheap range scans and sorted locality, but needs a range table to maintain and rebalance, and is vulnerable to hot spots on skewed or sequential keys. Hash partitioning is its mirror image.
