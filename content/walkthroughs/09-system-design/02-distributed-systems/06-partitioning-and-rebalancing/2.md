@0
A router and three nodes, and under them a table of twelve partitions, p0 to p11. The partition count is chosen once, far larger than the number of nodes, and never changes. A key's partition is its hash mod 12, fixed forever. A separate table assigns partitions to nodes, four each. Keep those two layers apart, because the whole trick is that growth only ever touches the second one.

@1
The first four keys. Alice hashes to p11 and bob to p8, both on N3. Dave hashes to p7 and erin to p1, both on N2. The router looks each partition up in the table to find its node.

@2
Four more. Ivan lands in p9 and judy in p3, both on N1. Mallory lands in p1, next to erin, and mia in p10, both on N2.

@3
The last four. Olivia joins judy in p3 and peggy joins alice in p11. Trent lands in p0 on N1, and walter in p2 on N3. Twelve keys in twelve partitions on three nodes.

@4
Now a fourth node, N4, joins. The table hands it three whole partitions: p0 from N1, p1 from N2 and p2 from N3, so every node now holds three. The keys inside them, trent, erin, mallory and walter, travel with their partitions as whole-partition copies. Look at the keys column: not one key changed partition, and nothing was rehashed.

@5
Compare plain hash mod N over the nodes. Going from three nodes to four would send eight of these twelve keys somewhere new, one by one. Here the movement was three partition copies, and the only metadata change was three entries in the table.

@6
The catch is that the partition count is fixed at creation. Too few, and you cannot grow past that many nodes or split a hot partition. Too many, and each one costs files, memory and a leader election. Kafka topics, Elasticsearch indices and Redis Cluster's 16,384 hash slots all work this way.
