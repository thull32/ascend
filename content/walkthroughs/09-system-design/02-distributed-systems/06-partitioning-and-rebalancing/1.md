@0
A ring: the hash space bent into a circle, with positions from 0 to 359 degrees. Four nodes sit on it, evenly spaced: N1 at 40 degrees, N2 at 130, N3 at 220 and N4 at 310. Keys are hashed onto the same circle, and the one rule is this: a key belongs to the first node you reach walking clockwise from it, wrapping past the top.

@1-4
User 1 lands at 159 degrees; walking clockwise, the first node is N3 at 220. User 7 lands at 124 and walks to N2 at 130. Video 9, at 35 degrees, stops at N1 at 40. Session 3, at 73, walks on to N2.

@5
Cart 5 lands at 322 degrees, past the last node. Walking clockwise it wraps past the top, and the first node it meets is N1 at 40.

@6-12
Seven more keys follow the same rule. Feed 2 and user 3 walk to N3. User 2, user 5 and user 12 walk to N4. User 6 walks to N2, and user 9, at 25 degrees, stops at N1.

@13
All twelve keys are placed, three on each node. Each node owns the arc that ends at its own position: N1 owns everything from just past 310 round to 40, and so on.

@14
Now a fifth node, N5, joins at 30 degrees, between N4 at 310 and N1 at 40. It claims only that arc. Cart 5 and user 9 sit on it, so they move from N1 to N5: two of the twelve keys, shown in red. No key moves between the old nodes. With hash mod N, growing from four nodes to five would have remapped nine of these twelve keys.

@15
The ideal is that the newcomer takes its fair share, about one fifth of the keys, and nothing else moves; two of twelve is close. But with one point per node, how much it takes depends on where it happens to land, and here all of its keys came from a single neighbour. That is why real systems give each physical node many positions on the ring, often around 100 to 200 virtual nodes: the arcs even out, a new node takes a little from everyone, and a failed node's load spreads across many neighbours instead of doubling one. Dynamo, Cassandra, memcached clients and CDN routing all work this way.
