@0
A ring: the hash space bent into a circle, from 0 to 360 degrees. Four nodes sit on it where their names hash to: N4 at 159 degrees, N2 at 161, N3 at 342 and N1 at 344. The one rule: a key belongs to the first node clockwise from its position.

@1-2
Keys are hashed onto the same ring. User 1 lands at 187 degrees, user 7 at 181. Walking clockwise from either, the first node is N3, at 342.

@3
Video 9 lands at 345 degrees, just past N1. Walking clockwise, it wraps past zero and reaches N4 at 159, so N4 owns it.

@4-6
Session 3 at 164 degrees, cart 5 at 294, and feed 2 at 165 all walk clockwise to N3 as well. Look at how lopsided that is: N3 owns five of the six keys, because the gap behind it, from 161 all the way to 342 degrees, is enormous.

@7
Now add a fifth node, N5, at 340 degrees. Its predecessor clockwise is N2 at 161, so N5 claims the arc from 161 to 340, and only the keys in that arc change owner. Everything else stays put: video 9 stays on N4. Here that arc happens to hold 5 of the 6 keys, which is the same unluckily placed gap showing again. With hash mod N, nearly every key would move whatever the positions. With a ring, only the new node's arc moves, but how big that arc is depends on where the positions fall.

@8
That is why real systems give each physical node many positions on the ring, often around 100 to 200 virtual nodes. Many small arcs average out the luck you just saw, so load evens out, and when a node fails its keys spread across many neighbours instead of doubling one. Dynamo, Cassandra, Memcached clients and CDN routing all use this.
