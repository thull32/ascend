@0
No leader here. A client, a coordinator, and three replicas, each holding x equals v1 with timestamp 9. Three numbers set the behaviour: N, the replica count, is 3. A write must be acknowledged by W of them, here 2. A read must hear from R of them, also 2. The one rule to hold onto: when W plus R is greater than N, here 4 against 3, every read overlaps every set of replicas that acknowledged a write, so a read reaches at least one copy of the latest acknowledged write.

@1
The client sends a write of v2 to a coordinator. Any node can coordinate.

@2
The coordinator sends it to all three replicas in parallel, stamped with timestamp 12.

@3
Replicas 1 and 2 apply it and acknowledge. Replica 3 is slow, stuck in a garbage collection pause or a disk stall, and still holds v1. Two acknowledgements meet W, so the coordinator does not wait.

@4
The write succeeds even though replica 3 never took it. A quorum of two out of three tolerates one slow or dead replica on the write path.

@5
Now a read arrives. The coordinator contacts only two replicas: it asks replica 2 for the data and replica 3 for a digest, a hash of its value.

@6
Replica 2 returns v2 at timestamp 12. Replica 3's digest does not match, so the coordinator fetches its full data: v1 at timestamp 9. This is the overlap rule paying off. Two readers and two writers out of three must share at least one replica, so the newest version is among the replies, and the highest timestamp wins.

@7-8
Before answering, the coordinator writes v2 back to replica 3 and waits for it. That is read repair, done in the blocking style Cassandra uses by default since version 4.0. Only then does the client get v2. Keys that nobody reads are fixed instead by background anti-entropy.

@9
Now the lesson's trace of why a quorum is not linearizable. A client writes v3. Replica 1 has applied it; the copy to replica 2 is still in flight, and the write is not yet acknowledged.

@10
Reader B consults replicas 1 and 3, sees v3 at timestamp 15 on replica 1, the newest, and returns it.

@11
Reader C starts after B has returned and consults replicas 2 and 3. Without any write-back, neither has v3, and C gets v2. B saw the new value, then C saw the old one: no single real-time order explains both.

@12
Blocking read repair fixes this case. B's read writes v3 to replica 3 before returning, so two replicas hold it, and C's read must find it.

@13
The failure mode in tuning is choosing W plus R no greater than N, say W of 1 and R of 1 for speed. There is no overlap at all, and the client can see v1 after v2 was acknowledged. So tune per workload: write to all and read from one for read-heavy data, or write to one for data you can afford to lose.

@14
What read repair still does not fix: concurrent writes to different replicas ordered by last-writer-wins, a write that failed before reaching W but sits on one replica and is later repaired into view, and sloppy quorums with hinted handoff, where nodes outside the key's home accept writes during a partition. Linearizable operations need consensus.

@15
The trade-off: no leader and no election, availability through partial failures, and consistency you can tune per request. But every operation touches several replicas, and the guarantees are weaker than they look. This is the model of Dynamo, Cassandra and Riak.
