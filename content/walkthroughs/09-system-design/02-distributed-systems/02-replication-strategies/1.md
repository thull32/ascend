@0
One leader and two followers, all holding x equals 1 at log position 7. The leader is the only node that accepts writes. It streams its write-ahead log to the followers, which apply it in order, serve reads, and stand by in case the leader dies. Everything this picture teaches comes down to one question: when does the leader tell the client OK?

@1
A client writes x equals 2. The leader appends it to its log as position 8 and applies it locally.

@2
In asynchronous mode, the leader acknowledges as soon as its own log is durable. Neither follower has the change yet.

@3
Now the leader streams position 8 to both followers. On a good day that takes a few milliseconds; under load or across regions it can take seconds.

@4
Reads are spread across the followers, so the client's next read goes to Follower 1. But Follower 1 is still at position 7, one entry behind.

@5
It answers x equals 1. The client just wrote 2 and reads 1: its own write seems to have vanished. That is a violation of read-your-writes, caused by nothing more than replication lag.

@6
A few milliseconds later both followers apply position 8 and catch up. The system is eventually consistent: the lag window is real, but usually short.

@7
Two fixes for that window. Route a client that wrote recently to the leader for its reads, or have the follower wait until its applied log position has reached the client's last write.

@8
Now switch Follower 1 to synchronous. The leader will wait for it to confirm each write before acknowledging. Usually only one follower is synchronous, so a single slow follower cannot stall every write.

@9-11
A write of x equals 3 arrives and becomes log position 9. The leader ships it to both followers, and this time it waits. Follower 1 writes position 9 durably to disk and acknowledges.

@12
Only now does the client hear OK, and at that moment the write exists on two nodes. The price is latency: the leader's own flush plus one round trip to Follower 1.

@13
Then the leader dies. Someone, a coordinator or the nodes themselves through consensus, must pick the most up-to-date follower, promote it, and repoint the clients.

@14
Follower 1 holds position 9, so it is promoted, and Follower 2 now follows it. Here is where the two modes differ. With asynchronous replication, any write the old leader acknowledged but had not yet shipped is simply gone. With a synchronous follower it is safe. And if the old leader comes back still believing it leads, two nodes accept writes, so it must be fenced first.

@15
The trade-off. Asynchronous gives low write latency and tolerates slow followers, but can lose acknowledged writes, roughly the write rate times the lag. Synchronous guarantees a second durable copy, at the cost of latency, and of availability whenever that follower is slow. Either way, reads scale out across followers; writes do not.
