@0
Three boxes: a producer, a buffer that holds 8 messages, and a consumer that can process 2 messages a tick. Backpressure is the consumer's ability to slow the producer down. Without it, a fast producer and a slow consumer are separated only by a buffer, and every buffer is finite.

@1
First, the push model. The producer fires 20 messages at once. The buffer keeps 8 and is full; the other 12 have nowhere to go, and the dropped counter reads 12. Had the buffer been unbounded, the process would keep growing until it was killed for running out of memory.

@2
The consumer drains 2 per tick, its real capacity, and the buffer falls to 6. The 12 dropped messages are gone, and nobody upstream was told, so the producer believes it delivered all 20.

@3
The fix is to flip the direction of control. The consumer tells the producer how many messages it may send, as credits, a window, or a request for n, and the producer may not exceed that number. Sending becomes driven by the consumer's pull.

@4
The consumer looks at its free space, 2 slots, and grants exactly 2 credits.

@5
The producer sends 2 and stops. With zero credits it is blocked, and the buffer is exactly full at 8 of 8. It can never overflow, by construction.

@6
Each tick the consumer processes 2 and hands back 2 credits. In steady state the producer runs at exactly the consumer's rate, and the buffer only absorbs jitter.

@7
The pressure travels upstream. A blocked producer stops reading from its own source, whose handlers slow down, whose clients see latency or a too-many-requests error. That is the point: the slowest stage sets the pace for the whole pipeline, and overload shows up at the edge instead of as silent data loss in the middle.

@8
There is a failure mode. Backpressure that reaches a user as a hung request is not always acceptable. For metrics or telemetry, dropping is the right policy, whether the oldest, the newest, or a sample; for orders it is not. And beware cycles: a producer blocked on a consumer that is blocked on the producer is a deadlock.

@9
In the lesson's consumer design, this is the bounded in-flight window. The consumer pulls only when it has capacity, so the backlog stays on the broker, where it is durable and observable, instead of in the consumer's memory. Size that window with Little's law: throughput times processing time. Backpressure buys bounded memory and honest overload signals, at the cost of tying the producer's speed to the consumer's.
