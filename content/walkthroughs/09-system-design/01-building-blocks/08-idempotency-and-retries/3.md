@0
One client retrying against an overloaded service. The rule: after each failure, wait longer, doubling the delay each time, 100, 200, 400 milliseconds and so on. Jitter randomises each wait, so that clients that failed together do not retry together.

@1-2
Attempt 1 fails with 503, overloaded. A 503 or a timeout is worth retrying; a 400 or a 404 is not, and retrying it only adds load. The schedule says wait up to 100 milliseconds, and full jitter picks a random point in that range: 24.

@3-4
Attempt 2 fails. The window doubles to 200 milliseconds, and the random pick is 183, so the next attempt goes at 207 milliseconds in total.

@5-8
Attempts 3 and 4 fail too. The windows grow to 400 and 800 milliseconds; the picks are 245 and 742, bringing the total to 1,194.

@9-10
Attempt 5 fails. The window is now 1,600 milliseconds, but the random pick happens to be just 79. Full jitter means any point in the window, including near zero.

@11
Attempt 6 succeeds, after 1,273 milliseconds of total waiting. Had every retry fired immediately, all six would have landed inside the same overload window and failed.

@12
Now scale it up. A thousand clients fail at the same instant and, without jitter, all sleep exactly 100 milliseconds and retry in lockstep. The service, just starting to recover, is hit by the same wave again. The lesson's simulation puts a number on it: 2,000 clients in lockstep took 971 seconds and about 100 attempts each to all get served.

@13
With full jitter, the same retries arrive spread across the whole interval, and the service sees a ramp it can serve. In the simulation, any jitter brought that 971 seconds down to about 5.

@14
Two more rules. Cap retries with a budget, around 10 percent of traffic, so a full outage cannot multiply load by the attempt count. And retry only idempotent operations, or a charge that timed out but succeeded is charged again.

@15
The trade-off: retries turn transient failures into successes at the cost of latency and extra load exactly when the system can least afford it. Backoff bounds the load and jitter de-synchronises it. But jitter only helps when failures are synchronised. In the lesson's ten-second stall, backoff and jitter changed nothing; budgets, and servers that drop requests whose deadline has passed, are what ended the storm.
