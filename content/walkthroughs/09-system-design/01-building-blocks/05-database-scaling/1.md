@0
One leader and two followers. Only the leader accepts writes; it streams its log to both followers, which apply it in order, serve reads, and stand by in case the leader dies. Keep one question in mind throughout: when the client hears OK, where does the write actually exist?

@1
The client writes x equals 2. The leader appends the change to its write-ahead log as log position 8 and applies it locally.

@2
This is asynchronous mode, so the leader acknowledges as soon as its own log is durable. Neither follower has the change yet. The answer to our question, right now, is one node.

@3
The leader streams position 8 to both followers. On a good day that takes a few milliseconds; under load or across regions it can take seconds.

@4-5
Reads are scaled out, so the client's next read goes to Follower 1, which is still at position 7. It answers x equals 1. The client just wrote 2 and reads 1: its own write seems to have vanished. That is a read-your-writes violation, and it is what users report as an edit that disappears on reload.

@6
A few milliseconds later both followers apply position 8 and catch up. The system is eventually consistent: the lag window is real, but usually short.

@7
Two fixes for the window. Route recent writers' reads to the leader, or have the follower wait until its applied log position has reached the client's last write. That second one is the token trick from the lesson: the write returns its position, and the replica serves the read only once it has replayed that far.

@8
Now switch to synchronous mode. The leader waits for Follower 1 to confirm each write before acknowledging. Usually only one follower is synchronous, so one slow follower cannot stall every write.

@9-11
The client writes x equals 3. The leader logs it at position 9, ships it, and waits. Follower 1 reports position 9 durably on its disk.

@12
Only now does the client hear OK, and the write exists on two nodes. The price is latency: the leader's flush plus one round trip to Follower 1, about a millisecond more per commit across availability zones.

@13
Then the leader dies. Something, a coordinator or the nodes themselves through consensus, must pick the most up-to-date follower, promote it, and repoint the clients. Follower 1 has position 9; Follower 2 is still at 8.

@14
Follower 1 is promoted and Follower 2 now follows it. Because position 9 was synchronous, nothing acknowledged is lost. Had it been asynchronous, any write the old leader acknowledged but never shipped would be gone, like the confirmed order in the lesson that no longer exists after failover. And if the old leader comes back believing it still leads, two nodes accept writes, so fence it first.

@15
The trade-off. Asynchronous gives low write latency and tolerates slow followers, but can lose acknowledged writes. Synchronous guarantees a second durable copy, at the cost of latency and of availability whenever that follower is slow. Either way, reads scale out and writes do not.
