@0
A hash ring. Nodes and keys share one circle of positions, from 0 to 359 degrees, and a key belongs to the first node at or after its position, walking clockwise. Four shards sit evenly around it: N1 at 40 degrees, N2 at 130, N3 at 220 and N4 at 310. Hold onto one sentence: each node owns the arc that ends at its own position.

@1-2
User 1 hashes to 159 degrees. Walking clockwise, the first node is N3 at 220, so N3 owns it. User 7, at 124 degrees, stops at N2.

@3-6
Video 9 lands at 35 and goes to N1. Session 3 at 73 goes to N2. Cart 5 lands at 322, past the last node, so the walk wraps over the top to N1. Feed 2 at 175 goes to N3.

@7-12
Six more user keys follow the same rule, landing at 278, 187, 236, 114, 25 and 307 degrees and walking to N4, N3, N4, N2, N1 and N4.

@13
All twelve keys are placed, three on each shard.

@14
Now a fifth shard, N5, joins at 30 degrees, between N4 at 310 and N1 at 40. It claims only that arc. Cart 5 and user 9 move from N1 to N5: 2 of the 12 keys, close to the fifth that is the new shard's fair share. No key moves between the old shards. With hash mod N, going from four shards to five would have remapped 9 of these 12.

@15
How much a newcomer takes depends on where it lands, and real positions land at random. The lesson's simulation says it plainly: with one token per server, the unluckiest server holds nearly three times its share while another holds almost none. So real systems give each physical node many virtual nodes, around 100 to 200, so the arcs average out, a new node takes a little from everyone, and a failed node's load spreads over many neighbours instead of one. Cassandra used 256 tokens for years, and later 16 placed by an allocation algorithm rather than at random.
