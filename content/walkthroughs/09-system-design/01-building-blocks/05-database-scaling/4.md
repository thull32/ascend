@0
A hash ring. Nodes and keys are hashed onto the same circle, and a key belongs to the first node you meet walking clockwise from it. Four nodes sit on this ring, but look where they landed: N4 at 159 degrees and N2 at 161, almost on top of each other, and N3 at 342 and N1 at 344, likewise. Hold onto that, because it decides how this plays out.

@1-2
User 1 hashes to 187 degrees. Walking clockwise, the first node is N3 at 342, so N3 owns it. User 7, at 181 degrees, also walks to N3.

@3
Video 9 hashes to 345 degrees, past every node, so the walk wraps around the top of the circle and reaches N4 at 159.

@4-6
Session 3 at 164, cart 5 at 294 and feed 2 at 165 all walk to N3 as well. Five of the six keys sit on N3, because N3 owns the whole arc from 161 to 342 degrees, about half the circle.

@7
Now add N5, at 340 degrees. The rule holds: only keys between the new node and its predecessor change owner, and nobody else's keys move. But here N5's predecessor is N2 at 161, so N5 takes over almost all of N3's huge arc, and five of the six keys move to it. On an evenly spread ring a new node takes about one Nth of the keys; with one position per node, the ring is rarely even. The lesson's simulation says the same: with one token per server, the unluckiest server holds nearly three times its share while another holds almost none.

@8
Which is why real systems give each physical node many virtual nodes, around 100 to 200 here, so the arcs average out and a failed node's load spreads over many neighbours instead of one. Cassandra used 256 tokens for years, and later 16 placed by an allocation algorithm rather than at random. This is how Dynamo, Cassandra, Memcached clients and CDN routing place their keys.
