@0
Three shards, each owning a contiguous slice of the key space: S1 has A to I, S2 has J to Q, S3 has R to Z. The router keeps this range table, so finding a key's shard is a binary search over the boundaries, with no hashing at all.

@1-3
Alice starts with A, so she goes to S1. Bob, B, also S1. Hank, H, S1 again. Inside each shard the keys are stored in sorted order.

@4
Judy starts with J, which falls in J to Q, so she goes to S2.

@5
Here is the payoff. A range query, names between A and I, touches exactly one shard and reads keys that sit next to each other in sorted order. That locality is the whole point of range sharding.

@6
Now the failure. Change the key to something that only increases, like an order number or a timestamp. The ranges become orders 1 to 1,000 on S1, 1,001 to 2,000 on S2, and everything from 2,001 up on S3. Every new key is the largest yet.

@7
So every insert, 3,001, 3,002, 3,003, lands on S3. That is the hot tail: one shard takes all the writes while the other two sit idle. Adding shards does not help, because a new shard receives nothing until the tail moves to it.

@8
Two fixes. Split the hot range automatically and move half of it to another shard, which is what Bigtable, HBase and CockroachDB do. Or salt the key with a hash prefix, which spreads the writes but gives up the cheap range scans you chose range sharding for.

@9
The trade-off: cheap range scans and sorted locality, in exchange for a range table to maintain and rebalance, and real exposure to hot spots on skewed or sequential keys. In the lesson's terms, created at is the right key for an archive and the wrong one for writes. Hash sharding, next, is the mirror image.
