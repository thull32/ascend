@0
Clients, a facade, the old monolith, and a new service. Below them, the facade's routing table, with four routes: users, orders, billing and search. This is the strangler fig. Put a facade in front of the legacy system, then move one capability at a time behind it, while the old system keeps serving everything not yet moved. No big-bang rewrite, ever.

@1
Request 1 asks for users. Today every route goes to the monolith, and the facade is a proxy that changes nothing. That is the safe first step: deploy the facade alone, and prove it adds no latency and no errors.

@2
Step 1. The users capability has been rebuilt in the new service, and the facade's table is flipped for that one route. Flipping is a configuration change, so it can be reversed in seconds if the new service misbehaves.

@3-4
Request 2, for users, goes to the new service, and the client cannot tell anything changed. Request 3, for orders, is still owned by the monolith, so the facade forwards it there untouched.

@5
Step 2 is safer still: orders is shadowed. The facade sends request 4 to both systems, returns the monolith's answer, and compares the new service's answer offline. Every mismatch is a bug found with no user impact.

@6
After a clean shadow period, orders is flipped for real. The new service owns two routes, and the monolith still owns billing and search and still runs. Nothing forced a cut-over.

@7-10
Requests 5 and 6, for billing and search, go to the monolith. Requests 7 and 8, for users and orders, go to the new service. Two systems are serving one product, and the clients see one API.

@11
Here is where it gets hard: the data. The new service and the monolith both need the orders table. Either the service reads the monolith's database, which is the coupling you meant to remove, or you replicate between them, with change data capture or the outbox, never two application writes, until the last consumer moves. Migrating the data is usually the slow part.

@12
Eventually every route points at the new side, and the monolith owns nothing. It is switched off, and the facade can stay on as the gateway. Each step was small, reversible, and shipped value on its own.

@13
The trade: many low-risk steps instead of one risky rewrite, with old and new running side by side for months. You pay for running both, two codebases to keep consistent, and a facade to keep honest. And the shared database is where most migrations stall, which is why moving data ownership gets its own careful steps: replicate, backfill, verify counts and checksums, cut reads, then writes.
