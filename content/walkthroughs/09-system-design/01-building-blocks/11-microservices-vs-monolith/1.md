@0
A client and five services in a row: gateway, orders, inventory, pricing and payments. One request must pass through all five in sequence. Each service on its own is 99.9 percent available, and answers in 5 milliseconds at the median and 50 at the 99th percentile. The table counts what compounds as the request moves down the chain.

@1
Hop 1, client to gateway: a network round trip, serialisation, and one service that must be up. So far the request is 99.9 percent likely to find everything up, and there is a 1 percent chance it has hit some service's slowest 1 percent.

@2-3
Hop 2, gateway to orders, and hop 3, orders to inventory. Each adds a round trip and one more service that must be up and fast. Three hops in, all-up is down to 99.7 percent, and the chance of having hit some slow tail is 3 percent.

@4-5
Hops 4 and 5, on to pricing and then payments. After five hops, the chance that every service was up is 99.5 percent, and nearly 5 percent of requests have met at least one service's slowest 1 percent.

@6
The answer unwinds back through every hop. The request was only as available as all five services at once, and only as fast as their sum.

@7
So five sequential hops turn 99.9 percent per service into 99.5 percent end to end: about 3.6 hours of downtime a month instead of 43 minutes. The latency compounds too. In the lesson's simulation the chain's median is 35 milliseconds, not 25, its 99th percentile is 124 milliseconds, and a quarter of requests take longer than any single service's 99th percentile.

@8
The levers: fewer hops on the hot path, calls in parallel instead of in series, tight timeouts on each call and hedged requests for the tail, or keeping the boundary inside one process, where a function call cannot time out. Every arrow in a microservice diagram is a hop like these.
