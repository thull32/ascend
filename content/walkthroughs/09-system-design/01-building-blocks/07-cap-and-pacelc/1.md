@0
Three replicas of one key, all at v1. A write needs two acknowledgements and a read needs two replicas. Two is a majority of three, so any two quorums overlap. Keep asking one question as this plays out: which side can still make progress when the network splits?

@1
A partition: R3 can no longer reach R1 or R2. Client A can reach the two-replica side; client B can reach only R3.

@2-3
Client A writes v2 through R1, which replicates to R2. That is two acknowledgements, so the write commits on the majority side, and client A gets OK. R3's copy has to wait for the partition to heal. The side holding two replicas keeps working as if nothing happened.

@4
Client B writes v3 through R3. R3 can collect only its own acknowledgement, one of the two the write needs.

@5
Choice C, consistency: reject the write. Client B is unavailable for as long as the partition lasts, but no replica ever holds a value that conflicts with v2.

@6
Choice A, availability, is the other branch: accept the write anyway, as a sloppy quorum with a hint for later, and reconcile when the partition heals. Client B stays available, and now the two sides hold different writes, v2 and v3.

@7
A read on the minority side faces the same choice. With two replicas required it cannot be served at all, which is C, or R3 answers alone, and the value may be stale or conflicting, which is A.

@8
The partition heals. Under the consistent choice, R3 simply catches up to v2. Under the available one, the system must reconcile v2 and v3: last-writer-wins silently drops one of them, or both are kept as siblings for the application to merge.

@9
So CAP is this choice, made for the clients that can reach only a minority while the network is split. Place the replicas so the common failures never cost a quorum: three replicas in three zones survive any single-zone loss. And PACELC adds the everyday half: even with no partition at all, waiting for a quorum costs latency that a single-replica answer would not.
