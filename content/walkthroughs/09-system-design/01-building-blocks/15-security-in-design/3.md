@0
A client, a rate limiter, and the login endpoint behind it. The limiter keeps a token bucket for one account: it holds at most 5 tokens and regains one every 12 seconds, which is five attempts a minute. Every login attempt takes a token, and an attempt that finds the bucket empty is rejected. Capacity is the burst you allow; the refill rate is the pace you sustain. A real user who mistypes a password never gets near five attempts.

@1-5
Someone fires attempts at this account in the same instant. Attempts 1 to 5 each take a token and reach the login endpoint: 4 left, 3, 2, 1, none.

@6-7
Attempts 6 and 7 find the bucket empty and are rejected with a 429 and a Retry-After hint. The attacker pays for the excess, not the login service.

@8
Attempt 8 comes 24 seconds later. Two tokens have trickled back, one every 12 seconds, so it takes one and goes through, leaving 1.

@9-10
At 36 seconds another token has arrived, so there are 2 again. Attempts 9 and 10 take them both and empty the bucket. Someone hammering one account is held to the refill rate, five a minute, however hard they push.

@11
Sizing matters in both directions. Too large a capacity lets one client dump a full bucket of attempts on the endpoint in an instant; too small rejects legitimate bursts.

@12
The bucket is cheap, two numbers per key, the token count and the last refill time, and it is what most gateways implement. But look at what it actually stops: many guesses at one account. Credential stuffing does the opposite. An attacker with a million leaked email and password pairs tries each account once, from 10,000 rented addresses, so the per-account bucket never fires, and at 100 attempts per address a per-IP limit of 10 a minute only stretches the run to 10 minutes. The defences that work are aggregate and per credential: alert on the global ratio of failed to successful logins, check passwords against known breaches, fingerprint devices, and push multi-factor or passkeys so a correct password is not enough. Key limits on account and device, and treat the IP address as one signal in a risk score.
