@0
Users, a load balancer, nine instances of the stable version, one slot for a canary, and a metrics box that compares them. Changes cause most incidents; the Google SRE book puts it at roughly 70 percent of outages. So the new version earns its traffic: a small slice goes to it, its metrics are compared with the stable version's over the same window, and only then does the slice grow. The promotion gate here: no more errors than stable, and a 99th percentile latency no worse than 1.2 times stable's.

@1
Version 2 is deployed to one instance alongside the nine stable ones. Health checks pass, but it receives no traffic yet.

@2
Stage one: 10 percent of traffic. Of the next 10 requests, 9 go to stable and 1 to the canary. If version 2 is broken, at most one user in ten sees it, and only briefly.

@3
The analysis compares the two versions on the same window: error rate, 99th percentile latency, saturation. The canary has no errors, like stable, and its 99th percentile of 120 milliseconds is 2 slower than stable's 118, well within 1.2 times, so the gate passes and the share widens.

@4
Stage two: 50 percent. Now the canary sees enough traffic to hit a code path that 10 percent never exercised, and its numbers jump: 2 of its 5 requests fail.

@5
The gate fails. In this window the canary returned 2 errors out of 5 requests, with a 99th percentile of 410 milliseconds, while stable, serving the other half, had none, at 118. Because both versions saw the same traffic at the same time, the difference belongs to the release, not to ambient noise.

@6
Automatic rollback: the balancer's weight for version 2 goes to zero, and all traffic is back on stable. The bad release reached only the canary's share of traffic, half of it here, for one analysis window, instead of everyone for as long as it took a human to notice.

@7
Version 2.1, with the fix, starts again at 10 percent: 1 canary request, 9 stable. Every promotion repeats the same gate; nothing is trusted because it passed last time.

@8
At 50 percent it is clean: no errors on either side, and 121 milliseconds against 118. Comparing like with like also means a traffic spike that slows both versions does not fail the canary.

@9
Promotion: the remaining instances roll to version 2.1, which becomes the new stable. The release took a few analysis windows longer than a blue-green flip.

@10
Three traps. With only a handful of canary requests per window there are too few samples to see a 1 percent error rate, so the gate must require a minimum count, not just a rate; the lesson's sum is that 1 percent of 1,000 requests a second for 15 minutes gives 9,000 requests, enough for a 1 percent error rate and not a hundredth of one. A canary that only gets easy traffic, cache hits or one region, passes and then fails at 100 percent. And stateful sessions need sticky routing, or one user bounces between versions.

@11
The trade: canaries limit a bad release's blast radius to the canary share and catch what only real traffic reveals. The price is slower rollouts, two versions live for hours, which demands schema and API compatibility, per-version metrics, and analysis that is statistically honest.
