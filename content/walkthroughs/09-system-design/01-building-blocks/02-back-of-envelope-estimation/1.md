@0
One read request, as five boxes: a client, a load balancer, a service, Redis, and Postgres. As the request travels, the table under the diagram puts a cost on every hop and keeps a running server-side total. The one fact to hold onto: the round trip dominates almost every request, and the database is rarely the slowest hop. The geography is.

@1
The first cost is the client's own round trip to the region: about 20 milliseconds from the same city, 70 to 90 across the Atlantic, up to 200 from Australia. Nothing on the server side can remove it.

@2
The balancer forwards to the service: one hop inside an availability zone, about half a millisecond.

@3-4
The service asks Redis, another half millisecond, nearly all of it network: Redis spends around a microsecond executing a GET. This time it is a miss, so the request goes on to the database.

@5-6
The cache miss reaches Postgres. The lesson measured a primary-key lookup at 0.13 milliseconds on a warm buffer pool; add the round trip and budget about a millisecond. The row comes back, and the running total is about 2 milliseconds.

@7
The response goes back. Server-side, the whole request cost about 2 milliseconds, plus serialisation. The client still waits its 20 to 200 millisecond round trip on top.

@8
So the distance to the user is the slowest hop, not the database. Five sequential same-zone calls cost 1 to 2 milliseconds, while one call across regions costs as much as a hundred of them, which is why multi-region designs replicate data instead of calling across an ocean on the hot path. And when you budget a path, use each hop's 99th percentile, not its mean: five calls that are each slow 1 percent of the time make about 5 percent of requests slow.
