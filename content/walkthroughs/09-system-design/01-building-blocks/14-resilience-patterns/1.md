@0
One client, 999 other clients behind it, and a service that is overloaded. The client will retry, and the schedule is exponential backoff: after each failure, wait twice as long as last time, 100 milliseconds, then 200, then 400. One more ingredient matters more than the doubling. Jitter randomises each wait, so clients that failed together do not retry together.

@1
Attempt 1 fails with a 503: the service is overloaded. A 503 or a timeout is worth retrying. A 400 or a 404 is not; the request is wrong, and retrying it only adds load.

@2
The schedule says wait 100 milliseconds. Full jitter picks a random value anywhere between zero and 100, here 24. So the next attempt goes at 24 milliseconds.

@3-8
Attempt 2 fails. The schedule now says 200, and jitter picks 183, so attempt 3 goes at 207 milliseconds. It fails too. The schedule says 400, jitter picks 245, and attempt 4 goes at 452. Another 503. The schedule says 800, jitter picks 742, and the last attempt is set for 1,194 milliseconds.

@9
Attempt 5 succeeds, after 1,194 milliseconds of waiting in total. Had every retry fired immediately, all five would have landed inside the same overload window and failed.

@10
Now the reason jitter is in the picture. Suppose 1,000 clients failed at the same instant and every one sleeps exactly 100 milliseconds. They retry in lockstep, and the service, which had just begun to recover, is hit by the same wave again, now synchronised. The retries turned an outage into a longer one.

@11
With full jitter, the same 1,000 retries arrive spread across the whole interval, and the service sees a gentle ramp it can actually serve. Jitter is not optional. It is the part that makes backoff work at scale.

@12
Two more rules. Cap retries with a budget, retries as a percentage of traffic, so a full outage cannot multiply load by the number of attempts. And only retry what is idempotent; otherwise a charge that timed out but succeeded is charged again.

@13
The lesson adds the third bound: the deadline. Retries live in exactly one layer of the call chain, and no attempt should start once the caller's remaining time is shorter than the call's expected latency. Measure this trace against the lesson's one second client budget: 1.2 seconds of waiting alone would blow it, so a real caller stops earlier and degrades. Backoff bounds the load, jitter spreads it, and a budget, a deadline and a circuit breaker stop it when the failure is not transient.
