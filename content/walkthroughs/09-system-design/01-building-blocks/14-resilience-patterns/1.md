@0
One client, 999 other clients behind it, and a service that is overloaded. The client retries with exponential backoff: after each failure, wait twice as long as last time, 100 milliseconds, then 200, then 400, with jitter randomising each wait so that clients that failed together do not retry together. And every attempt lives inside the caller's deadline of one second. That is the rule to hold onto: an attempt that cannot finish before the deadline is never started.

@1
Attempt 1 starts at zero and fails with a 503 after 120 milliseconds, the per-attempt timeout. A 503 or a timeout is worth retrying. A 400 or a 404 is not; the request is wrong, and retrying it only adds load.

@2
The schedule says wait 100 milliseconds. Full jitter picks a random value anywhere between zero and 100, here 24. So the next attempt starts at 144 milliseconds, leaving 856 of the deadline.

@3-6
Attempt 2 fails at the same point. The schedule now says 200, jitter picks 183, and attempt 3 starts at 447 milliseconds, with 553 left. It fails too. The schedule says 400, jitter picks 245, and attempt 4 starts at 812, with only 188 milliseconds of the deadline remaining.

@7
Attempt 4 still fits, because 188 milliseconds is more than the 120 an attempt needs. It fails as well, at 932 milliseconds.

@8
Here the deadline decides. The next wait would be 742 milliseconds, and attempt 5 would then need another 120: it could not finish before 1,794 milliseconds, far past the one second deadline. So the client does not sleep and does not send it. It gives up at once, at 932 milliseconds, with a deadline exceeded error, instead of sending an attempt whose answer nobody would be waiting for. Retrying past the caller's deadline is only extra load on a dependency that is already struggling.

@9
Now the reason jitter is in the picture. Suppose 1,000 clients failed at the same instant and every one sleeps exactly 100 milliseconds. They retry in lockstep, and the service, which had just begun to recover, is hit by the same wave again, now synchronised. The retries turned an outage into a longer one.

@10
With full jitter, the same 1,000 retries arrive spread across the whole interval, and the service sees a gentle ramp it can actually serve. Jitter is the part that makes backoff work at scale.

@11
Two more rules. Cap retries with a budget, retries as a percentage of traffic, so a full outage cannot multiply load by the number of attempts. And only retry what is idempotent; otherwise a charge that timed out but succeeded happens twice.

@12
So there are three bounds. Backoff bounds the load, jitter spreads it, and the deadline caps the total time, with retries in exactly one layer of the call chain. In the lesson's budget, checkout makes two attempts at pricing, 120 milliseconds each, inside the client's one second. A budget and a circuit breaker stop retrying altogether when the failure is not transient.
