@0
Checkout on the left, a payments API on the right, and a circuit breaker between them. The breaker exists because a timeout still spends the timeout. It has three states. Closed passes calls and counts failures. After 3 consecutive failures it opens and fails every call instantly. After 5 seconds it goes half-open and lets one trial call decide.

@1
Normal operation. The breaker is closed, call 1 returns 200 in 40 milliseconds, and a success resets the failure count.

@2
The payments API starts timing out. Call 2 holds a checkout thread for the full 2 second timeout before failing. Failure 1 of 3.

@3
Failure 2 of 3. Each slow failure has cost the caller a thread for 2 seconds, and under load that is how one slow dependency exhausts a whole thread pool. Slow is worse than dead.

@4
Call 4 times out as well. Failure 3 of 3: the threshold is reached and the breaker trips open.

@5
Call 5 is rejected in under a millisecond with a 503 from the breaker itself, without touching the dependency. A slow failure has become a fast one, and the checkout thread is free again.

@6
Over the next 5 seconds the breaker sheds calls 6 to 20, 15 more, the same way. The payments API gets quiet time to recover instead of a queue of retries, and checkout can serve a fallback: retry later, a cached quote, or payment pending.

@7
The 5 second open period ends. The breaker moves to half-open and lets exactly one trial call through to probe the dependency.

@8
The trial, call 21, times out; payments is still sick. Back to open for another 5 seconds. Many implementations back this period off exponentially.

@9
Meanwhile the dependency recovers. Five more seconds pass, and the breaker goes half-open again.

@10
This trial, call 22, returns 200 in 45 milliseconds. The breaker closes and resets its count, and traffic flows normally. The whole episode cost two probe calls instead of 15 more two-second timeouts.

@11
Three ways breakers go wrong. Counting a raw run of failures trips too easily on a flaky call; use a failure rate over a sliding window with a minimum number of calls, which the lesson's trace sets at 4, so one failure out of one call is never a 100 percent failure rate. One breaker shared by unrelated endpoints lets a broken refund endpoint take down charges. And a breaker without a fallback still fails the user, just faster.

@12
The trade: the breaker sacrifices the few calls that would have succeeded while it was open, and in exchange frees the caller's threads and gives the dependency room to recover. Count slow calls as failures too, key a breaker per dependency, and decide the fallback before the outage.
