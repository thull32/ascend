@0
Clients on the left, an edge that admits requests, a queue with room for 8, and a worker that serves 2 per tick. The rule is the whole pattern: a request that finds the queue full is rejected at once, with a clear signal, instead of being left to wait.

@1
A burst of 20 requests arrives at the same moment. The first 8 fit and are queued, and the queue reads full, 8 of 8. The other 12 are rejected in microseconds with a 503 and a Retry-After header. Nothing is dropped silently: every one of those 12 callers knows straight away.

@2
One tick passes. The worker serves 2, and 6 remain. The table shows how long each slot waits: the request at the back of a full queue waits 4 ticks, and that is the worst delay any admitted request can ever see.

@3
Five more arrive. 2 fit into the free slots, and the other 3 are rejected immediately. The queue never grows past 8, so the delay of anything admitted stays bounded.

@4
Compare an unbounded queue. It would have accepted all 20 of the first burst, and the last would wait 10 ticks. If callers time out sooner, the worker spends those ticks serving requests nobody is still waiting for, and their retries add more. Size the queue from the delay you will tolerate, not from memory: at a thousand requests a second, 100 slots add at most 100 milliseconds.

@5
The rejected callers back off and retry after the Retry-After interval, or degrade. Failing fast is the point: the overload is visible at the edge, where something with a user in front of it can decide what to do, instead of as latency buried in the middle.

@6
The trade-off: a small bounded queue sheds some requests during a spike, and in exchange keeps latency and memory bounded and gives honest overload signals. A large queue accepts more, and serves them too late to matter. Small queues fail fast; that is the point.
