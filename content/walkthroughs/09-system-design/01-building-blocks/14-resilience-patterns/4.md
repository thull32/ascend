@0
A producer, a buffer with 8 slots, and a consumer that handles 2 messages per tick. Backpressure is the consumer's ability to slow the producer down. Without it, a fast producer and a slow consumer are separated only by a buffer, and every buffer is finite.

@1
The producer pushes a burst of 20 messages at once. The buffer keeps 8. The other 12 have nowhere to go, so they are dropped. Had the buffer been unbounded, the process would instead grow until it was killed for running out of memory, holding requests whose clients gave up long ago.

@2
The consumer drains 2, its real capacity. The 12 dropped messages are gone, and nobody upstream was told: the producer thinks it delivered 20.

@3
The fix flips the direction of control. The consumer tells the producer how many messages it may send, as credits or a window, and the producer may not exceed that number. Sending becomes pull-driven.

@4
The consumer looks at its free space, 2 slots, and grants exactly 2 credits.

@5
The producer sends 2 and stops. With zero credits it must wait, so the buffer cannot overflow, by construction.

@6
Each tick the consumer processes 2 and hands back 2 credits. In steady state the producer runs at exactly the consumer's rate, and the buffer only absorbs jitter.

@7
The pressure travels upstream. A blocked producer stops reading from its own source, whose handlers slow down, whose clients see latency or 429 responses. That is the point: the slowest stage sets the pace, and overload becomes visible at the edge, where something with a user in front of it can decide what to do, instead of as silent loss in the middle.

@8
Backpressure that reaches a user as a hung request is not always acceptable. For metrics and telemetry, dropping is the right policy; for orders it is not. And beware cycles: a producer waiting on a consumer that is waiting on the producer is a deadlock.

@9
The trade: backpressure guarantees bounded memory and honest overload signals, at the cost of tying the producer's speed to the consumer's. Per kind of data, choose between slowing the source, buffering to durable storage such as a log, and shedding. Then size the bound from the delay you will tolerate. At 1,000 requests a second, 100 slots add at most 100 milliseconds of waiting; 100,000 slots add 100 seconds, and nothing that has waited 100 seconds is still wanted. Small queues fail fast, and that is the point.
