@0
A stateless tier behind a load balancer: three replicas, all sharing one Redis and one Postgres. Each replica holds a connection pool of 10, a common default. Postgres allows 100 connections. The idea to hold onto: the balancer divides the requests, but everything the replicas share receives the sum.

@1
Peak traffic arrives at the balancer: 2,000 requests a second.

@2
The balancer spreads it evenly, about 667 requests a second per replica. This part scales: add a replica and each one's share shrinks.

@3
But every replica opens its own pool to the same database: 3 times 10 is 30 connections, and every cache miss and every write from all three lands on one Postgres.

@4
The cache sees the sum too: each replica's reads, and the refills after its misses.

@5
Scale the tier to 10 replicas for headroom. Each now serves 200 requests a second, but the database is asked for 10 times 10, 100 connections: its whole limit.

@6
At 30 replicas the pools want 300 connections. Postgres runs a process per connection and refuses connections past its limit, so new connections fail even though every replica is nearly idle. The stateless tier scaled; the shared component took the sum.

@7
The work itself needs only about 10 queries in flight: 2,000 requests a second, times 5 milliseconds each. So the fixes bound the sum. Size pools from that arithmetic, multiplex through PgBouncer, cut reads with the cache and read replicas, scale the primary up, and only then partition.

@8
The load balancer distributes requests; nothing distributes the database. Every replica you add brings its connections, misses and writes to the same shared components, which is exactly where horizontal scaling stops.
