@0
An app server, a cache, and a database, both holding price 7 at 10. In write-through, every write goes to the cache, and the cache writes it to the database synchronously before acknowledging. The two stores move together, at least while one writer owns the key.

@1-2
The app sets the price to 12, and the write enters through the cache, the front door of the write path. The cache marks 12 as pending, forwards the update to the database, and waits. That synchronous hop is what makes it through.

@3-4
The database commits, and only then is the app acknowledged. Any read from now on sees 12. The cost is on screen: write latency is the cache write plus the database write, about 6 milliseconds instead of about 1.

@5
A read hits the cache and gets 12. There is no invalidation step to forget, and a read right after a write through the cache always hits.

@6-8
Now the database goes down, and a write of 13 arrives. The cache cannot complete the synchronous write, so the write fails, and the cache keeps 12. Writes are exactly as available as the database, never more: consistency is kept at the cost of availability.

@9
The trade-off: agreement between cache and database and simple reads, against slower writes and a cache filled with keys nobody reads. Two holes remain, and the lesson traces both. With two writers and no per-key ordering, writer A can reach the cache first and the database last, leaving the cache at 12 and the database at 11, with no error anywhere. And writes that bypass the cache, such as a batch job, are invisible to it until the time to live. Compare write-behind, which trades durability for write speed.
