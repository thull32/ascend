viz: A breaker turns slow failure into fast failure

@0
A checkout service calling a payments API through a circuit breaker. The breaker has three states. Closed passes calls through and counts failures. After 3 failures in a row it opens, and fails every call instantly. After 5 seconds it goes half-open, and lets one trial call decide what happens next.

@1
Normal operation. The breaker is closed and forwards the call, and a success resets the failure count.

@2
The payments API starts timing out. Call 2 holds a checkout thread for the full 2 second timeout before failing. Failure 1 of 3.

@3
Failure 2 of 3. Every slow failure so far has cost the caller a thread and 2 seconds. Under load, this is exactly how one slow dependency exhausts a whole thread pool.

@4
Failure 3 of 3. The threshold is reached, and the breaker trips open.

@5
While open, the breaker rejects call 5 in under a millisecond, without touching the dependency. A slow failure has become a fast one, and the checkout thread is free again.

@6
Over the next 5 seconds the breaker sheds calls 6 to 20, 15 more, the same way. The dependency gets quiet time to recover instead of a queue of retries, and the caller can serve a fallback: retry later, a cached quote, or payment pending.

@7-8
The 5 seconds elapse, the breaker goes half-open, and lets exactly one trial call through, call 21. It fails: the dependency is still sick. Back to open for another 5 seconds, and many implementations back this timeout off exponentially.

@9-10
The dependency recovers. After another 5 seconds the breaker goes half-open again, the trial, call 22, succeeds, and the breaker closes and resets its count. The whole outage cost two probe calls, one failed and one successful, instead of 15 more waits for a timeout.

@11
The traps. A raw count lets a few flaky calls trip it too often, so use a failure rate over a sliding window with a minimum volume. One breaker shared across unrelated endpoints lets a broken refund endpoint take down charging. And a breaker without a fallback still fails the user, only faster.

@12
The trade-off. The breaker sacrifices the few calls that would have succeeded while it was open, in exchange for freeing the caller's resources and giving the dependency room to recover. It makes failure explicit and fast, which is what lets the rest of the system stay up.
