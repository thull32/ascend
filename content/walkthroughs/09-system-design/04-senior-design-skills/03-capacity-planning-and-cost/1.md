@0
A user in Tokyo, an edge server of a CDN in Tokyo, and the origin in Virginia, across the Pacific. The usual story about a CDN is latency, and you will see that story. But listen for the other one: every byte the origin sends to the internet is a line on the bill, and in the lesson's running example, internet egress was about a fifth of it.

@1
User A asks for a video segment. DNS and anycast have sent the request to the nearest edge, in Tokyo.

@2
The edge does not have it. A cache miss, so the edge fetches it from the origin, across the Pacific, a round trip of about 150 milliseconds.

@3
The origin responds, and its Cache-Control header tells the edge it may keep the segment for a day. This response is the expensive one: those bytes leave the origin and are billed as origin egress.

@4
User A gets the bytes after about 160 milliseconds, and the edge now holds a cached copy.

@5-6
User B, also in Tokyo, asks for the same segment. This time it is a hit, served from the edge in about 5 milliseconds, and the origin never sees the request. No ocean crossing, and no origin egress.

@7
On the bill the two paths differ too. The miss was origin egress across the ocean, metered by the cloud provider; the hit was edge bytes at CDN prices, and the origin sent nothing. Here is the takeaway for a capacity review. For a product that moves a lot of bytes, the CDN hit ratio is a cost line, not just a latency number: each point of hit ratio is bytes the origin never sends. The dominant costs in most bills are bytes moved, so the design should minimise bytes moved, and you control that through bytes per request and the hit ratio.
