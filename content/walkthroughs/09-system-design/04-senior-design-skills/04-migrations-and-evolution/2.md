@0
An app, a Postgres database, a connector, and a search index that needs the same data. Instead of the app writing to both, a dual write, the connector tails the database's write-ahead log and turns every committed change into an event. One idea carries this whole picture: the database's own commit order becomes the event stream.

@1
Setup. The connector creates a replication slot, so Postgres keeps the log from that point on, and takes a consistent snapshot of the existing rows, streaming them as synthetic inserts. From then on it needs only the log.

@2-3
The app commits an insert on users. The database writes it to the log at position 1 before acknowledging, and the app knows nothing about the search index. The connector decodes position 1 into a change event, with the row's before and after images, and the sink applies it as an upsert. The gap between commit and apply is the staleness a search user can see.

@4-7
The same happens for an update on users at position 2, and an insert on orders at position 3. Each change reaches the index in exactly the order the database committed it.

@8-9
Then a delete on users, at position 4, which the sink applies as a delete by primary key. Deletes are a change polling would miss, and the log catches them.

@10
The first failure mode. The connector crashes after emitting position 4, but before confirming it to the slot. On restart it resumes from the last confirmed position and emits 4 again. Delivery is at least once, so the sink must apply events idempotently: upsert by key, or deduplicate by log position.

@11
The second. The replication slot pins the log. If the connector is down for days, Postgres keeps every log segment since the confirmed position, and the primary's disk fills. Monitor slot lag, and set a retention cap.

@12
And why not just dual-write? If the app wrote to Postgres and to the index itself, a crash between the two loses an update, and two concurrent writers can apply their updates to the index in opposite orders. Tailing one log makes the database the single arbiter of order.

@13
The trade-off. Change data capture gives you exactly the database's order, with no application changes, and catches deletes. But it couples you to the schema, so a renamed column breaks consumers unless a schema registry mediates. It delivers at least once. And it adds operational load: slots, lag, and connector restarts.
