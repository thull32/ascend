@0
An app, the old store, which here is Postgres, a connector, and the new store that needs the same data. Instead of the app writing to both, a dual write, the connector tails the database's write-ahead log and turns every committed change into an event. One idea carries this whole picture: the old store's own commit order becomes the event stream.

@1
Setup. The connector creates a replication slot, so Postgres keeps the log from that point on, and takes a consistent snapshot of the existing rows, streaming them as synthetic inserts. From then on it needs only the log.

@2-3
The app commits an insert on users. The database writes it to the log at position 1 before acknowledging, and the app knows nothing about the new store. The connector decodes position 1 into a change event, with the row's before and after images, the new store applies it as an upsert keyed by primary key, and the connector confirms position 1 to the slot. The gap between commit and apply is the staleness a reader of the new store can see.

@4-7
The same happens for an update on users at position 2, and an insert on orders at position 3. Each change reaches the new store in exactly the order the old store committed it, and each is confirmed.

@8-9
Then a delete on users, at position 4, which the new store applies as a delete by primary key. Deletes are a change polling would miss, and the log catches them. This one has been emitted but not yet confirmed: the slot still says 3.

@10
The first failure mode. The connector crashes right there. On restart it resumes after the last confirmed position, 3, emits position 4 again, and this time confirms it. A failure delayed the change rather than losing it, but the new store saw it twice. Delivery is at least once, so the new store must apply events idempotently: upsert by key, or deduplicate by log position.

@11
The second. The replication slot pins the log. If the connector is down for days, Postgres keeps every log segment since the confirmed position, and the primary's disk fills. Monitor slot lag, and set a retention cap.

@12
And why not just dual-write? If the app wrote to Postgres and to the new store itself, a crash between the two loses an update, and two concurrent writers can apply their updates to the new store in opposite orders. Tailing one log makes the old store the single arbiter of order.

@13
The trade-off. Change data capture gives you exactly the database's order, with no application changes, and catches deletes. But it couples you to the schema, so a renamed column breaks consumers unless a schema registry mediates. It delivers at least once. And it adds operational load: slots, lag, and connector restarts.
