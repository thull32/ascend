@0
Two clients that both want a lock, a lock service, and the storage they write to, which holds version 1 of a file. Because a client can die while holding a lock, the lock is a lease that expires after 10 seconds. That expiry is where the trouble starts.

@1-2
A asks for the lock and gets it, with token 33. It must finish or renew within 10 seconds.

@3-4
B asks too, and is denied while A holds it. So far mutual exclusion works.

@5
Now A stops for 15 seconds: a stop-the-world garbage collection, a VM migration, a slow network. A is not dead, only late, and it cannot tell.

@6
The lease expires. The lock service cannot tell a paused client from a dead one, and releasing is the only way not to block forever.

@7-8
B acquires the lock with the next token, 34, and writes version 2. That write is legitimate: B holds the lock.

@9-10
A wakes up. From its point of view nothing happened: it holds a lock and is about to write. It writes, and storage accepts it on top of B's work. Two clients wrote under one lock, and the file is corrupted. No choice of time to live fixes this, because a pause can always be longer.

@11-13
The fix is fencing. Every grant carries a strictly increasing token, every write carries it, and storage remembers the highest token it has seen, here 34. Replay A's late write: it carries the stale token 33, storage sees 33 is lower than 34, and rejects it. The safety check lives on the resource, the only party that can enforce it.

@14
So ask what a lock is for. To avoid duplicate work, a simple lease is fine. For correctness, you need a fence on every resource. In the editor, the fence is the op log itself. Each document has one owner holding a lease, and the owner appends version N plus 1 only if no N plus 1 exists. A paused owner that wakes after a new owner has written that version finds its append rejected, and steps down. The log position is the fencing token.
