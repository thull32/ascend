@0
Users, a load balancer, nine healthy instances of the stable version 1, which serve as the baseline, one slot for a canary, and a metrics service that compares them. A canary release sends a small slice of real traffic to the new version, compares it against stable on the same window, and only then widens the slice. Like the bird in the coal mine, it fails first, and few are exposed.

@1
Version 2 is deployed to one instance alongside the nine. It gets no traffic yet, and its health checks pass.

@2-3
Stage 1: 10 percent of traffic goes to the canary, 1 of the last 10 requests. The analysis compares error rate and 99th percentile latency on the same window: no errors on either side, and 120 milliseconds against stable's 118, within the gate's 1.2 times, so the gate passes and the share widens.

@4-5
Stage 2: 50 percent. Now the canary sees enough traffic to hit a code path that 10 percent never exercised. 2 of its 5 requests fail, where stable's 5 have none, and its 99th percentile jumps to 410 milliseconds. The gate fails. Because stable is serving the other half at the same moment, the comparison isolates the release from any ambient noise.

@6
Automatic rollback: the canary's weight goes to zero. The bad release reached only the canary's share of traffic for one analysis window, instead of everyone for as long as it took a human to notice.

@7-8
Version 2.1, with the fix, starts again from 10 percent. Every promotion repeats the same gate; nothing is trusted because it passed last time. At 50 percent it is clean: no errors, and 121 milliseconds against 118. Comparing like with like means a traffic spike that slows both versions does not fail the canary.

@9
Promotion: the remaining instances roll to 2.1, and it becomes the new stable version.

@10
The traps. With a handful of canary requests per window there may be too few samples to see a 1 percent error rate, so the gate needs a minimum count. A canary that only gets easy traffic passes and then fails at 100 percent. And sticky sessions are needed, or one user bounces between versions.

@11
Canaries cost slower rollouts and two versions live for hours. At Netflix the guard is wider still: a bad global change degrades every region at once, and evacuation cannot help. So changes roll out region by region, with bake time and automated canary analysis between them.
