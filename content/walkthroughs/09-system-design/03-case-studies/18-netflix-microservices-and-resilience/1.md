@0
Three services, orders, payments and inventory, each with a sidecar proxy beside it, and a control plane above. The sidecar intercepts all of the service's traffic in and out. At Netflix, discovery, load balancing, retries and timeouts once lived in Java libraries linked into every service: Eureka's client, Ribbon, Hystrix. A mesh moves the same work out of the application, for any language.

@1
The control plane pushes routing rules, retry and timeout policy, and short-lived certificates to every sidecar. The policy on screen: mutual TLS required, certificates rotated hourly; orders may call payments with a 500 millisecond timeout and 2 retries; and eject an endpoint after 5 server errors in a row. The application containers never see any of it.

@2-3
Orders calls payments as if it were a plain HTTP call. The connection is redirected into orders' own sidecar, which picks a healthy payments endpoint and opens a mutually authenticated TLS connection to payments' sidecar. Each side verifies the other's identity from its certificate, not its IP address.

@4-5
Payments' sidecar checks that orders is allowed to call it, and passes the request to the application. The answer, 200 in 32 milliseconds, comes back through both sidecars, and each records latency, status and a trace span. The two extra proxy traversals cost on the order of a millisecond or less.

@6-7
Now payments starts failing. Orders' sidecar applies the retry policy, within budget and under the timeout, with no change to orders' code; it is safe because the call is an idempotent GET. After 5 consecutive errors it ejects that payments endpoint from its pool, a per-endpoint circuit breaker, and fails fast with a 503. Once payments recovers, the endpoint is let back in after a cool-down.

@8
Every sidecar reports the same metrics and traces, so the mesh gives you a service graph and per-edge golden signals for free, in the same shape for every language.

@9
Traffic shaping becomes configuration: the control plane tells orders' sidecar to send 10 percent of payments calls to a version 2 subset. Canaries, header-based routing and fault injection need no code.

@10
The costs. The control plane is a new critical dependency: if it goes down, sidecars keep their last config, but new pods cannot join and certificates stop rotating. Every pod pays a proxy's memory and up to about a millisecond per call, and debugging now spans app, sidecar and mesh config.

@11
So a mesh is worth it at dozens of services in several languages, and overkill for a handful, where a shared library would do. That is the arc in the lesson: once a mechanism stabilises, move it down the stack. The patterns are unchanged; only where they run has moved.
