@0
On screen: the application being monitored, the agent beside it, the ingest gateway, and under the agent a spool on local disk with room for 4 batches. The agent batches metrics and logs and pushes each batch to the gateway. Hold onto one rule, because it is the whole design: when the gateway answers 429, the agent neither blocks the application nor throws the batch away. It writes the batch to the spool and tries again later, and if the spool fills, the oldest telemetry goes first.

@1
Batch 1 is pushed and accepted with a 204. This is normal operation: the spool is empty, and the application never notices the agent at all.

@2
Then the tenant goes over its ingest limit. The gateway refuses batch 2 with a 429 and a Retry-After. That is backpressure, not an error. The agent writes batch 2 to the spool, one of 4 slots, and backs off. The application keeps running and is never made to wait.

@3-5
While the agent is still backing off, batches 3, 4 and 5 become ready. They do not hit the gateway at all; each goes straight to the spool, and the spool fills: 2 of 4, 3 of 4, then 4 of 4.

@6
Batch 6 arrives and the spool is full. For telemetry the right policy is to drop the oldest, so batch 2 is discarded and counted, and batch 6 takes its place. Disk use stays bounded, and the newest data, the most useful during an incident, survives.

@7
Retry-After has passed and the gateway accepts again. The agent drains the spool oldest first: batches 3 and 4 are accepted.

@8
The rest of the spool, batches 5 and 6, goes out, followed by the new batch 7. The spool is empty again. Only batch 2 was lost, and the agent counted the drop.

@9
Batch 8 is pushed directly, just as before the refusal. Seven of eight batches arrived: the refused ones late rather than lost, except batch 2.

@10
The lesson's edge case lives here. Replayed batches are older than live data, and an ingester that accepts samples only up to a fixed window older than its newest rejects the oldest replayed ones as out of order. So size that window to the spool, or accept the gap and count the rejections per host, which also exposes broken clocks.

@11
So a 429 is the platform saying slow down, not something broken. The spool turns a refusal into delay instead of loss and never pushes the slowdown into the application. The price is local disk, stale data during a long refusal, and a bounded loss of the oldest telemetry when the refusal outlasts the spool.
