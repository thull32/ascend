@0
A Bloom filter of 16 bits, all zero, with 3 hash functions, sitting between the querier and the chunk store. It answers one question, have I seen this key, with definitely not or probably yes. Never definitely yes, and it never forgets a key it was given.

@1-2
Add trace-4bf9: its three hashes are 0, 5 and 14, so those bits are set. Then trace-a3c1 sets 15, 6 and 13.

@3-4
Then trace-77e0 sets 4, 1 and 10. trace-19d2 hashes to 11, 6 and 13, and two of those are already set, so only bit 11 is new. Four keys, 10 of 16 bits set, and the estimated false-positive rate has climbed to about 15 percent.

@5-7
Query trace-4bf9: bits 0, 5 and 14 are all 1, so it is probably present. The filter cannot miss a key it holds. Only now does the querier pay for a fetch from the chunk store, and it finds the trace.

@8-9
Query trace-118e: bits 6, 11 and 12. Bit 12 is 0, so this ID was never added: definitely absent. The fetch is skipped entirely. That is the win: most lookups for traces that are not in a chunk cost no fetch at all.

@10-11
Query trace-7560, which was never added either: bits 5, 4 and 11. Every one of them happens to have been set by the four IDs that were added. A false positive: the querier fetches the chunk and finds nothing. Harmless for correctness, but it is the cost you tune away.

@12
Two limits. You cannot delete, because clearing a bit might erase another key's evidence and create a false negative. And the rate climbs as the filter fills, so size it for the final key count: about 10 bits per key with 7 hash functions gives roughly 1 percent.

@13
In the logging platform, each compressed chunk of log lines carries a filter over its trace IDs. A chunk of about 5,000 IDs needs about 6 kilobytes for 1 percent, under 1 percent of the chunk. A no is always right, so the querier skips that chunk; a maybe costs one fetch that is occasionally wasted. Over a whole day that still means reading many filters and some wasted fetches, but it beats scanning every log line by two orders of magnitude.
