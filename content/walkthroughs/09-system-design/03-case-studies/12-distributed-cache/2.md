@0
Nodes and keys are hashed onto the same circle, and a key belongs to the first node clockwise from it. Look at where the four nodes landed: N4 at 159 degrees and N2 at 161, then N3 at 342 and N1 at 344. With one point per node, they came out in two tight pairs.

@1-2
User 1 hashes to 187 degrees. Walk clockwise and the first node is N3, at 342. User 7, at 181 degrees, also belongs to N3.

@3
Video 9 lands at 345, just past N1. Nothing lies clockwise before the top of the circle, so the walk wraps round to N4, at 159.

@4-6
Session 3 at 164, cart 5 at 294 and feed 2 at 165 all land in the long gap between N2 and N3, so all three go to N3. Five of the six keys sit on one node, and N1 and N2 own nothing at all.

@7
Now add N5, at 340 degrees. Only the keys between its predecessor, N2, and N5 change owner. That is the promise of the ring: with hash mod N, nearly every key would move. But here the arc it takes over is most of the circle, so 5 of the 6 keys move. On a ring with one point per node, arcs are wildly uneven, and that is the real lesson of this picture.

@8
Virtual nodes fix it: each physical node gets 100 to 200 points on the ring, so the arcs even out and a dead node's keys spread across many neighbours. The lesson measured it with 130 nodes. With one point each, the busiest node carried almost 6 times the average load. With 160 points each, 1.2 times, and adding a 131st node moved under 1 percent of the keys, close to the ideal of one in 131. This is the scheme Dynamo, Cassandra and Memcached clients use.
