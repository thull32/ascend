@0
Nodes and keys share one circle of positions, and the rule is a single sentence: a key belongs to the first node you reach walking clockwise from it, wrapping past the top. Four cache nodes sit a quarter of the circle apart: N1 at 55 degrees, N2 at 145, N3 at 235 and N4 at 325.

@1-2
User 1 hashes to 159 degrees. Walking clockwise, the first node is N3, at 235, so N3 owns it. User 7 lands at 124, just before N2 at 145, so it belongs to N2.

@3-4
Video 9 lands at 35 degrees and walks to N1, at 55. Session 3, at 73, has passed N1 already, so it walks on to N2.

@5-6
Cart 5 sits at 322, just short of N4 at 325, so N4 owns it. Feed 2, at 175, joins user 1 on N3.

@7
All six keys are placed: one on N1, two on N2, two on N3 and one on N4. Each node owns the arc that ends at its own position.

@8
Now add a fifth node, N5, at 98 degrees, between N1 at 55 and N2 at 145. It claims only that arc. Session 3, at 73, now meets N5 before N2, so it moves from N2 to N5: one key of six. No key moves between the old nodes, so every other cache entry stays warm. With hash mod N, going from four nodes to five would have remapped five of these six keys, and the hit rate would collapse while they refilled.

@9
The ideal is that the newcomer takes its fair share, about a fifth of the keys, and nothing else moves. With one point per node, how much it takes depends on where it lands, so real rings give each node many points. The lesson measured it with 130 nodes. With one point each, the busiest node carried almost 6 times the average load. With 160 points each, 1.2 times, and adding a 131st node moved under 1 percent of the keys, close to the ideal of one in 131. This is the scheme Dynamo, Cassandra and Memcached clients use.
