@0
A client, a leader and two followers, all holding x equals 1 at log position 7. The leader accepts every write and streams its log to the followers, which apply it in order, serve reads, and stand by for failover. Replication starts in asynchronous mode.

@1-2
The client writes x equals 2. The leader appends it at log position 8, applies it, and acknowledges straight away, before any follower has the change.

@3
Then it streams position 8 to both followers. On a good day that takes a few milliseconds; under load or across regions it can take seconds.

@4-5
Meanwhile the client reads x from follower 1, which is still one entry behind, and gets 1. It just wrote 2, and its own write seems to have vanished. That is replication lag breaking read-your-writes.

@6
A few milliseconds later both followers apply position 8 and catch up. The lag window is real, but usually short.

@7
The fix is to route a recent writer's reads to the leader for a few seconds, or to send the follower the client's last log position and let it wait until it has applied it.

@8
Now synchronous mode: the leader waits for follower 1 to confirm each write before acknowledging. Usually only one follower is synchronous, so a slow one cannot stall every write.

@9-12
The client writes x equals 3, at position 9. The leader ships it and waits. Follower 1 confirms it has position 9 on disk, and only then does the client hear OK. The write now exists on two nodes, and the latency is the leader's flush plus one round trip.

@13
The leader dies. Someone, a coordinator or the nodes by consensus, must pick the most up-to-date follower, promote it, and repoint clients.

@14
Follower 1, which has position 9, is promoted, and follower 2 now follows it. With asynchronous replication, writes the old leader acknowledged but never shipped would be lost; with synchronous, they are safe. And if the old leader comes back believing it still leads, fence it first.

@15
Asynchronous is fast and tolerates slow followers but can lose acknowledged writes; synchronous is durable at the cost of latency. In the cache, the point of a replica in another zone is the failover. When a primary dies, clients read the replica, which is already warm, so the database never sees the miss storm, and the replacement is filled by bulk copy. The price is double the RAM and a cross-zone round trip of about a millisecond until the copy finishes.
