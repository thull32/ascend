@0
One cache shard, with a copy in each of two availability zones, and an application in each zone. The rule: clients write to every copy and read only the copy in their own zone. While both copies are up, a read never crosses a zone.

@1
A write goes to both copies. Each one is a full replica of the shard, holding the same keys, not a cold standby waiting to be filled.

@2-3
Zone A's application reads zone A's copy, and zone B's reads zone B's: same-zone round trips, and each zone's traffic stays in its zone.

@4
Now zone A's cache node dies. Without a replica, every key it held would miss at the same moment and fall through to the database.

@5
Instead, zone A's clients switch to zone B's copy on their first timeout. That copy is already warm, so they hit. Each of those reads pays a cross-zone round trip, about half a millisecond to a millisecond.

@6
The database never sees the spike. There is no miss storm, because the misses were absorbed by a copy that already held the keys.

@7
A replacement node in zone A is filled by bulk copy from the survivor: 69 million items, 76 gigabytes, about two and a half minutes at 500 megabytes a second. It starts serving only once the copy is done.

@8
Zone A reads locally again, and the shard is back to one warm copy per zone.

@9
The price is double the memory. The decision comes from the failure the estimates say you must survive: losing a whole zone takes out far too many nodes for a small gutter pool to absorb, so every shard keeps a warm copy in another zone.
