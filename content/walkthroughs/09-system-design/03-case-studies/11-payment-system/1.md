@0
An orchestrator on the left drives three services: orders, payments and inventory, each with its own database. Nothing spans them, so there is no shared lock and no coordinator that could commit all three at once. A saga is the answer: run the business transaction as a sequence of local transactions, each committed immediately, and if a later step fails, undo the earlier ones with compensating transactions instead of a rollback.

@1
Step 1: the order service creates a pending order and commits. The change is visible to everyone straight away. There is no isolation across a saga.

@2
Step 2: the payment service charges the card 40 dollars, and that commits too.

@3
Step 3 fails: the inventory service is down. The first two steps are already committed, and no database rollback can reach them.

@4
So the orchestrator switches to compensation. It runs the undo action for every completed step, in reverse order, and each one is itself a local transaction, retried until it succeeds.

@5
First, undo step 2 with a refund of 40 dollars. A refund is a new transaction that reverses the effect; the original charge still happened, and it stays in the ledger.

@6
Then undo step 1 by cancelling the order.

@7
The saga ends aborted. Every transition was written to a saga log before the next call, so if the orchestrator itself crashes halfway, its replacement reads the log and resumes from the last recorded step instead of leaving a half-done order behind.

@8
Three things bite. Another request can see the intermediate state, an order that is pending and then cancelled. A compensation might arrive twice, so it must be idempotent. And some actions have no undo at all, so order the steps so the irreversible one comes last.

@9
That last rule is exactly how the lesson's checkout is built. It authorises, grants the entitlement, and captures only at the end, so a failure in between is undone by voiding the authorisation and revoking the entitlement, and the customer sees a payment that was never taken rather than a charge and a refund. The price of a saga is the same everywhere: services stay independent and available, but you give up atomicity and isolation, and you design every compensation by hand.
