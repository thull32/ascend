@0
An orchestrator on the left drives two parties: the payment provider, the PSP, and the entitlements service that grants the product. The saga log underneath lists the checkout's three steps: authorise, grant the entitlement, capture. The PSP is not in our transaction, so nothing can commit all of this at once. A saga is the answer: run the checkout as a sequence of steps, each committed on its own, and if a later step fails, undo the earlier ones with compensations instead of a rollback.

@1
Step 1: the PSP authorises 15 dollars 99. The issuer reserves the funds, but nothing has moved yet. The change is visible straight away; there is no isolation across a saga.

@2
Step 2: the entitlements service grants the subscription, and that commits too.

@3
Step 3, the capture, fails. The first two steps are already committed, in two different systems, and no database rollback can reach them.

@4
So the orchestrator switches to compensation. It runs the undo action for every completed step, in reverse order, and each one is itself a step, retried until it succeeds.

@5
First, undo step 2: revoke the entitlement, so the customer does not keep a product nobody paid for.

@6
Then undo step 1: void the authorisation. Nothing was captured, so voiding just releases the hold, and the customer sees a payment that was never taken, instead of a charge and a refund days later. That is why the checkout authorises first and captures last.

@7
The saga ends aborted. Every transition was written to the saga log before the next call, so if the orchestrator itself crashes halfway, its replacement reads the log and resumes from the last recorded step instead of leaving a half-done checkout behind.

@8
Three things bite. Other requests can see the intermediate state while it lasts. A compensation might arrive twice, so it must be idempotent. And some actions have no undo at all, so order the steps so the irreversible one comes last, which is exactly what capturing at the end does.

@9
The price of a saga is the same everywhere: services stay independent and available, with no locks held across them, but you give up atomicity and isolation, and you design every compensation by hand.
