@0
The raw clicks topic, with a producer, three partitions and a consumer group of two. Each partition is an ordered, append-only log, and the producer picks a partition by hashing the message key. Here the key is the impression ID, the identity of one ad shown to one person. A double-click produces two events with the same impression ID, and the question is where they end up.

@1-2
The first imp-51 hashes to partition 2 and lands at offset 0. imp-07 also hashes to partition 2, and takes offset 1.

@3
Then imp-51 arrives again: a duplicate. Same key, same hash, same partition. It is appended at offset 2, in the same log as the first copy.

@4-6
Next, imp-93 goes to partition 0. Then the second imp-07 arrives and lands in partition 2, right with its first copy, at offset 3. imp-22 joins imp-93 in partition 0. Partition 1 happens to get nothing in this small sample.

@7
The coordinator assigns each partition to exactly one consumer: consumer 1 owns partitions 0 and 2, consumer 2 owns partition 1. Because both copies of every duplicate sit in one partition, one consumer sees both, and a local check of the impression IDs it has seen catches the second. No cross-machine lookup is needed.

@8-9
Consumer 1 polls partition 0, reads offsets 0 and 1 in order, and commits offset 2 after processing. Committing after processing gives at-least-once: a crash in between replays those clicks, which is one more reason the dedupe check matters.

@10
If consumer 2 dies, the group rebalances after a timeout and consumer 1 takes over partition 1 from its last committed offset. The group pauses during the rebalance, and anything processed but not committed is delivered again.

@11-12
Three partitions cap the group at three useful consumers, and a hot key loads one partition alone. Ordering holds within a partition only. That is why the stream job dedupes by impression ID first, then re-keys the clicks by ad and counts them in windows on event time, never on arrival order.
