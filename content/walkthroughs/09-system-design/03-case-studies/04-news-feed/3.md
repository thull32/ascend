@0
A queue between the post service and the fan-out workers. The producer enqueues and moves on; workers pull when they have capacity. That separation is what lets an author get a reply in milliseconds while fan-out to thousands of followers happens behind it.

@1
A burst of 12 messages arrives. The broker persists each one and acknowledges immediately, so the producer never waits for the fan-out.

@2-3
Worker 1 receives m1. The broker does not delete it; it hides it for a visibility timeout of 30 seconds while the worker works. Worker 2 takes m2. Two workers in parallel, and each message goes to exactly one of them.

@4
Worker 1 finishes and acknowledges, and only now is m1 deleted. Acknowledging after the work is what protects against worker crashes.

@5-6
Worker 2 crashes halfway through m2. No acknowledgement was sent, so the message is still there, merely invisible. When the timeout expires it becomes visible again, and worker 1 receives it: attempt 2. Nothing is lost, but a message can be delivered twice.

@7
The frame's example is a card charged twice. In the feed, the same thing happens to timelines: a worker that crashes after pushing a post ID, but before recording its progress, replays the page, and a push is not idempotent, so a timeline can hold an ID twice. The lesson deduplicates the 300 candidate IDs at read time, which is cheaper than a sorted set that would roughly triple memory.

@8
Worker 2 restarts and both workers drain the backlog, m3 to m11. Throughput scales by adding workers, but messages taken by different workers can complete in any order.

@9-11
m12 is a poison message: its payload makes the handler throw every time. The worker rejects it, the broker makes it visible again and counts the attempt. After 3 receives it moves m12 to the dead-letter queue instead of retrying forever, so one bad message cannot block a worker.

@12
The trade-off: decoupling and elastic workers, at the price of at-least-once delivery, no end-to-end ordering, and a backlog that can grow silently. When producers outrun consumers, feeds go stale rather than failing, so alert on the age of the oldest message, not just depth: for the feed, that age is the fan-out lag against its 5 second target.
