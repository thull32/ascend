@0
A client, a rate limiter and the API behind it. The bucket holds at most 5 tokens and gains 1 a second. Each request takes a token, and with none left it is turned away. Two numbers: capacity sets the largest burst, and the refill rate sets the sustained rate.

@1-5
Five requests arrive at time zero. Each takes a token and goes through: 4 left, 3, 2, 1, and then 0.

@6-7
Requests 6 and 7 arrive in the same second and find the bucket empty. Each is rejected with a 429 and a hint about when to retry. The client, not the API, pays for the overload.

@8
At 2 seconds, request 8 arrives. Two tokens have refilled since time zero, so it takes one, leaving 1.

@9-10
At 3 seconds one more token has arrived, so two are available. Requests 9 and 10 take them both, and the bucket is empty again. Out of 10 requests, 8 got through and 2 were turned away.

@11
The capacity is a judgment call. Too large, and a client can dump 5 times the sustained rate on the API in an instant. Too small, and legitimate bursts are rejected.

@12
In the waiting room, each token admits one queued user to the booking tier, and the refill rate is not a constant. It follows feedback: booking latency, error rate, and remaining inventory. In the traced on-sale it admitted 125 users a second while hold latency stayed healthy, reached 30,000 active users in four minutes, and then paused while every remaining seat was held. Everyone else waits cheaply at the edge, instead of hammering a database sized for thirty thousand.
