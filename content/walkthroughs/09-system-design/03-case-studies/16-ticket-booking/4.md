@0
On the left, the writers: the booking service, the hold sweeper and the support tools. Then Postgres, a change data capture connector, and the seat-map publisher. None of the writers tells the seat map anything. The connector tails the database's write-ahead log and turns every committed change into an event, so the database's own commit order becomes the stream the seat map follows.

@1
Setup: the connector creates a replication slot, so Postgres keeps the log from that point, and takes a consistent snapshot of the existing rows. From then on it needs only the log.

@2-3
The booking service holds seat A12, an update on the seats table, and commits. Postgres writes it to the log at position 1 before acknowledging; the booking service knows nothing about the seat map. The connector decodes the change, the publisher flips that seat's bit in the bitmap it publishes every second, and the connector confirms position 1. The gap between commit and flip is the staleness a fan looking at the seat map can see.

@4-5
Checkout completes and A12 goes from held to sold, at position 2. Decoded, flipped, confirmed.

@6-7
Now a writer that is not the booking service: the hold sweeper releases an expired hold on B3, back to free, at position 3. It reaches the seat map the same way, because it went through the same log.

@8-9
And a support tool frees seat C7, at position 4. The publisher flips the bit, but the connector has emitted position 4 without confirming it yet: the slot still says 3.

@10
The first failure mode: the connector crashes right there. On restart it resumes after the last confirmed position, 3, emits position 4 again, and confirms it. The publisher sees the change to C7 twice. Delivery is at-least-once, so it must apply events idempotently; setting a seat's bit to the same value twice is harmless.

@11
The second: the slot pins the log. If the connector is down for three days, Postgres keeps every segment since the confirmed position, and the primary's disk fills toward 95 percent. Monitor slot lag and cap retention.

@12-13
Why not have each writer publish seat changes itself? A crash between the two writes loses an update, two concurrent writers can apply updates in opposite orders, and a sweeper or a support tool someone wrote later can simply forget. Tailing one log makes the database the single arbiter of order, so every path that changes a seat reaches the map with no second write anyone could forget.
