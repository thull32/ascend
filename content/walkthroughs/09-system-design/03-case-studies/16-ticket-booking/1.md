@0
Two clients, a lock service and storage. The picture says file, but read it as seat 14C: the tempting design puts a lock per seat in Redis, and whoever holds the lock may sell the seat. Because a client can die while holding a lock, the lock is a lease that expires after 10 seconds, and that expiry is where the trouble starts.

@1-4
Client A asks for the lock and gets it, with token 33. Client B asks too and is told to wait. So far mutual exclusion works.

@5-6
Then A stops for 15 seconds: a garbage-collection pause, a VM migration, a slow network. A is not dead, only late, and it cannot tell. The lease expires, because the lock service cannot tell a paused client from a dead one.

@7-8
B gets the lock with token 34 and writes. That is legitimate: B holds the lock, and B has the seat.

@9-10
A wakes up believing nothing happened, still holding a lock in its own mind, and writes on top of B. Storage accepts it. Two fans now believe they own seat 14C. No choice of time to live fixes this, because a pause can always be longer.

@11-13
The fix is a fence at the storage. Every grant carries an increasing token, every write carries its token, and storage rejects anything lower than the highest it has seen. A's write with token 33 arrives after 34, and it is rejected.

@14
For efficiency, avoiding duplicate work, a simple lease is fine. For correctness it needs that fence. And once the check lives in storage anyway, the database's conditional update already is that check: set the seat to held only where it is available, or held with an expired hold, in one statement. There is no window between check and write, and no second system to keep consistent, so the Redis lock adds nothing.
