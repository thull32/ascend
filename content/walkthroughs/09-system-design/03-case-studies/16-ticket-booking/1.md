lesson: ticket-booking
viz: Why a per-seat Redis lock is not the answer
frames: 15
source: 23d45e5c4741f891
---
@0
Two fans, a lock service and the database, where seat 8 is available. The tempting design puts a lock per seat in Redis, and whoever holds the lock may sell the seat. Because a client can die while holding a lock, the lock is a lease that expires after 10 seconds, and that expiry is where the trouble starts.

@1-4
Fan A asks for the lock and gets it, with token 33. Fan B asks too and is told to wait. So far mutual exclusion works.

@5-6
Then A stops for 15 seconds: a garbage-collection pause, a page fault, a VM migration. A is not dead, only late, and it cannot tell. The lease expires, because the lock service cannot tell a paused client from a dead one.

@7-8
B gets the lock with token 34 and sells seat 8 to itself. That is legitimate: B holds the lock.

@9-10
A wakes up believing nothing happened, still holding a lock in its own mind, and writes on top of B. With no check at the database, the write lands. Two fans now believe they own seat 8. No choice of lease length fixes this, because a pause can always be longer.

@11
The fix is a check at the storage, and here the database already has one: make the write conditional, setting the seat to sold only where it is still held by the writer. That condition is a fencing token the database keeps for free.

@12-13
Replay A's late write with the condition. B's sale already changed seat 8, so A's condition no longer holds, and the update changes no rows. A learns it lost the seat, and B's sale is safe.

@14
So the Redis lock is at most an efficiency measure, keeping two fans from doing the same work. Correctness comes from the conditional update, in one statement, with no window between check and write and no second system to keep consistent. Once that check exists, the per-seat lock adds nothing.
