@0
A token bucket in front of a provider. Read each request as one call from a channel sender to a push, email or SMS provider. The bucket holds at most 5 tokens and gains 1 a second. The refill rate is the sustained rate the provider allows; the capacity is the burst allowed after a quiet period.

@1-5
Five sends arrive at once, and each takes a token: 4 left, 3, 2, 1, none. All five go to the provider.

@6-7
Sends 6 and 7 find the bucket empty and are refused. For a sender, that is the bucket doing its job: the provider's limit is enforced on your side, before the provider has to enforce it with a 429. When a provider does answer 429 anyway, that is a signal to slow down, not to retry at once.

@8-10
At 2 seconds, two tokens have refilled, and send 8 takes one. At 3 seconds there are two again, and sends 9 and 10 take them. After the burst, sends go out exactly as fast as tokens refill, which is how a backlog should drain after a provider recovers: at the bucket's rate, with retries going through the same bucket.

@11
Capacity is a choice. Too large, and a burst hits the provider all at once. Too small, and legitimate bursts wait.

@12
The whole state is two numbers, tokens and the last refill time, so the bucket can live in Redis and be shared by every sender instance using one provider credential. The design's key move is to have more than one bucket. The critical lane has its own, a reserved share of the provider's limit, so a campaign draining 28,000 messages a second can never spend the tokens a login code needs.
