@0
The picture shows publish and subscribe in its general form: a topic called orders, and three subscribers, billing, email and analytics. A publisher emits an event to a topic, and every subscriber gets its own copy. For presence, read the topic as one user's channel, and the subscribers as gateways that currently have a client with that user on screen.

@1
Each subscriber registers interest. The publisher never learns who listens, and adding a fourth needs no change to it. In presence, a client subscribes only to the roughly 20 users on its screen, and unsubscribes when they scroll away.

@2-3
One event is published, and the publisher returns as soon as the broker has it. The broker fans it out to all three subscribers. One publish became three deliveries, and only to parties that asked.

@4-6
Now analytics goes offline, and event 2 is published. The online subscribers get it at once. What happens to the missing one depends on the subscription type: an ephemeral subscription misses it forever, a durable one parks it in a backlog.

@7-8
Event 3 follows and the backlog grows to two. When analytics reconnects, the broker replays both, in order. The broker now keeps state per subscriber, which costs memory or disk.

@9
A subscriber that is permanently slow accumulates a backlog without bound, so every broker needs a retention policy. Log-based brokers avoid the per-subscriber copy by giving each subscriber an offset into one shared log.

@10
Why this shape for presence? The naive design pushes every status change to every contact: 100 million online users, 200 contacts each, changing state about every 10 minutes, is 33 million notifications a second, almost all to people not looking. Publishing once per user, to only the gateways that subscribed, makes the fan-out follow attention, not the contact list. Pair it with a 30 second debounce on offline, so a switch from Wi-Fi to cellular does not flash offline to 200 people.
