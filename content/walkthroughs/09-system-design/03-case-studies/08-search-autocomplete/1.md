@0
An empty trie. A trie stores each query as a path from the root, one character per edge, so every query that starts with net lives under the node for net. Autocomplete is two steps: walk to the prefix's node, then enumerate everything below it. Watch which of the two is expensive.

@1-4
First, seven queries go in. Net creates three nodes. Netflix shares those three and adds four. Network shares net and adds four of its own, and networth shares six nodes, all the way to networ, and adds two.

@5-7
News shares only n and e, and adds two. New adds nothing at all: its nodes already exist, so it just marks the node for new as the end of a query. Nest shares two nodes and adds two. Shared prefixes are stored once.

@8-11
Now someone types net. The walk follows the edge for n, then e, then t, one step per character. That is the cheap part: it costs the length of the prefix, however many queries the trie holds. We have reached the node for net, and every query below it starts with net.

@12
Now the enumeration, depth-first, children in sorted order. Net itself is the end of a query, so it is suggestion 1.

@13-16
Down through netf, netfl and netfli, none of which is a query, to netflix: suggestion 2. Notice the cost: four nodes visited for one suggestion.

@17-20
Back up, and down the other branch: netw, netwo, networ, and then network, suggestion 3.

@21-23
From networ, one more branch: networt, then networth, suggestion 4. The suggestions are net, netflix, network and networth, in alphabetical order because children were visited sorted, and the cost is the length of the prefix plus the size of the whole subtree.

@24
For seven words that is nothing. For real traffic it is everything: in the lesson's measured log, 11.3 percent of all queries sit under the single letter t, which at 100 million queries is 11 million to rank on one keystroke. So production tries do the enumeration once, offline, and store the top k at every node at build time. A lookup becomes a walk to the node plus a read of k entries. And there is a neat trick for building it: insert queries in descending score order, and the first k queries to pass through a node are exactly its top k.
