@0
A client retrying against an overloaded service. Read each attempt as one part upload, one 16 mebibyte chunk of the video, sent again after a failure. The rule: after each failure, wait twice as long as last time, 100, then 200, then 400 milliseconds, and randomise each wait, so clients that failed together do not retry together.

@1-2
Attempt 1 fails with a 503. A 503 or a timeout is worth retrying; a 400 or a 404 is not. The schedule says wait up to 100 milliseconds, and full jitter picks a random point in that range: here 24.

@3-4
Attempt 2 fails. Now the ceiling is 200 milliseconds, and jitter picks 183.

@5-8
Attempt 3 fails, and the client waits 245 of a possible 400. Attempt 4 fails, and it waits 742 of a possible 800. The waits grow, so a struggling service gets more and more room.

@9
Attempt 5 succeeds, after about 1.2 seconds of total waiting. Had every retry fired immediately, all five would have landed inside the same overload and failed. For the upload, only this part was resent: the parts the store already acknowledged are never uploaded again.

@10
Now the failure mode without jitter. A thousand clients that failed at the same instant all sleep exactly 100 milliseconds and retry in lockstep, and the service, just starting to recover, is hit by the same wave again. In the lesson, that is 20,000 uploading clients reconnecting after an edge outage.

@11
With full jitter, the same thousand retries arrive spread across the whole interval, and the service sees a ramp it can serve. Jitter is the part that makes backoff work at scale.

@12
Two more rules. Cap retries with a budget, a share of traffic, so a full outage cannot multiply load. And retry only idempotent operations. A complete upload call that timed out but succeeded would otherwise happen twice, unless it is keyed on the video ID, as the lesson's is. A part upload is safe to repeat: its URL is scoped to one part of one upload, and the store verifies each part's checksum.

@13
The trade-off: retries turn transient failures into successes, at the cost of latency and extra load exactly when the system can least afford it. Backoff bounds the load, jitter spreads it out, and a budget plus a circuit breaker stop it when the failure is not transient.
