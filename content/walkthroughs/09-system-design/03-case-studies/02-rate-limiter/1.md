@0
A rate limiter in front of an API, with a limit of 3 requests per 10 seconds. The sliding window log keeps the timestamp of every accepted request. When a request arrives at time t, drop every timestamp older than t minus 10 seconds, and accept only if fewer than 3 remain. That is the whole algorithm, and it is exact.

@1-3
Request 1 arrives at time 0: the window is empty, so it is accepted and 0 goes into the log. Request 2 at 2 seconds sees one entry: accepted. Request 3 at 4 seconds sees two: accepted. The log now holds 0, 2 and 4, and it is full.

@4-5
Request 4 at 7 seconds finds 3 timestamps in the window: rejected with a 429. Request 5 at 9 seconds: still 3, rejected too. The limiter can tell both clients exactly when to come back. The oldest entry, time 0, leaves the window at 10 seconds, and that is the Retry-After.

@6-8
At 11 seconds, timestamp 0 has slid out. Two remain, so request 6 is accepted. At 12 seconds, timestamp 2 drops and request 7 gets in; at 15 seconds, timestamp 4 drops and request 8 gets in. Each arrival evicts the stale entries first, then counts.

@9-10
Requests 9 and 10 arrive at 21 and 24 seconds, spaced out, and both are accepted. Eight accepted, two rejected, and at no instant did any 10 second window hold more than 3.

@11
Compare fixed windows. A counter that resets every 10 seconds would allow 3 requests at 9 seconds and 3 more at 10 seconds: 6 in one second, double the intended rate. The sliding log has no boundary to game.

@12
The price is one stored timestamp per request. Fine for 3 per 10 seconds, ruinous for 10,000 per hour. That is why the design uses the log only for very low limits, such as 10 login attempts a minute, and uses the sliding window counter, which weights the previous window's count by its overlap, for per-IP limits at scale.
