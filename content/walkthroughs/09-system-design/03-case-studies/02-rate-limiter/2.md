@0
The token bucket, the default for per-key limits. This bucket holds at most 5 tokens and gains 1 every second. Each request takes a token, and a request that finds none is rejected. Two numbers, two jobs: capacity sets the largest burst, the refill rate sets the sustained rate.

@1-5
Seven requests arrive at time 0, all at once. The first five each take a token: 4 left, 3, 2, 1, and then none. All five are forwarded to the API.

@6-7
Requests 6 and 7 find the bucket empty and get a 429 with a Retry-After hint. The client, not the API, pays for the overload.

@8
Request 8 arrives at 2 seconds. Refill is lazy: nothing ticked in the background, the limiter just computes the 2 seconds that passed and adds 2 tokens. It takes one, and 1 is left.

@9-11
At 3 seconds, one more token has refilled, so there are 2. Requests 9 and 10 take them, and request 11, in the same second, finds the bucket empty and is rejected. After the first burst, requests succeed only as fast as tokens refill.

@12
Then the client goes quiet for 6 seconds. Request 12 at 9 seconds finds the bucket full again, capped at 5, not 6, and takes one. Idle time buys back a burst, but never more than capacity.

@13
So capacity is a product decision. Too large, and a client can dump 5 times the sustained rate on the API in one instant. Too small, and you reject a dashboard that fires many calls at once. In the lesson's replay, a client averaging 80 a second under a 100 a second limit, but sending each second's requests in the first 200 milliseconds, had half its requests rejected with a capacity of 20, and none at 100 or more.

@14
The whole state is two numbers per client: tokens and the time of the last refill. That is why most API gateways implement it, and why GCRA can squeeze it into one timestamp.
