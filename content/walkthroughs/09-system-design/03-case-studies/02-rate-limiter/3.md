@0
This is affinity routing, the design the limiter grows into at 10 times the scale. Hash every limiter node onto a circle, and hash every rate-limit key onto the same circle. A key belongs to the first node clockwise from it, and its bucket lives in that node's memory, with no shared store at all.

@1
API key k 81f2 hashes to 235 degrees. Walking clockwise, the first node is N3, so N3 holds its buckets and makes every decision for it.

@2-6
The rest follow the same rule. Key k 0a9c at 91 degrees and key k 5b10 at 146 both land on N4. Key k 77de at 229 degrees, an IP prefix at 292, and another IP prefix at 211 all walk clockwise to N3. One rule, no coordination: any gateway can compute the owner of any key.

@7
Now add N5 at 340 degrees. Only the keys between its predecessor and N5 change owner. Here that is 4 of the 6, because four nodes placed at random are badly spread, and N5 lands just in front of N3. With hash mod N, nearly every key would move. For a limiter, the moved keys' buckets restart full on their new owner, which errs in the client's favour: the safe direction.

@8
Look at the ring and you can see why real systems add 100 to 200 virtual nodes per physical node: here N1 and N2 own nothing at all. Virtual nodes even out the load, and a node that leaves spreads its keys across many neighbours. The cost for the limiter is one extra hop to the owning node, and when a node leaves, its share of keys starts over.
