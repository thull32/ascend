@0
This is affinity routing, the design the limiter grows into at 10 times the scale. Four limiter nodes sit on a circle of positions from 0 to 359 degrees: N1 at 57, N2 at 147, N3 at 237 and N4 at 327. Every rate-limit key is hashed onto the same circle. A key belongs to the first node at or after it, walking clockwise, and its bucket lives in that node's memory, with no shared store at all.

@1
API key k 81f2 hashes to 144 degrees. Walking clockwise, the first node is N2 at 147, so N2 holds its buckets and makes every decision for it.

@2-6
The rest follow the same rule. Key k 0a9c, at 300 degrees, walks to N4. Key k 77de, at 87, walks to N2. The first IP prefix, at 180, goes to N3. Key k 5b10, at 252, goes to N4. The second IP prefix, at 153, just past N2, walks on to N3. One rule, no coordination: any gateway can compute the owner of any key.

@7
All six keys are placed: two each on N2, N3 and N4, and none on N1. Six keys is a small sample, and nothing happened to land in N1's arc.

@8
Now add a fifth node, N5, at 115 degrees, between N1 and N2. It claims only that arc. Key k 77de sits on it, so it moves from N2 to N5: one key of six. No key moves between the old nodes. With hash mod N, going from four nodes to five would have remapped all six. For a limiter, a moved key's bucket restarts full on its new owner, which errs in the client's favour: the safe direction.

@9
The ideal is that the newcomer takes its fair share, about a fifth of the keys. With one point per node, how much it takes depends on where it lands, which is why real rings give each node 100 to 200 virtual nodes: the arcs even out, a new node takes a little from everyone, and a node that leaves spreads its keys across many neighbours. The cost for the limiter is one extra hop to the owning node, and when a node leaves, its share of buckets starts over.
