@0
A gateway on the left, Redis on the right, and a circuit breaker between them, with three states. Closed passes calls to Redis and counts failures. After 3 consecutive failures it opens and fails every call instantly. After 10 seconds it goes half-open and lets one trial call decide. The point of the whole machine: a request should not keep paying the 2 millisecond timeout on a Redis that is not answering.

@1
Normal operation. The breaker is closed, the call succeeds, and the success keeps the failure counter at zero.

@2-3
Then the primary for one slot range dies, and Redis starts timing out. Call 2 holds a gateway request for the full 2 milliseconds before failing: failure 1 of 3. Call 3, failure 2 of 3. Two milliseconds sounds small, but every request in flight pays it, and that is how one sick dependency drags its callers down.

@4
Call 4 times out too. Failure 3 of 3: the threshold is reached, and the breaker trips open.

@5
Now call 5 is rejected in under a millisecond, without touching Redis. A slow failure has become a fast one, and the gateway decides from its fallback, the local bucket, at twice the node's fair share.

@6
Over the next 10 seconds the breaker sheds calls 6 to 20 the same way. Redis gets quiet time instead of a queue of retries, and no request waits on it.

@7-8
The open timeout elapses and the breaker goes half-open: exactly one trial call is allowed through, call 21. It times out. Redis Cluster has not yet promoted a replica, so the breaker opens again for another 10 seconds. Many implementations back this timeout off.

@9-10
Redis recovers: the cluster declares the failure after its node timeout, 15 seconds by default, and promotes a replica. Ten seconds after the failed trial the breaker goes half-open again, and this trial, call 22, succeeds. The breaker closes and resets its counter, about 21 seconds after the primary died in the lesson's trace. Two probe calls in all, one failed and one successful.

@11
Three traps. A threshold that counts a single flaky call trips too often, so use a failure rate over a window with a minimum volume. One breaker shared across unrelated endpoints lets one broken path take down the others. And a breaker without a fallback still fails the user, just faster.

@12
The trade-off: the breaker gives up the few calls that would have succeeded while it was open, and in return frees the caller and lets the dependency recover. For the limiter, the fallback errs toward admitting, at most twice the limit fleet-wide, which is right for fairness limits. For security limits such as login attempts, the fallback is a strict local limit, or rejection: fail open for fairness, fail closed for security.
