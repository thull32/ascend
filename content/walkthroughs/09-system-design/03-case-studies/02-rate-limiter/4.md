@0
A circuit breaker sits between a caller and a dependency, with three states. Closed passes calls and counts failures. After 3 consecutive failures it opens and fails every call instantly. After 5 seconds it goes half-open and lets one trial call decide. The picture uses a slow payments API with a 2 second timeout; in the limiter, the dependency is Redis and the budget is 2 milliseconds, but the states work the same way.

@1
Normal operation. The breaker is closed, the call returns 200 in 40 milliseconds, and the success keeps the failure counter at zero.

@2-3
The dependency starts timing out. Call 2 holds the caller for the full timeout before failing: failure 1 of 3. Call 3, failure 2 of 3. Every slow failure costs a thread and the whole timeout, and under load that is how one slow dependency exhausts the caller.

@4
Call 4 times out too. Failure 3 of 3: the threshold is reached, and the breaker trips open.

@5-6
Now call 5 is rejected in under a millisecond, without touching the dependency. A slow failure has become a fast one. Over the next 5 seconds the breaker sheds 15 more calls the same way, and the dependency gets quiet time instead of a queue of retries. The caller serves a fallback; in the limiter, that is a local bucket at twice the node's fair share.

@7-8
The open timeout elapses and the breaker goes half-open: exactly one trial call is allowed through. It fails, the dependency is still sick, so the breaker opens again for another 5 seconds. Many implementations back this timeout off.

@9-10
The dependency recovers. Five more seconds, half-open again, and this trial succeeds. The breaker closes and resets its counter. In the lesson's trace, this is Redis Cluster promoting a replica after about 15 to 20 seconds, and the breakers closing at about 21.

@11
Three traps. A threshold that counts a single flaky call trips too often, so use a failure rate over a window with a minimum volume. One breaker shared across unrelated endpoints lets one broken path take down the others. And a breaker without a fallback still fails the user, just faster.

@12
The trade-off: the breaker gives up the few calls that would have succeeded while it was open, and in return frees the caller and lets the dependency recover. For the limiter, the fallback errs toward admitting, at most twice the limit fleet-wide, which is right for fairness limits. For security limits such as login attempts, the fallback is a strict local limit, or rejection: fail open for fairness, fail closed for security.
