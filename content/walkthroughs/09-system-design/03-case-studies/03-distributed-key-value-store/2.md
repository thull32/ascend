@0
Four nodes on a circle of positions from 0 to 359 degrees: N1 at 0, N2 at 90, N3 at 180 and N4 at 270. Each key is hashed onto the same circle, and it belongs to the first node at or after it, walking clockwise. Compare hash mod N: with 48 nodes, adding a 49th keeps a key in place only about 1 time in 49, so 98 percent of the data moves.

@1
The key cart 9912 hashes to 142 degrees. Walking clockwise, the first node is N3 at 180, so N3 owns it.

@2-6
The rest follow the same rule. User 42, at 250 degrees, walks to N4. Session 7f, at 299, runs past the last node, wraps past the top, and lands on N1. Cart 1180 at 45 degrees and prefs 42 at 34 both walk to N2. User 77, at 139, goes to N3. No lookup table and no coordinator: any node, or a token-aware driver, can compute the owner.

@7
All six keys are placed: one on N1, two on N2, two on N3 and one on N4. Each node owns the arc that ends at its own position.

@8
Now add a fifth node, N5, at 39 degrees, between N1 and N2. Only the keys on the arc it takes over change owner. Prefs 42 sits there, so it moves from N2 to N5: one key of six. Cart 1180, at 45 degrees, is just past N5 and stays on N2. No key moves between the old nodes. With hash mod N, going from four nodes to five would have remapped four of these six keys.

@9
The ideal is that the newcomer takes its fair share, about a fifth of the keys, and nothing else moves. With one position per node, how much it takes depends on where it lands, and all of it comes from one neighbour. So real systems give each machine many positions, virtual nodes: the arcs even out, a dead node's load spreads over many peers, and a replacement streams from many in parallel. But more is not free: with 256 tokens per node, any two failures in different zones leave some keys below quorum. That is why Cassandra 4 lowered its default to 16.
