@0
P is the pickup, and the weights on the roads are minutes. Two drivers are candidates: D1, up and to the left, which is closer in a straight line, and D2, down and to the right, which is farther on the map. Straight-line distance lies, so the matcher asks the road graph. Dijkstra's rule is on screen: always settle the closest unsettled node, because with no negative weights its distance can never improve.

@1-3
P settles at 0. Its two roads give A a distance of 2 and F a distance of 4.

@4-6
A is the closest unsettled node, so it settles at 2. From A, D2 becomes 5 and B becomes 5.

@7
F settles at 4. Its road to D2 would give 4 plus 2, which is 6, no better than 5, so nothing changes.

@8
D2 settles at 5. That is one candidate done: 5 minutes by road. The matcher stops once every candidate is settled, so it keeps going for D1.

@9-10
B settles at 5, and the road from B to C is the bridge across the river: C becomes 7.

@11-14
C settles at 7 and gives E 10. E settles and gives D1 13.

@15
D1 settles at 13 minutes. The driver who looked closest on the map is more than twice as far as D2 by road.

@16
The highlighted edges are the shortest-path tree, and with a binary heap the cost grows with the number of nodes plus edges, times a logarithm. In production the matcher runs one such search from the pickup and stops when all candidates are settled: on a million-intersection grid that settled about 2,000 nodes in just over a millisecond. The expensive query is the fare estimate across the city, which settled 444,000 nodes in 365 milliseconds, and that is why real engines precompute shortcuts with contraction hierarchies.
