@0
Three hosts: a viewer in Tokyo, an edge cache in Tokyo, and the origin in Virginia. This is the classic CDN, the model Open Connect deliberately departs from. The rule: the first request for an object goes all the way to origin and is cached at the edge on its way back; later viewers nearby get it from the edge.

@1
Viewer A asks for a video segment, segment 42. DNS or anycast sent the request to the nearest edge.

@2
The edge does not have it: a miss. So it fetches from origin, across the Pacific, about 150 milliseconds of round trip.

@3-4
The origin responds, and its Cache-Control header lets the edge keep the segment for a day. Viewer A gets the bytes after about 160 milliseconds, and the edge now holds a copy.

@5-6
Viewer B, also in Tokyo, asks for the same segment. Served from the edge in about 5 milliseconds, and the origin never sees it.

@7
Notice who paid: the first viewer, with a slow fetch across the internet, at whatever moment they pressed play. For Netflix, that moment is 9 at night, the worst time to fetch anything across the internet. So Open Connect is described as proactive instead. Each day the system predicts what each site's members will watch, and appliances inside ISPs download the changes in an off-peak fill window. That works because the catalogue is finite and scheduled, popularity is predictable, the catalogue is small next to daily demand, and off-peak bandwidth sits idle. A miss at peak becomes rare by design.
