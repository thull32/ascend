@0
A stream of events, a window operator, and a sink. Windowing turns an endless stream into finite groups. Tumbling windows are fixed-size and never overlap. Here they are 10 seconds long; for stream starts per second, Netflix's primary health signal, think one-minute windows per region, device and ISP. Every event belongs to exactly one window, and the window emits one result when it closes.

@1-4
Events at 1, 3, 6 and 9 seconds all fall in the window from 0 to 10. The count climbs to 4, and the window stays open, because a later event might still belong to it.

@5
An event at 12 seconds falls in the next window. So the first one is complete: the operator emits a count of 4 and discards that window's state. A window's result is available only once the next has started.

@6-8
Events at 14 and 17 seconds join the second window. At 22 seconds it closes with 3.

@9-12
25 and 27 join the third window, which closes at 31 seconds with 3. Then 33 joins the fourth. One small counter per open window, per key. That is why tumbling windows scale to a firehose of start events across thousands of region, device and ISP combinations.

@13
Sliding windows overlap. With 10 second windows sliding every 5, each event counts in two windows, and a result comes out every 5 seconds. Smoother output, twice the state.

@14
Session windows have no fixed size. Events closer than 4 seconds apart merge into one session, which closes after a gap. The same 12 events form 2 sessions: 7 events from 1 to 17 seconds, and 5 from 22 to 33.

@15
All three assume you know when a window is complete, and that is the job of watermarks: an event that arrives after its window fired is either dropped or forces a correction. For stream starts, the result is then compared with the expected count for that minute, because viewing follows strong daily and weekly cycles. A shortfall in one ISP's window points at that ISP's appliances or peering, not at the whole service.
