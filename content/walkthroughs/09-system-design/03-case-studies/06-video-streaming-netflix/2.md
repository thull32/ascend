@0
One Open Connect site, with four appliances placed on a ring of positions from 0 to 359 degrees: N1 at 12, N2 at 102, N3 at 192 and N4 at 282. Files are hashed onto the same ring, and each file lives on the first appliance at or after it, walking clockwise. Any component can compute a file's appliance with no lookup table.

@1-3
Title 81's 1080p AV1 encode hashes to 169 degrees, and the first appliance clockwise is N3. Title 81's 4K HEVC encode lands at 3 degrees and walks to N1. Title 17's 720p encode, at 143 degrees, goes to N3. Notice that the unit is a file, one encode of one title, not a title: the two renditions of title 81 landed on different appliances.

@4-6
Title 44's 1080p AV1 encode, at 279 degrees, walks to N4. Title 17's English audio, at 81, goes to N2. Title 44's 480p encode lands at 344, past the last appliance, wraps past the top, and reaches N1.

@7
All six files are placed: two on N1, one on N2, two on N3, one on N4.

@8
Now add an appliance, N5, at 353 degrees, between N4 and N1. Only the files on the arc it takes over move: title 44's 480p encode goes from N1 to N5, one file of six. No file moves between the old appliances. With hash mod N, four of these six would have moved. That matters because filling is done in an off-peak window a few hours long, and every moved file is bytes to download in it.

@9
The ideal is that a new appliance takes its fair share, about a fifth of the files. With one position per machine, how much it takes depends on where it lands, so real rings give each machine many positions, and the load evens out. And for a hit title, one copy is not enough: popular files get several copies across the site, so no single appliance takes all of that title's traffic.
