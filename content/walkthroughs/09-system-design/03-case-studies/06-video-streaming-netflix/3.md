@0
A sender and a receiver, and a chart of the congestion window, measured in segments per round trip. A new TCP connection does not know the link's capacity, so it starts small and probes. Slow start doubles the window every round trip, until a threshold, here 16; after that it grows by one per round trip. The picture starts at 1 segment; modern stacks start at 10.

@1-8
Round trip 1 sends one segment, and the acknowledgement doubles the window to 2. Round trip 2 sends 2, then 4, then 8. Each doubling costs a full round trip, and that is the point for video: the first segment's bytes cannot arrive faster than the window allows. With an appliance 15 milliseconds away, the window passes the link's bandwidth-delay product, about 37.5 kilobytes on a 20 megabit link, in about two round trips. From a server 150 milliseconds away, the same ramp costs 300 milliseconds. That is one reason appliances sit inside ISPs.

@9-10
Round trip 5 sends 16 segments, the threshold, and one of them is lost. Duplicate acknowledgements reveal the loss, so the threshold drops to half, 8, and the window drops to 8. A timeout would have reset it to 1.

@11-16
From here the window grows by one per round trip: 8, then 9, then 10, then 11. Cautious, linear probing.

@17
The shape is a sawtooth: additive increase, multiplicative decrease. Cubic grows faster on long, fat links, and BBR models bandwidth and round-trip time instead of reacting to loss. For the start path, the lesson's levers come straight from this picture: put the appliance close, so each round trip is short, and make the first segment small. Fetching the first segment at 750 kilobits instead of 1,750 removes about 200 milliseconds from play delay.
