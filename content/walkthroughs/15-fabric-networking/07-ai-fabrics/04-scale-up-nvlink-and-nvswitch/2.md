@0
On screen are two of the 128 GPU servers in Meridian's San Jose cluster, srv1 and srv2, with eight GPUs each, numbered 1 to 8 in this drawing and joined inside each server by NVLink through NVSwitch chips. Every GPU has its own 400 gigabit per second network card. A rail is the set of leaves that card k of every server is cabled to: every GPU1 to rail 1, every GPU2 to rail 2, and so on across eight rails. The one idea to hold: traffic between same-numbered GPUs crosses a single switch, and NVLink can move other traffic onto the right rail before it ever reaches the network.

@1
The table sizes it for 1,024 GPUs. Each leaf is a 51.2 terabit per second switch, 128 ports of 400 gigabits, Spectrum-4 class. 64 ports face the network cards and 64 face the spines, so no leaf is oversubscribed. 128 cards per rail over 64 ports makes 2 leaves per rail, one for srv1 to 64 and one for srv65 to 128, so 16 leaves in all. Their 1,024 uplinks fill 8 spines of 128 ports. The drawing shows only the first leaf of each rail and one spine.

@2
Why build it this way? Because of the traffic. When NCCL runs a collective across servers, it binds each GPU to its nearest card and connects GPU k on one server to GPU k on the others, so nearly every byte that leaves a server travels inside one rail. The exception is all-to-all traffic in mixture-of-experts models, where GPU k must reach other GPU numbers too. That case comes shortly.

@3
First, the easy one. GPU3 on srv1 sends to GPU3 on srv2. Its card puts the RDMA write on its own 400 gigabit link to rail3-leaf1.

@4
rail3-leaf1 forwards it straight out of srv2's GPU3 port. One switch between the two GPUs: no spine, no ECMP choice, and nothing shared with the other seven rails.

@5
Now GPU7 on srv1 has to send to GPU8 on srv2, a different rail. Sent straight from GPU7's own card, it climbs rail7-leaf1, crosses a spine, and comes down rail8-leaf1. That is three switches instead of one, plus an ECMP choice among the eight spines, where it can collide with other flows.

@6
NCCL avoids this with PXN, short for PCIe times NVLink, in NCCL 2.12 and later. GPU7 first copies the data over NVLink to GPU8 in its own server. NVLink gives each H100 GPU about 450 gigabytes per second each way, against 50 for a 400 gigabit card, so the extra copy costs little.

@7
Then srv1's GPU8 card sends the data on rail 8, and rail8-leaf1 delivers it to srv2's GPU8. One switch again, and the traffic stays on its rail. The scale-up fabric has just done routing work for the scale-out fabric.

@8
The second table lines up the paths. Same rail: one switch. Other rail, sent directly: three. Other rail through PXN: one switch plus an NVLink copy. The spines still matter, though. srv1 and srv65 are both on rail 1 but hang off different leaves, so their GPU1s talk through a spine, three switches, which is why every leaf has eight 400 gigabit links to every spine.

@9
Last, the failure. If srv2's GPU8 cable or card fails, every connection NCCL built through that card breaks. A synchronous collective cannot finish without GPU8, so the job typically stops with an NCCL error and restarts from its last checkpoint. That generalises: in a rail-optimised fabric, one card, optic or leaf port belongs to specific GPUs and a specific job. So the platform should map every card, optic and leaf port to the GPUs and jobs behind it, and watch link flaps and optic receive power per rail, not just per switch.
