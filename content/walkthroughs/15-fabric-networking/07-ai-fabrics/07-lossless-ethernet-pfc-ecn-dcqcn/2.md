@0
Same switch, same two senders into one port, now with the throttle instead of the brake. DCQCN, the congestion control RoCE NICs run, has three roles: rail1-leaf1 is the congestion point that marks packets, srv3's NIC is the notification point that reports the marks, and the NICs on srv1 and srv2 are reaction points that set their own rates. The parameters are the ones published with DCQCN in 2015; production values are tuned per platform. The one idea: the switch only flips two bits, and every rate decision is made in the sender's NIC, after the mark has travelled all the way round.

@1
For the first 20 microseconds only srv1 sends, at the full 400 gigabits per second srv3's port can carry, so the queue stays empty. Every packet leaves with its ECN field set to one zero, ECT zero: this sender reacts to marks.

@2
At 20 microseconds srv2 joins, straight at 400 gigabits per second, because DCQCN has no slow start. 800 arrives for a 400 gigabit port, and the queue grows by 50 kilobytes every microsecond.

@3
The queue passes Kmin, 5 kilobytes, almost at once, and Kmax, 200 kilobytes, at 24 microseconds. Between them at most 1 percent of packets are marked, and each flow sends only about 47 packets in that window, so none is marked in this run. Above Kmax the switch marks every packet.

@4
rail1-leaf1 sets the ECN field to one one, congestion experienced, and still forwards the packet: marking replaces dropping. It marks as the packet joins the queue, so the mark waits 4 microseconds behind 200 kilobytes before srv3 sees it. That wait is part of the loop.

@5
At 28 microseconds srv3's NIC receives marked packets on both connections and answers each sender with a congestion notification packet, a CNP: a small RoCEv2 packet with BTH opcode 81 in hex, addressed to the sender's queue pair, sent with DSCP 48 into strict priority queue 6 so it overtakes the queued data. At most one CNP per queue pair every 50 microseconds, however many marks arrive.

@6
The CNPs land at 29 microseconds and each sender cuts. The target rate takes the current rate, 400, and the current rate is multiplied by one minus half of alpha, the sender's estimate of congestion, which starts at 1. So the rate halves to 200 gigabits per second, and alpha stays 1. Together they send exactly 400, so the queue stops growing, but it is already 450 kilobytes deep.

@7
400 in, 400 out: the queue stands still, adding 9 microseconds to every packet, all of them marked. srv3 stays silent until 50 microseconds have passed since its last CNP. And no pause: each sender's 225 kilobyte share is below the PFC threshold a switch with tens of megabytes of shared buffer computes, though the previous example's fixed 100 kilobytes would have fired.

@8
At 78 microseconds srv3 may send again, and at 79 both senders cut a second time: target 200, current rate 100 gigabits per second, alpha still 1. Now 200 arrives for 400 out, and the queue drains at 25 kilobytes per microsecond.

@9
The queue is empty at 97 microseconds, and no third CNP follows. The first cut fixed the rate but not the queue built during the feedback delay; the second drained it and overshot, leaving srv3's port at 50 percent. DCQCN trades a short spell of under use for an empty queue.

@10
55 microseconds after the last CNP, the rate increase timer fires and fast recovery begins: the current rate moves halfway back to the target, from 100 to 150 gigabits per second, and the port is at 75 percent. The alpha timer fires too, decaying alpha to 0.9961, so the next cut will be slightly gentler.

@11
Every further 55 microseconds without a CNP halves the remaining gap: 175, 187.5, 193.75, then 196.875 gigabits per second at 354 microseconds, the fifth and last fast recovery step. The pair is back to 98.4 percent of srv3's port, with the queue still empty. That is the shape on the chart: cut hard, climb slowly.

@12
Then additive increase: the target rises by 40 megabits per second to 200.04, and the current rate moves halfway to it, 198.46. The byte counter, the other increase trigger, has counted only 6.9 of its 10 megabytes, so at this speed the timer drives recovery.

@13
From here the senders creep up until their total passes 400, a mark produces a CNP, and the cycle repeats with the queue mostly near empty, so PFC rarely fires. To see the loop in production, read three places side by side: ECN marks and queue depth on the switch port, marked packets received and CNPs sent at the receiving NIC, and CNPs handled at the senders. More CNPs sent than handled means the network is losing them.
