@0
On screen, a small piece of Meridian's San Jose back end: the rail 1 NICs of srv1 and srv2 both sending RDMA writes to srv3 through one switch, rail1-leaf1, every link 400 gigabits per second. Priority 3 is lossless, because one dropped RoCE packet makes the sender resend everything after it. Hold onto one idea: when the buffer fills, the switch does not drop, it tells the senders to stop, early enough to catch the bytes already on their way.

@1
One of those packets: its IPv4 header carries DSCP 26, which the switch trusts and maps to priority 3. The RDMA payload rides in UDP to port 4791, in a 4,158 byte frame. A priority is all PFC ever acts on: never a flow, a queue pair or a destination.

@2
At time zero both NICs send at 400 gigabits per second into one 400 gigabit port, so the egress queue to srv3 grows by 50 kilobytes every microsecond: 100 after 2 microseconds. The switch also charges each packet to the port and priority it arrived on, its priority group, because a pause must go to the port the bytes came from. Priority group 3 from srv1 holds 50 kilobytes, and so does srv2's. The chart plots srv1's.

@3
At 4 microseconds each priority group reaches XOFF, 100 kilobytes, fixed here for simple numbers; real switches derive it from free shared buffer. rail1-leaf1 sends a PFC pause out of both ingress ports: upstream, to the senders, not to the receiver.

@4
The pause frame: 64 bytes, to a reserved address no switch forwards, EtherType 88 08, opcode 01 01. Its class enable vector is 8, bit 3 only, so the other seven priorities keep flowing on the same link. Priority 3's timer is 65,535 quanta of 512 bit times, about 83.9 microseconds at this speed, and the switch re-sends the pause before it runs out if it still needs one.

@5
The senders do not stop at once. For 0.6 microseconds more, bytes keep landing: the pause's trip up 50 metres of fibre, a quarter of a microsecond, the bytes already on the fibre, another quarter, and about a tenth for the NIC to react. That is 30 kilobytes per port; srv3's port drains half, so each priority group peaks at 115 kilobytes. Headroom is the buffer reserved above XOFF for exactly this, here 50 kilobytes: those 30 plus a 9,216 byte frame in progress at each end. The peak stays under the 150 kilobyte line, and nothing is dropped.

@6
With nothing arriving, each priority group drains by 25 kilobytes per microsecond and reaches XON, 80 kilobytes, at 6 microseconds. XON sits below XOFF so the switch does not flap. rail1-leaf1 sends the same frame with priority 3's timer at zero, which means resume.

@7
The cycle repeats every 4 microseconds. Each NIC transmits half the time and averages 200 gigabits per second, srv3's queue never falls below 130 kilobytes, so its port never idles, and no packet is lost. Pause frames never appear in a host capture; on a ConnectX NIC the evidence is the priority 3 pause count and pause duration rising.

@8
PFC kept priority 3 lossless, but bluntly. While paused, srv1 cannot send priority 3 traffic to anyone, even an idle receiver. And a switch that fills up pauses its own upstream neighbours the same way, which is congestion spreading, the next lesson's subject. That is why RoCE fabrics add ECN and DCQCN to slow senders before PFC fires, leaving the pause as a rare backstop. The number to watch is pause duration, the fraction of time a port spends paused.
