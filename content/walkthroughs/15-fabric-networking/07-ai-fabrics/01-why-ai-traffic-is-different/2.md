@0
On screen are seven servers of the San Jose cluster on the rail 1 leaf: six senders, srv002 to srv007, and one receiver, srv001, each using GPU0's 400 gigabit per second network card. The leaf is non-blocking, meaning it can switch every port at line rate at once. So hold onto one idea: the only place traffic can pile up is an egress port that several senders aim at together, and what happens there depends on whether the switch drops or pauses.

@1
At the same instant every sender starts a 1 megabyte transfer to srv001, as happens in an all-to-all, a gather of results to one rank, or a storage read. Six times 400 is 2,400 gigabits per second, converging on a single 400 gigabit egress port. That is six to one incast.

@2
Only 400 gigabits per second can leave towards srv001, so the excess, 2,000 gigabits per second, piles up in the leaf's egress queue at 250 kilobytes every microsecond. After 4 microseconds the queue already holds 1 megabyte, while only 0.2 megabytes have gone out to srv001.

@3
At 8 microseconds the queue reaches 2 megabytes, the share of the switch's buffer this port may take in this illustrative example; the real limit depends on the chip and its settings. The leaf starts tail-dropping: of every six packets that arrive from now on, one finds room and five are discarded.

@4
At 20 microseconds every sender has pushed its 1 megabyte at line rate and stops. Of the 6 megabytes that arrived, 1 has gone out to srv001, 2 sit in the queue, and the switch has dropped 3.

@5
The queue drains at 400 gigabits per second and is empty at 60 microseconds. srv001 received 3 of the 6 megabytes. The 3 megabytes the switch dropped must be sent again.

@6
For RDMA it is worse. A reliable-connected queue pair accepts packets only in order. After a sender's first loss, about 8 microseconds in, srv001's network card sends one NAK, a negative acknowledgement, for the gap, and discards everything else that sender delivers until the missing packet is resent. That is go-back-N, the classic RoCE behaviour; newer cards can resend selectively. The table shows each sender had 0.4 megabytes accepted in order and must resend 0.6, so 3.6 megabytes in all, more than the 3 the switch dropped.

@7
Now the same burst in a lossless class. RoCE traffic marked DSCP 26 is mapped to priority 3, which has priority flow control, PFC, enabled on every port. The leaf counts the bytes each ingress port has queued in that class against a threshold called XOFF, the fill level at which it tells the sender on that port to stop. Here it is 200 kilobytes per port, again illustrative.

@8
At 4.8 microseconds each of the six ingress ports has 200 kilobytes queued, 1.2 megabytes in all, and the leaf sends a PFC PAUSE frame out of each of those six ports, for priority 3 only. Each sender's card stops transmitting in that class. The counters show six pauses and zero drops.

@9
For the rest of the burst the leaf pauses and resumes each sender as its share crosses the threshold. Each one averages 400 divided by 6, 66.7 gigabits per second, so it is paused about 83 percent of the time. On the chart the queue holds flat at 1.2 megabytes, srv001's link stays full, and nothing is dropped.

@10
The last sender finishes at 96 microseconds and the queue is empty at 120 microseconds. That is 6 megabytes through a 400 gigabit port in exactly the time that port needs, with zero drops and nothing to resend.

@11
The price is paid upstream. A paused sender cannot send any priority 3 traffic, even to receivers that are not congested. And if the senders sat behind spines, the leaf would pause a spine instead, whose own queue would fill until it paused every port feeding it: congestion spreading, which the lesson on when lossless goes wrong takes apart. ECN marking with DCQCN, in the lesson on lossless Ethernet, exists to slow the senders down before the queue ever reaches XOFF.

@12
Side by side: the lossy class dropped 3 megabytes and resent about 3.6; the PFC class dropped nothing and paused its senders 83 percent of the burst. Each side of the link records a different half. The switch shows queue depth and either egress drops or pause frames sent; the network cards show out of sequence packets at the receiver and sequence error NAKs at the senders, or pauses received. The general lesson: incast lasts tens of microseconds, so a 30 second average never sees it, and a platform that collects both halves can tell incast, deep queues on one egress port, from a faulty link, which shows CRC errors on the port itself.
