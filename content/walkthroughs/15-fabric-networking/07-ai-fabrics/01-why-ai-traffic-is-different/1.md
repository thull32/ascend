@0
On screen are eight GPUs from Meridian's San Jose cluster: GPU0 of servers srv001 to srv008, each with its own 400 gigabit per second network card, all plugged into the rail 1 leaf, rail1-leaf1. They train one 1 billion parameter model with data parallelism, so every GPU holds a full copy of the model and works on its own slice of each batch. Hold onto one sentence, because it is the whole story: a synchronous collective finishes only when its slowest transfer does.

@1
Step 1 starts with the forward pass. Each GPU runs its slice of the batch through the model for 100 milliseconds. The chart at the bottom tracks srv001: its GPU line sits at 100 percent busy, and its link line sits at zero. Nothing needs to be exchanged yet, so the network is silent.

@2
The backward pass takes 200 milliseconds, about twice the forward, and leaves each GPU with 2 gigabytes of gradients: one 2 byte bf16 number for each of the billion parameters. The eight sets differ, because each GPU saw different data, and no GPU may update its weights until all eight have been summed.

@3
At 300 milliseconds all eight start the all-reduce at the same instant. With NCCL's ring algorithm each GPU sends two times seven eighths of its 2 gigabytes, which is 3.5 gigabytes, and receives as much, so every rail 1 link runs flat out in both directions. At 400 gigabits per second, which is 50 gigabytes per second, that takes 70 milliseconds. Watch the chart flip: the link jumps to 100 percent and the GPU drops to zero, with nothing to compute until the all-reduce ends.

@4
Step 1 is done at 370 milliseconds, 300 of compute plus 70 of communication, leaving aside the few milliseconds each GPU spends applying the summed gradients. For 70 of those 370 milliseconds, 18.9 percent of the step, every GPU sat idle waiting for the network. Real frameworks start reducing early gradients while the backward pass is still running, but whatever does not overlap adds straight onto every step.

@5
Now the fault. Before step 2, srv005's link flaps and comes back at 200 gigabits instead of 400, for example because the two ends negotiated a lower speed over a marginal copper cable. Its label on screen now reads 200 G. The link is up and passing traffic, so a monitor that only checks up or down sees nothing wrong.

@6
Step 2's forward and backward passes run from 370 to 670 milliseconds, the same 300 milliseconds as before, srv005 included. A slow link costs nothing while nobody is sending.

@7
The all-reduce begins, and every GPU again has 3.5 gigabytes to move. But a ring advances in lockstep: each of its 14 rounds, twice the eight GPUs minus one, waits for its slowest transfer, the one through srv005's 200 gigabit link. So every other link drops to half its rate too.

@8
70 milliseconds in, the moment step 1's all-reduce had already finished, every GPU has moved only half its data. The chart shows srv001's healthy link at 50 percent. At 25 gigabytes per second the 3.5 gigabytes take 140 milliseconds, twice as long, and all eight GPUs sit idle for every one of them.

@9
Step 2 ends at 810 milliseconds: 440 milliseconds long, 70 more than step 1, which is 18.9 percent longer. Each GPU's idle time has doubled to 140 milliseconds, 31.8 percent of the step. One slow link out of eight slowed all eight GPUs equally.

@10
Here is the signal. The GPUs cannot point at the culprit, because all eight report the same step time and the same idle time. The leaf's port counters from that all-reduce can. Seven ports carry 200 gigabits per second at 50 percent of their 400 gigabit speed. srv005's port carries the same 200 gigabits at 100 percent of its reduced speed. The one saturated port, the only one not running at 400, is the straggler, and an alert on operational speed differing from intended speed catches it before any job notices.

@11
Scale it to the full 1,024 GPU job. One such link among 1,024 has the same effect: if it stretches every step from 370 to 440 milliseconds, 15.9 percent of every GPU-hour is wasted, about 3,900 GPU-hours a day, close to 10,000 dollars a day at an illustrative two and a half dollars per GPU-hour. The pattern to remember: in a synchronous job the fabric runs at the speed of its worst link, average utilisation hides it, and that is why AI fabrics are judged by job completion time and by the worst link, not the median one.
