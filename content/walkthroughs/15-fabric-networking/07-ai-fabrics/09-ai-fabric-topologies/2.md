@0
On screen are both halves of rail 1 in San Jose, as a small example: rail1-leaf1, serving servers 1 to 64, and rail1-leaf2, serving 65 to 128, joined by four of the eight spines with one 400 gigabit per second uplink to each. The GPU1 cards of servers 1 to 4 are about to send to partners on servers 65 to 68 at full rate: exactly 1,600 gigabits offered to exactly 1,600 of uplinks. The idea to hold onto: what matters is the unit the switch balances, a whole flow or a single packet, and whether the receiving card can live with the answer.

@1
Each flow is one RDMA connection, one queue pair, with one fixed 5-tuple. The destination port is always UDP 4791, and the card derives the source port from the queue pair: flow A, for example, goes from 10.200.1.1, port 51003, to 10.200.1.65. Static ECMP hashes that 5-tuple and pins the whole flow to one uplink. The table shows the hashes modulo 4: flow A gives 1, B gives 2, C gives 1 and D gives 3.

@2
Counting from zero, 1 is ai-spine2, so flows A and C both land there, and ai-spine1 gets nothing. Two 400 gigabit flows share one 400 gigabit uplink while another idles. With four flows on four links this is the usual outcome, not bad luck: all four land on different spines only 24 times in 256, about 9 percent of the time.

@3
The chart fills in. Flows A and C get 200 gigabits each, B and D keep their 400, and the leaf delivers 1,200 of its 1,600. A collective waits for its slowest transfer, so the step's communication now runs at half speed. More queue pairs per connection only improve the odds, because the hash still knows nothing about load.

@4
Now turn on adaptive routing, as in NVIDIA's Spectrum-X, and in InfiniBand switches before it. The leaf chooses an uplink for every packet instead of every flow, picking whichever has the least data queued at that instant. Flow A's next four packets, sequence numbers 1001 to 1004, leave on four different spines: 1001 on ai-spine1, 1002 on ai-spine2, 1003 on ai-spine3, 1004 on ai-spine4. Spreading one flow over every path like this is called packet spraying.

@5
Spread packet by packet, every uplink carries 400 gigabits, flows A and C climb back to their full 400 on the chart, and the leaf delivers all 1,600 of 1,600. No flow is tied to one path any more.

@6
The price is order. The four paths have different queues: 0.4 microseconds through ai-spine1, 0.7 through ai-spine2, 0.3 through ai-spine3 and 0.6 through ai-spine4. So 1003, on the shortest queue, overtakes 1002, and flow A reaches server 65's card as 1001, 1003, 1002, 1004. But RoCE's reliable connection expects packets in strict sequence order.

@7
A standard RoCE card that expects 1002 and receives 1003 treats it as loss. It discards 1003, counts it as out of sequence, and sends a negative acknowledgement saying it expected 1002. The sender goes back and resends 1002, 1003 and 1004. On such cards spraying turns every reordering into retransmission, and in the lesson's simple model a 1 megabyte write sprayed over four spines crosses the fabric 64 times. That is why plain RoCE fabrics keep per-flow ECMP, and why adaptive routing without a reorder-tolerant receiver is worse than ECMP.

@8
A receiver built for spraying, a BlueField-3 SuperNIC in Spectrum-X for example, writes each payload straight to its place in GPU memory as it arrives. The table shows where: 1001 at the message start, 1003 at 8,192 bytes in, 1002 at 4,096, and 1004 at 12,288. Each packet's place is the start address plus its sequence number minus 1001, times 4,096 bytes. The card tracks which packets have arrived and reports completion only when all four are there, so 1003 before 1002 costs nothing. This is a card feature, not a setting: in a standard RoCE write only the first packet carries the remote address, so the card must know the destination from its own per-message state.

@9
What adaptive routing cannot fix is the last link. If servers 1 and 2 both write to server 65, every path ends on server 65's single 400 gigabit port, and that incast remains congestion control's job. And a leaf deciding on its own queues cannot see a hot link further down the path.

So the general rule: balance on the finest unit your endpoints can tolerate. Flows never reorder but collide; packets never collide but reorder, which makes the switch and the receiving card one design, not two settings. On the platform, watch the spread between the most and least loaded uplinks, which falls to near zero with adaptive routing on, the out-of-sequence and negative acknowledgement counters on every card, and the time of each collective step.
