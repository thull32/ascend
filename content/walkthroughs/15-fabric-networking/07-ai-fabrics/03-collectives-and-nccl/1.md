@0
On screen are four ranks of a training job: GPU0 of servers srv001 to srv004 in Meridian's San Jose cluster, each plugged into the rail 1 leaf at 400 gigabits per second. Each holds a 400 megabyte gradient vector, cut into four chunks of 100 megabytes. The table follows one sample element per chunk, chosen so the digits show who has contributed: rank 0 holds 1 to 4, rank 1 holds 10 to 40, and so on up to rank 3's thousands. Every rank must end holding the sums, 1111, 2222, 3333 and 4444. Hold onto one sentence, because it is the whole algorithm: an all-reduce is a reduce-scatter followed by an all-gather.

@1
NCCL arranges the ranks in a logical ring, 0 to 1 to 2 to 3 and back to 0, drawn dashed. Physically every hop runs card to leaf to card. In every step each rank sends one chunk to its right-hand neighbour and receives one from its left, so every link is busy in both directions at once.

@2
Reduce-scatter, step 1. Rank 0 sends its chunk 0 to rank 1, which adds it to its own: 10 plus 1 makes 11. At the same moment rank 1 sends chunk 1 to rank 2, making 220, rank 2 sends chunk 2 to rank 3, making 3300, and rank 3 sends chunk 3 to rank 0, making 4004. Each transfer is 100 megabytes, which at 400 gigabits per second takes 2 milliseconds.

@3
Step 2. Each rank forwards the partial sum it just made one place further round. Rank 1 sends chunk 0, now 11, to rank 2, which adds its own 100 to make 111. Every rank has sent 200 megabytes, and 4 milliseconds have passed.

@4
Step 3 completes the sums. Rank 2 sends 111 to rank 3, which adds its 1000 and holds 1111, chunk 0's total over all four ranks. In the same step rank 0 completes chunk 1 as 2222, rank 1 completes chunk 2 as 3333, and rank 2 completes chunk 3 as 4444.

@5
Reduce-scatter is done after three steps, one fewer than the number of ranks. Each rank owns one fully reduced chunk: rank 0 chunk 1, rank 1 chunk 2, rank 2 chunk 3, rank 3 chunk 0. Each rank has sent 300 megabytes, three quarters of its data. Stopping here is a collective in its own right, the reduce-scatter that sharded training such as ZeRO and FSDP uses.

@6
All-gather, step 1. Now each rank sends its complete chunk to the right, and the receiver overwrites its partial value instead of adding. Rank 3 sends chunk 0, 1111, to rank 0, replacing the 1 it held, and the other three complete chunks move the same way.

@7
Step 2: the complete chunks move one more place round the ring. Chunk 0, 1111, reaches rank 1, and every rank now holds three of the four totals.

@8
Step 3: the last complete chunks arrive, and every row of the table reads 1111, 2222, 3333, 4444.

@9
Six steps in all, twice the number of ranks minus one. Each rank sent six chunks of 100 megabytes, 600 megabytes, which is one and a half times its 400 megabyte vector. That factor is two times n minus one, over n. At 400 gigabits per second the whole all-reduce took 12 milliseconds.

@10
Compare the obvious alternatives. If every rank sends its whole vector to every other, each sends 1,200 megabytes. If they all funnel through rank 0, which sums and sends back, rank 0's one link takes 1,200 megabytes in and 1,200 out. Either way that is at least 24 milliseconds. The ring keeps every link equally busy and finishes in half the time.

@11
The chart plots the factor for 2 to 16 ranks. It barely grows: 1.5 for 4 ranks, 1.75 for 8, and 1.998 for 1,024, never reaching the limit of 2. Per-rank traffic stays under twice the data however large the ring, which is why data parallelism scales at all. What does grow is the number of steps, each paying a fixed latency, and that is why NCCL switches to trees for small messages at scale.

@12
This is also why nccl-tests prints two numbers. Algorithm bandwidth is the data size over the time: 400 megabytes in 12 milliseconds, 33.3 gigabytes per second. Bus bandwidth multiplies that by the factor, one and a half, to give 50 gigabytes per second, the rate each link actually ran at. Bus bandwidth is the one to compare with the card's 50 gigabytes per second whatever the number of ranks; a healthy real run lands a few percent below it, lost to headers and protocol overhead. The pattern to remember: the ring trades a few extra steps for perfectly even load, so its cost per rank is bounded by twice the data, and its speed is set by its slowest hop.
