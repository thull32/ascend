@0
On screen is a hypothetical InfiniBand build of Meridian's San Jose back end, which in reality runs RoCE. Three servers, srv1, srv2 and srv3, each have a host channel adapter, InfiniBand's name for a network card, on one 400 gigabit switch, ib-leaf1. The subnet manager has already given every port a local ID and filled the switch's forwarding table; it takes no part in moving packets. Hold onto one sentence, because it is the whole mechanism: a sender may transmit only into buffer the receiver has already promised it.

@1
srv1 builds one packet from the middle of a long RDMA WRITE to srv3. An 8 byte local route header carries srv3's local ID, 19, and virtual lane 0, one of several separately buffered lanes on every link. Then the 12 byte base transport header, 4,096 bytes of data, and 6 bytes of checksums: 4,122 bytes in all. Link flow control counts buffer in 64 byte blocks, so this packet needs 65 blocks, rounded up.

@2
Before srv1 may send anything on lane 0, the switch tells it how much room it has, in a 6 byte flow control packet. It advertises a credit limit: the blocks it has received so far, zero, plus its free blocks, 256, which is 16 kilobytes, kept small here so the counts stay readable. srv1 may send until its running total of blocks sent reaches 256.

@3
For packet 1, srv1 takes the limit, subtracts its total sent plus the 65 it needs, and works modulo 4,096. 256 minus 65 is 191, which is no more than 2,048, so the credit is there and the packet goes. srv1's total sent becomes 65. srv3's link is busy with srv2's traffic, so the switch holds packet 1 in its lane 0 buffer.

@4
Packets 2 and 3 pass the same test with 126 and 61 blocks to spare, and the total sent reaches 195. They wait behind srv2's traffic too, so 195 of the switch's 256 blocks are in use and only 61 are free.

@5
Now packet 4. 256 minus 260 is minus 4, which modulo 4,096 is 4,092, far above 2,048. Only 61 credits are left and the packet needs 65, so srv1 simply does not send it. Nothing is dropped and no pause message is needed. The one trace it leaves is a counter: PortXmitWait counts the time the port has data ready and no credit.

@6
srv3's link frees up and the switch forwards packet 1, releasing 65 blocks, so 126 are free. Its next flow control packet raises the limit to the 195 blocks received plus the 126 free, which is 321. Notice that it is an absolute value, not 65 more.

@7
srv1 runs the test again: 321 minus 260 is 61, so packet 4 goes and the total sent reaches 260. From here srv1 sends exactly as fast as the switch drains towards srv3, and the buffer cannot overflow, because nothing leaves without space already reserved for it at the other end.

@8
Both ends send flow control packets regularly, and the counters in them are running totals, 12 bits wide, wrapping every 4,096 blocks, which is why the test works modulo 4,096. Here srv1 reports its total sent, 260, and the switch sets its count of blocks received to match. So a lost flow control packet, or a packet thrown away for a bad link checksum, is corrected by the next one instead of leaking credit forever.

@9
The price is distance. Credit comes back only after a round trip, so to keep a 400 gigabit link full the receiver needs buffer for everything in flight meanwhile: 50 bytes per nanosecond of round trip. With 16 kilobytes, a 3 metre or 30 metre cable runs at full rate. A 100 metre fibre has a 1 microsecond round trip and 50 kilobytes in flight, so it is held to about 131 gigabits per second. That is why real switch ports have far more buffer per lane, and why InfiniBand over long distances needs deep-buffered range extenders.

@10
Credits make each link lossless by construction, where PFC, Ethernet's pause, reacts after a threshold and must keep headroom for bytes already in flight. What credits do not stop is congestion spreading: while srv3 is busy, srv1 is out of credit for everything it sends on lane 0 through this port, including packets for other servers. So InfiniBand adds several virtual lanes and end-to-end congestion control, where the switch sets FECN, the receiver returns BECN and the sender slows down, the same idea as ECN marks and CNPs on RoCE. The pattern to remember: proactive credit trades drops for waiting, so on InfiniBand the signal of trouble is not a drop counter but PortXmitWait rising on the ports that feed a hot spot.
