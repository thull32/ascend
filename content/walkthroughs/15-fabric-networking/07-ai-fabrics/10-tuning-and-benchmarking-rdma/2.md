@0
Two servers of Meridian's San Jose cluster. Inside srv001, GPU0 and GPU1 meet at the NVSwitch. GPU0 also sits under a PCIe switch with its own 400 gigabit ConnectX-7 NIC on the rail 1 leaf, and srv002 is built the same way. Hold onto one idea: network diagrams stop at the NIC, but every byte the NIC sends has first crossed PCIe, and that link, the one nobody draws, decides whether the NIC can reach line rate.

@1-2
Scale up first. GPU0 copies 1 gibibyte into GPU1's memory, a plain memory copy through the NVSwitch: no NIC, no headers, no protocol stack. Each GPU has 18 NVLink links, 450 gigabytes per second each way, so the gibibyte arrives in about 2.4 milliseconds.

@3
Now scale out: the same gibibyte to GPU0 of srv002. With GPUDirect RDMA the NIC reads straight from GPU memory through the PCIe switch the two share, with no copy into the CPU's memory. That link is PCIe generation 5 with 16 lanes, about 63 gigabytes per second each way on the lanes. After PCIe's own packet overhead that leaves roughly 18 percent above what the NIC needs. Thin headroom: the same card trained at 8 lanes halves the link and caps the NIC near 230 gigabits per second.

@4-5
The NIC cuts the data into RoCE packets of 4,096 bytes, 262,144 of them, and sends them at 400 gigabits per second, which is 50 gigabytes, one ninth of NVLink. Each frame takes 83 nanoseconds to serialise. The rail 1 leaf forwards every packet on its destination IP address, with no idea a GPU is behind the NIC.

@6-7
The leaf adds a few hundred nanoseconds to about a microsecond, far more if a queue has built up. srv002's NIC checks sequence numbers and the memory key, then writes the payload through its own PCIe switch straight into GPU0's memory, GPUDirect RDMA again. The whole gibibyte takes about 21.5 milliseconds, nine times NVLink's 2.4.

@8
Side by side in the table: NVLink gives each GPU nine times the bandwidth and about a microsecond of latency, but only to the eight GPUs in its server. The NIC and the fabric reach all 1,024 GPUs, through five devices instead of one.

@9
The chart plots effective bandwidth against message size, with an assumed fixed cost of 1 microsecond per NVLink transfer and 3 per network transfer. At 1 mebibyte NVLink reaches 315 gigabytes per second and the network 44. Messages of a few kilobytes are latency bound on both, and the network only comes within a few percent of its peak from about 4 mebibytes. That is why a benchmark sweeps sizes, why you quote the plateau of the large messages, and why you judge the small ones by their time.

@10
Now the bring-up point. Without GPUDirect RDMA, the data would first be copied from GPU memory into the CPU's memory, and the NIC would read it from there: an extra copy and an extra PCIe crossing at each end. A NIC under the wrong PCIe switch or CPU socket forces a similar detour through the CPU. Neither fails loudly; the job runs, just slower. On srv113 the lesson's GPU memory test gave 261 gigabits against 390 from host memory, because access control services on the PCIe switch ports were sending peer traffic up to the CPU. With them off, 388.

@11
The scale up domain keeps growing. An NVL72 rack joins 72 GPUs with NVLink 5 at 1.8 terabytes per second per GPU, yet each GPU's network link is still about a ninth of its NVLink or less, so placement keeps mattering. The general rule: the slowest link between a GPU's memory and the wire sets the ceiling. On a fresh server, check it in the order the bytes travel: the GPU's pairing with its NIC, the PCIe link's width and speed, GPUDirect actually in use, then the wire.
