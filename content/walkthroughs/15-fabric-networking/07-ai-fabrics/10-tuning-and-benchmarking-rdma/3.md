@0
Four ranks of a training job: GPU0 of srv001 to srv004, each on the rail 1 leaf at 400 gigabits per second, each holding a 400 megabyte gradient vector in four chunks of 100 megabytes. The table tracks one sample element per chunk, and every rank must end with 1111, 2222, 3333 and 4444. The idea that makes nccl-tests readable: an all-reduce makes each rank send nearly twice its data, so the honest measure of the network is not data over time but the rate each link actually ran at. That is bus bandwidth.

@1
NCCL arranges the ranks in a logical ring, 0 to 1 to 2 to 3 and back to 0, each hop physically NIC to leaf to NIC. In every step each rank sends one chunk to its right and receives one from its left, so every link is busy in both directions at once.

@2
Reduce-scatter, step 1 of 3. Each rank sends one chunk to its neighbour, which adds it to its own copy: rank 1 turns chunk 0 into 10 plus 1, 11. Each transfer is 100 megabytes, so at 400 gigabits per second the step takes 2 milliseconds.

@3-4
Steps 2 and 3. Each rank forwards the partial sum it just made one more place round the ring. Rank 2 adds its 100 to make 111, then rank 3 adds the last contribution and holds 1111, the full total. At 6 milliseconds every rank has completed one chunk the same way.

@5
Reduce-scatter is done after 3 steps, n minus 1. Each rank owns one fully reduced chunk and has sent 300 megabytes, three quarters of its data. Stopping here is a collective in its own right, reduce-scatter, which sharded optimisers such as ZeRO and FSDP use.

@6-8
All-gather. Each rank sends its complete chunk to the right, and the receiver overwrites instead of adding. Rank 3's 1111 reaches rank 0, then rank 1, and after the third step, at 12 milliseconds, every rank holds all four totals.

@9
Six steps in all, 2 times n minus 1. Each rank sent 600 megabytes, one and a half times its 400 megabyte vector. That ratio is the factor to remember: 2 times n minus 1, divided by n.

@10
The obvious alternatives cost more. Everyone sending to everyone makes each rank send 1,200 megabytes, and funnelling through rank 0 pushes 1,200 megabytes through one link. At least 24 milliseconds either way, while the ring keeps every link equally busy and finishes in 12.

@11
The factor barely grows: 1.5 for 4 ranks, 1.75 for 8, 1.998 for 1,024, so per-rank traffic stays under twice the data however large the ring. What grows is the number of steps, each paying a fixed latency. That is why NCCL prefers tree or hierarchical algorithms for small messages at scale, and why nccl-tests' small-message rows are judged by their time, not their bandwidth.

@12
Now the two numbers nccl-tests prints. Algorithm bandwidth is size over time: 400 megabytes in 12 milliseconds, 33.3 gigabytes per second. Bus bandwidth multiplies it by the factor, 1.5, giving 50 gigabytes per second, exactly the NIC's rate. Algorithm bandwidth falls as the group grows even on a perfect network; bus bandwidth does not, so it is the one to hold against hardware. On a real Meridian server, eight GPUs leave through eight NICs, so the ceiling is the server's total, 400 gigabytes per second, and a healthy large-message plateau lands at 85 to 95 percent of it. The lesson's two-server run reached 362, 90 percent. Two traps: the average on the summary line mixes latency into bandwidth, so compare plateaus; and any wrong result is a hardware or driver fault that outranks every performance number.
