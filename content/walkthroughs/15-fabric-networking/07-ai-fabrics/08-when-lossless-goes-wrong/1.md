@0
On screen is a slice of Meridian's San Jose back end: two of its eight spines and three of its sixteen leaves, every link 400 gigabits per second, priority 3 lossless everywhere. Hold onto one sentence, because every failure here follows from it: a PFC pause can say "stop priority 3 on this link", and nothing finer. No flow, no destination.

@1
Three RDMA flows start. srv1 at 400 gigabits per second and srv65 at 200 both write to srv3: 600 offered to a port that can send 400, an incast. The third flow, srv66 writing 200 to srv5, is unrelated, and srv5's port is idle. rail1-leaf2 hashes both its flows onto ai-spine1, which fills that uplink exactly.

@2
A switch counts lossless bytes against the port they came in on, because a pause can only go back out of that port. So rail1-leaf1's buffers cross XOFF on both ports feeding srv3, from srv1 and from ai-spine1, and it pauses both. The pause to ai-spine1 stops all priority 3 on that link, srv66's packets for the idle srv5 included.

@3
ai-spine1 can no longer send down to rail1-leaf1, so those packets pile up inside it, charged to the port from rail1-leaf2. That buffer crosses XOFF, and ai-spine1 pauses rail1-leaf2. The congestion has moved one more hop upstream.

@4
rail1-leaf2's buffers from srv65 and srv66 fill, and it pauses both cards. Five pauses now form a tree rooted at srv3's port. The flow table shows srv66 at 0 gigabits per second, waiting, although srv5 is idle: its packets sit behind srv3's in frozen queues. That is head-of-line blocking, and srv66's flow is the victim.

@5
What srv66's owner sees: throughput collapses with zero drops, zero ECN marks at srv5's port, and no errors, while pause counters climb on every link back towards the incast. Only following the pauses hop by hop, rail1-leaf2, ai-spine1, rail1-leaf1, leads to srv3's port.

@6
When the incast ends, or DCQCN slows its senders, the buffers drain, the pause table empties and srv66 is back at 200. Congestion spreading is temporary. The next failure is not.

@7
Later the optic on the ai-spine1 to rail1-leaf1 link fails. ai-spine1 sees the loss of light at once and recomputes first. Its only ways left to rail1-leaf1 go down through another leaf and back up through ai-spine2: valley paths, a down then up turn a healthy Clos never uses. rail1-leaf2's table still lists both spines.

@8
So rail1-leaf2 still hashes srv66's flow to ai-spine1, which sends it straight back down, and up it goes again. The packets ping-pong in a micro-loop, losing 1 from their TTL each pass: 63 going up, 62 coming down. The link is offered srv66's new packets plus every looping one, far more than 400 gigabits per second.

@9
Both directions fill. rail1-leaf2's buffer for bytes from ai-spine1 crosses XOFF, so it pauses ai-spine1; ai-spine1's buffer for bytes from rail1-leaf2 crosses XOFF, so it pauses rail1-leaf2. One link, paused both ways.

@10
Now each switch waits for the other, and the who-waits-for-whom table closes on itself: a cyclic buffer dependency, and a deadlock. Nothing moves again, and the TTL cannot rescue it, because TTL only falls when a packet is forwarded.

@11
Milliseconds later rail1-leaf2 converges and sends rail1-leaf1 traffic via ai-spine2 only. The loop is gone; the deadlock is not, because the stuck packets are queued for ports that stay paused. srv66's old packets still fill its buffer at rail1-leaf2, so it stays paused, and srv65's flow to srv2 on rail 2, still hashed onto ai-spine1, is trapped behind them. A few milliseconds of disagreement left an outage with every link healthy.

@12
The PFC watchdog on each switch notices a priority 3 queue paused, with packets waiting and nothing sent, for longer than its detection time: 200 milliseconds in SONiC's defaults. It declares a storm, ignores further pauses for that queue and drops what it holds. Of the four conditions for deadlock, it removes one: no preemption.

@13
The buffers free, the cycle breaks, srv66 resumes through ai-spine2 and srv65 reaches srv2. After 200 more milliseconds without a storm, the queue is lossless again. The hosts count the cost: out of sequence packets at the receivers, and resends after a NAK, or after an ACK timeout when a burst's tail was dropped.

@14
The general shape: a pause tree pauses each link one way, towards the senders, and ends when its root drains; a deadlock pauses links both ways or around a ring, and ends only when something drops on purpose. Prevention beats recovery: an eBGP underlay in which all spines share one AS rejects valley paths. So alert on a queue paused continuously for milliseconds, on any watchdog event, and on pauses rising around a ring rather than up a tree. Against a 410 millisecond training step, 200 milliseconds of detection is about half a step, paid by every GPU in the job.
