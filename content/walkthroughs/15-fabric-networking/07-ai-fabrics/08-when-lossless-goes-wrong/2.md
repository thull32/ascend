@0
On screen are seven servers on San Jose's rail 1 leaf: six senders, srv002 to srv007, and one receiver, srv001, each on a 400 gigabit per second card. The leaf is non-blocking, so the only place traffic can pile up is an egress port that several senders aim at together. The one idea: incast arrives faster than any feedback loop can react, so what decides the outcome is whether the switch drops or pauses.

@1
At the same instant every sender starts a 1 megabyte transfer to srv001, as in a gather or an all-to-all step. Six times 400 is 2,400 gigabits per second converging on one 400 gigabit port: six to one incast.

@2
Only 400 can leave, so the excess 2,000 gigabits per second piles up in the egress queue at 250 kilobytes every microsecond. After 4 microseconds the queue holds 1 megabyte, and only 0.2 megabytes have reached srv001.

@3
First, a lossy class. At 8 microseconds the queue reaches 2 megabytes, the share of buffer this port may take here; the real limit depends on the chip. The leaf starts tail-dropping: of every six packets that arrive, one finds room and five are discarded.

@4
At 20 microseconds every sender has sent its megabyte and stops. Of the 6 megabytes that arrived, 1 has gone out, 2 sit in the queue, and 3 were dropped.

@5
The queue drains and is empty at 60 microseconds. srv001 received half of what was sent; the 3 dropped megabytes must be sent again.

@6
For RDMA it is worse. A reliable-connected queue pair accepts packets only in order, so after each sender's first loss, about 8 microseconds in, the receiver sends one NAK and discards everything else from that sender until the gap is resent. That is go-back-N. The table shows each sender had 0.4 megabytes accepted and must resend 0.6: 3.6 megabytes in all, more than the switch dropped.

@7
Now the same burst in a lossless class, priority 3 with PFC on every port. The leaf counts what each ingress port has queued against XOFF, 200 kilobytes per port here, an illustrative number; real thresholds come from the chip's buffer, and the headroom above XOFF from cable length, link speed and MTU.

@8
At 4.8 microseconds each of the six ports holds 200 kilobytes, 1.2 megabytes in all, and the leaf sends a pause for priority 3 out of each one: six pauses. The senders stop, and headroom absorbs the bytes already on the cable.

@9
For the rest of the burst the leaf pauses and resumes each sender as its share crosses the threshold. The queue holds flat at 1.2 megabytes on the chart, srv001's link stays full, and each sender averages 66.7 gigabits per second, a sixth of its link, so it is paused 83 percent of the time.

@10
The last sender finishes at 96 microseconds and the queue is empty at 120: 6 megabytes through a 400 gigabit port in exactly the time that port needs, zero drops, nothing to resend.

@11
The price is paid upstream. A paused sender cannot send priority 3 to anyone, even an idle receiver. If these senders sat behind spines, the leaf would pause a spine instead, and that spine would pause every port feeding it: the congestion spreading in the first visualisation of this lesson. ECN with DCQCN exists to slow senders before the queue reaches XOFF.

@12
The summary table shows each side recording a different half. The switch has queue depth, plus egress drops or pauses sent. The cards have out of sequence packets and NAKs, or pauses received. Now scale it: rail1-leaf1 serves 64 servers, so 63 senders to one is 25.2 terabits per second into one port. A lossy queue is full in 0.65 microseconds, before any congestion mark can make a round trip, and PFC parks 12.6 megabytes for one port. ECN converges long transfers; PFC carries every short burst, and pays for it in buffer and in pauses.
