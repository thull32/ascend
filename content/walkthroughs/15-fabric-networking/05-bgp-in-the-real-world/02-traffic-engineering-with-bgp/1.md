@0
Meridian, AS 64500, owns 203.0.113.0 slash 24 and buys transit from two ISPs: gw1 in Amsterdam connects to ISP1, AS 64501, and gw2 connects to ISP2, AS 64502. At the top sits AS 64511, a content network holding 192.0.2.0 slash 24. The goal: ISP1 in both directions, ISP2 only as a backup. Hold onto one asymmetry, because it is the whole answer. Traffic leaving Meridian follows a decision Meridian's routers make, so it can be controlled exactly. Traffic arriving follows decisions every other network makes, so it can only be influenced.

@1
With no policy, gw2 holds two paths to 192.0.2.0 slash 24: its own eBGP route through ISP2, and the iBGP route from gw1 through ISP1. They tie on local preference, 100 each, on AS path length, 2 each, and on origin. MED is not compared, because the paths come from two different neighbouring ASes. The next step, eBGP over iBGP, makes each gateway keep its own ISP, so outbound traffic leaves by whichever gateway is nearer.

@2
The fix is one rule of import policy on gw1: every route learned from ISP1 gets a local preference of 200. iBGP carries it to every router in the AS, and it is the first attribute in the decision process that every implementation shares.

@3
Now gw2 compares 200 with 100 and picks the path through gw1 before it ever reaches the eBGP over iBGP step. Every router in AS 64500 exits via ISP1. Outbound is fully under control, because Meridian makes the decision.

@4
Inbound is another matter. Meridian announces its slash 24 to both ISPs with an AS path of just 64500, so AS 64511 sees two paths of length 2, one through each ISP. Its own final tie-breaker happens to pick ISP2. Traffic leaves by ISP1 and returns by ISP2.

@5
The first tool is prepending. gw2 adds its own AS twice more to what it sends ISP2, so the update carries 64500, 64500, 64500. The path is still valid; it just loses wherever the length comparison is reached.

@6
AS 64511 now compares length 2 through ISP1 with length 4 through ISP2, and moves to ISP1. MED could not have done this: it is compared only between paths from the same neighbouring AS, for choosing between several links to one ISP.

@7
But prepending has a limit, and ISP2 shows it. ISP2 gives its paying customers' routes a local preference of 200 and peers' routes 100, so Meridian's prepended route sits at 200 and the path via its peer ISP1 at 100. Local preference is compared before path length, so ISP2 keeps the direct path, and everything ISP2's own customers send still arrives at gw2. Prepending only moves networks whose local preference ties.

@8
The second tool reaches that earlier step: the upstream's own communities. ISP2 publishes that a customer route tagged 64502 colon 70 gets a local preference of 70 inside AS 64502, as real transit providers do; one large transit network, AS 3356, documents the same thing. gw2 adds the tag, the direct route drops to 70, below the peer path at 100, and ISP2 itself sends Meridian's traffic over its peering with ISP1.

@9
The third tool acts before any BGP attribute is compared: more-specific prefixes. gw1 also announces the two halves, 203.0.113.0 slash 25 and 203.0.113.128 slash 25, to ISP1 only, while the slash 24 still goes to both. In AS 64511's forwarding table for 203.0.113.10, the slash 25 via ISP1 is the longest match and wins, whatever local preference or path length say. One caveat: most networks drop IPv4 routes longer than a slash 24, so in production this trick is two slash 24s carved from a slash 23.

@10
Now the failure the design exists for. The fibre between gw1 and ISP1 is cut. Both ends see loss of light and drop the eBGP session at once. A failure that left the light on would be noticed only by BFD or by the hold timer: 90 seconds as RFC 4271 suggests, 180 seconds on many platforms.

@11
Outbound first. gw1 withdraws its ISP1 routes over iBGP, the paths at local preference 200 vanish, and gw2's own route through ISP2, at 100, becomes best. Outbound traffic moves within seconds.

@12
Inbound follows more slowly. ISP1 withdraws the two slash 25s and the slash 24 it learned from Meridian, and every network falls back to the prepended slash 24 via ISP2; at AS 64511 that is the path of length 4. Prepending and a local preference of 70 make a path less preferred, never unusable, so the backup carries everything once it is the only path left. The withdrawals take seconds to minutes to spread, as the convergence lesson shows.

@13
The closing table is the answer. Outbound, Meridian decides, and local preference gives it exact control. Inbound, every other AS decides, and Meridian has three levers, each acting at a different step of someone else's decision: prepending at path length, an upstream's community at that upstream's own local preference, and a more-specific at longest-prefix match, before BGP compares anything. The earlier the step, the stronger the tool. None is a guarantee, so measure where inbound traffic actually arrives, per ISP, and prove the backup with a planned failover drill before you need it.
