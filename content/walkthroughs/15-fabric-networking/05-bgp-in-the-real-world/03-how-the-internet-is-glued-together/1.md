@0
Six networks and two kinds of link. At the top, the transit provider, AS 64496. Below it, ISP1 and ISP2, AS 64501 and 64502, each a customer of the transit provider and joined to each other by a peering link. Meridian, AS 64500, buys transit from both ISPs. The content network, AS 64511, buys from ISP2, and at the bottom AS 64505 is Meridian's own customer. On a transit link the lower network pays the upper one to reach the whole internet. On the peering link, ISP1 and ISP2 swap customer traffic for free.

@1
Hold onto one sentence, because it is the whole rule: a network carries traffic for others only when someone pays it to. The table on screen is the Gao-Rexford export rule. Routes learned from a customer go to everyone. Routes learned from a peer or a provider go only to customers. The second table is the import side: prefer customer routes, local preference 200, then peer routes at 150, then provider routes at 100, whatever the path length.

@2
AS 64505 announces 198.51.100.0 slash 24 to Meridian. For Meridian that is a customer route, so it sends it to both of its providers with the AS path 64500, 64505. Carrying traffic towards a paying customer is the job.

@3
ISP1 and ISP2 also learned the route from a customer, Meridian, so each sends it everywhere: up to the transit provider, across to its peer, and down to its own customers. A customer route climbs as high as the hierarchy goes and is visible from the whole internet.

@4
Now the other direction. The content network announces 192.0.2.0 slash 24 to its provider, ISP2. It is a customer route for ISP2, so ISP2 sends it to the transit provider, to its peer ISP1 and to its customer Meridian, each with the AS path 64502, 64511.

@5
Here is the first restriction biting. ISP1 learned that prefix from a peer, so it passes it only down to its customer Meridian, and not up to the transit provider. Sending it up would mean carrying its peer's traffic unpaid. Meridian now holds two provider routes, both at local preference 100: 64502, 64511 through ISP2, and the longer 64501, 64502, 64511 through ISP1. Equal preference, so the shorter path wins, through ISP2.

@6
So what may Meridian send to ISP1? Its own prefix, 203.0.113.0 slash 24, and its customer's 198.51.100.0 slash 24. The content network's prefix, learned from its other provider, ISP2, must not be sent. In practice the filter does not list prefixes: it matches the "learned from a customer" community that Meridian stamps on routes at import, which the policy and communities lesson covers.

@7
Look at the shape of the paths this allows. Zero or more links up, from customer to provider, at most one peering link across, then only down. A mountain. Traffic from AS 64505 to the content network goes up to Meridian, up to ISP2, and down to AS 64511.

@8
Now the leak. A policy change on Meridian's session to ISP1 drops the export filter. Meridian announces 192.0.2.0 slash 24, a route from its other provider, to ISP1, with the AS path 64500, 64502, 64511.

@9
ISP1 compares two routes. From its customer Meridian, local preference 200. From its peer ISP2, 150. Local preference is compared before path length, so ISP1 picks the leaked route even though it is one network longer. And because it is now a customer route, ISP1 sends it up to the transit provider too. There, both paths are customer routes at 200, so path length decides: 64502, 64511 through ISP2 beats the four-network path through ISP1, and the leak stops spreading.

@10
Watch the traffic. Everything ISP1 sends to the content network now goes down into Meridian and back up to ISP2: a valley in the mountain. Meridian's two transit links fill with traffic that is not its own, which it pays both providers to carry, and what does not fit is dropped.

@11
The filter is restored, Meridian withdraws the route, and ISP1 returns to its peer path. The leak needed two mistakes: Meridian's export, and ISP1 accepting from a customer a prefix that customer never registered. The guard rails close both doors. On Meridian's export, send only its own and customer-tagged routes. On ISP1's import, accept only the customer's registered prefixes, with a maximum prefix limit on the session. And on both ends, BGP roles with the Only-to-Customer attribute from RFC 9234, so a route that has come down or across can only keep going down. In June 2019 that same pair of mistakes degraded parts of Cloudflare and other large networks for about two hours. The general lesson: export policy is where the internet's economics live, preference for customer routes amplifies any leak, and the signal is your own routes to a provider or peer containing anything but your prefixes and your customers'.
