@0
Meridian's border gateway gw1, in Amsterdam, is going out of service for a software upgrade. On screen: users in AS 64511 at the top, ISP1 and ISP2 below them, the two gateways gw1 and gw2, two spines, and leaf1, which holds Meridian's public servers, 203.0.113.0 slash 26. Today gw1 carries all of Meridian's ISP1 traffic, in and out. Hold onto one rule, because this run breaks it: never leave a router attracting traffic it can no longer deliver. This is the wrong order, the IGP shut before BGP.

@1
gw1 leans on two protocols, and they fail differently. It announces the whole slash 24 to ISP1 from a static discard route, a hand-made route that drops anything no more specific route claims, so the announcement needs nothing else to stay up. But to reach the servers it follows a BGP route for the slash 26 whose next hop, leaf1's loopback 10.255.1.11, only IS-IS can resolve. ISP1 holds two paths to Meridian: from its customer gw1 at a local preference of 200, and through its peer ISP2, prepended, at 100. It uses gw1.

@2
Step one, done backwards. gw1's IS-IS adjacencies to both spines are shut while its BGP is left untouched. Setting only the overload bit would have kept gw1's own IS-IS routes; shutting the adjacencies removes every one of them. The next hop 10.255.1.11 can no longer be resolved, so the slash 26 leaves gw1's forwarding table, marked unresolvable. The slash 24 discard route stays, and so does the eBGP session to ISP1.

@3
Outbound heals by itself. Every other router sees gw1's loopback, 10.255.1.201, vanish from IS-IS, so every BGP route with that next hop becomes unusable. gw2's own routes through ISP2 take over, the route reflectors pass them on, and leaf1 sends its traffic up through spine2 to gw2. The cost is seconds to tens of seconds of loss, depending on how many routes gw2 must re-send.

@4
Inbound does not heal, because nothing on the inbound side depends on IS-IS. The eBGP session runs over the direct link to ISP1 and stays Established. The slash 24 comes from a static route, so it stays announced. ISP1 still prefers its customer at 200. So a packet for 203.0.113.10 still arrives at gw1.

@5
Inside gw1, the longest match for 203.0.113.10 is now the slash 24 discard route, and the packet is dropped. So is every other packet ISP1 delivers, which is nearly all of Meridian's inbound internet traffic. This is the BGP black hole once more: a router advertising a destination it cannot reach.

@6
Now look at what a protocol dashboard shows. The eBGP session is Established. One prefix advertised to ISP1, as usual. IS-IS on gw1 is down, which is what the maintenance plan says. The ISP1 link shows normal inbound traffic, because packets arrive before they are thrown away. Only two signals show the outage: the discard counter on gw1, climbing, and a probe to 203.0.113.10 from outside the network, failing. On the night traced here, that lasted 45 minutes, until a customer reported Meridian unreachable.

@7
The fix is the order: withdraw BGP first, prove nothing arrives, and cost out the IGP last, so a router never attracts traffic it can no longer deliver. Each protocol here did exactly what it was designed to do; the combination black-holed the traffic. The same trap opens with no human mistake at all if both of gw1's uplinks fail. So build the guard rails: announce the aggregate only while the routes behind it are present, probe 203.0.113.10 through each ISP from outside, and have change tooling check the order of the steps.
