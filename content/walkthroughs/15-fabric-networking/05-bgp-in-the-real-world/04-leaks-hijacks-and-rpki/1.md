@0
On screen is Meridian's corner of the internet. Meridian, AS 64500, announces 203.0.113.0 slash 24 through ISP1, AS 64501, and ISP2, AS 64502. Both buy transit from AS 64496, which carries traffic from the content network, AS 64511, towards Meridian. AS 64496 has one more customer, AS 64510, which has nothing to do with Meridian. Hold onto one idea, because it is the whole attack: forwarding uses the longest matching prefix, and BGP's best-path rules only ever compare routes for the same prefix.

@1
Normally AS 64496 holds two paths to Meridian's slash 24, one via ISP1 with the AS path 64501 64500, and one via ISP2 with 64502 64500. Both end in the origin, AS 64500, and a tie-breaker picks the one via ISP1. A packet from the content network to Meridian's web server, 203.0.113.10, follows it.

@2
Now AS 64510 announces 203.0.113.0 slash 25, the lower half of Meridian's block, with itself as the origin. A typo, or an internal route that escaped. The UPDATE carries that prefix, an AS path of just 64510, and AS 64510's own address as the next hop. The documentation ranges here are single slash 24s, so the example uses a slash 25; on the real internet most networks drop IPv4 routes longer than slash 24.

@3
AS 64496 applies no prefix filter to this customer. A filter built from the prefixes the customer registered in an internet routing registry would have stopped it. So it accepts the slash 25, adds it to its table as a third route, and passes it on to the content network and its other neighbours. Nothing about it conflicts with the slash 24. To BGP a different prefix is a different route, and the two are never compared.

@4
Here is where it bites. Every router that accepted the slash 25 now has two entries containing 203.0.113.10: the slash 24 and the slash 25. The lookup takes the longest, the slash 25, so AS path length and local preference never get a say. In February 2008, Pakistan Telecom's slash 24 inside YouTube's slash 22 took YouTube offline worldwide for about two hours in exactly this way.

@5
Traffic for 203.0.113.10 now goes to AS 64510, which has no such server and drops it. Traffic for 203.0.113.200, in the upper half, is unaffected and still reaches Meridian via ISP1. Meridian's own sessions and announcements are all unchanged. What it sees is half its inbound traffic vanishing, and only public route collectors, such as RIPE's and Route Views, show who is announcing its space.

@6
The defence is RPKI. Meridian publishes a route origin authorisation, a ROA, with its regional internet registry: a signed statement that 203.0.113.0 slash 24, and nothing longer than slash 24, may be originated only by AS 64500. The signature chains up to the registry's trust anchor, so anyone can verify it.

@7
AS 64496 runs a validator, software such as Routinator, which downloads every published ROA, checks the signatures, and sends the validated list to its routers over the RPKI-to-Router protocol, the dashed link on screen. The routers never fetch or verify certificates themselves.

@8
Each route is now checked against that list. The slash 24 from AS 64500 is Valid. The slash 25 is Invalid twice over: its origin is AS 64510, not AS 64500, and even the right origin could not use a slash 25 when the max length is 24. The third row, 192.0.2.0 slash 24 from the content network, is covered by no ROA, so it is Not Found, and it is still accepted, because not every network publishes ROAs.

@9
AS 64496's import policy rejects Invalid routes. It drops the slash 25, and sends a withdrawal for it to the content network and its other neighbours, which had already received it.

@10
With the slash 25 gone, the slash 24 is again the longest match for 203.0.113.10, and traffic reaches Meridian via ISP1. AS 64510 can keep announcing the slash 25, but every network that drops Invalid routes ignores it.

@11
The last table shows what origin validation checks, and what it does not. It checks the origin and the prefix length, never the path. A slash 25 with Meridian forged as the origin, the AS path 64510 64500, is still Invalid, because of the max length of 24. That is why a ROA should not allow lengths you never announce. But the exact slash 24 with the same forged origin is Valid, and catching that needs a path check, ASPA or BGPsec. So the pattern to remember: a more-specific beats every defence based on path length, origin validation stops it only where networks between you and the attacker validate, and a tight max length is what keeps a forged origin on a more-specific Invalid.
