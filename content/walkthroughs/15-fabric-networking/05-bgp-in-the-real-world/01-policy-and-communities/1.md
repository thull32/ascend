@0
Meridian's Amsterdam edge: two border gateways, gw1 and gw2, in AS 64500 and joined by iBGP. Below gw1 is a customer, AS 64505, which pays Meridian. Above gw1 is the transit provider ISP1, AS 64501, which Meridian pays. Beside gw2 is a peer, the content network AS 64511, which exchanges traffic for free. Hold onto one sentence, because it is the whole design: tag a route once, where it enters, and make every later decision by matching the tag.

@1
The tags are communities: 32-bit values a route carries in a list, written as two 16-bit halves, an AS number and a value. So 64500 colon 100 is 64500, F B F 4 in hex, followed by 100. A network with a 4-byte AS number uses large communities instead, three 32-bit parts. Meridian's plan: 64500 colon 100 for learned from a customer, colon 200 for a peer, colon 300 for transit. The fourth row is no export, 65535 colon 65281, a well-known value every BGP implementation must obey: never send this route to another AS.

@2
The customer announces its block, 198.51.100.0 slash 24, with an AS path of just 64505 and no communities. Everything Meridian decides about it happens in policy, not in the update.

@3
gw1's import policy for this session is a match, then set actions. The prefix lies inside the customer's registered block, at a length of up to slash 25, so the route is accepted, tagged 64500 colon 100, and given a local preference of 200, which makes a customer route beat any other path to the same prefix.

@4-5
The customer also sends a more-specific, 198.51.100.128 slash 25, marked no export by the customer itself. It wants Meridian to use the slash 25 internally, to choose which of its links carries that half, without the route spreading to the internet. The same term accepts it and adds 64500 colon 100. Communities are a list, so it now carries both tags.

@6
On gw2 the peer announces 192.0.2.0 slash 24. The peering import policy tags it 64500 colon 200 with local preference 150: above transit, below customers.

@7
ISP1 sends gw1 the full internet table, roughly a million IPv4 prefixes. Apart from bogons and Meridian's own 203.0.113.0 slash 24, every route is tagged 64500 colon 300 with local preference 100. A million routes have become one group that later policy can match with a single tag.

@8
Over iBGP each gateway gives the other the best paths it learned from outside, communities and local preference intact, so both hold the same table. Several platforms send no communities until the session is told to, the classic way for a tag to go missing. No export allows this: it forbids sending the route out of AS 64500, not around inside it.

@9
Now export, and here is why the tag matters. gw2 never learned the customer route itself; over iBGP it sees a prefix, an AS path and a next hop, and nothing in them says customer. The tag says it. gw2's policy towards the peer: send Meridian's own slash 24 and anything tagged 64500 colon 100, deny the rest. The peer route and the full table are denied. The slash 25 is held back whatever the policy says, because the router checks no export before export policy runs.

@10
gw2 sends the peer two updates: 203.0.113.0 slash 24 with an AS path of 64500, and 198.51.100.0 slash 24 with 64500, 64505. It removes Meridian's internal tags on the way out, since they mean nothing to another network.

@11
gw1 sends ISP1 the same two routes. Denying 64500 colon 200 matters here: offered a path to the peer's 192.0.2.0 slash 24, ISP1 could send that traffic across the link Meridian pays for, and Meridian would be giving ISP1 free transit to its peer.

@12
Towards the customer the rule is the opposite, because a customer pays for the whole internet. gw1 sends the full table, the peer's route and Meridian's own. It skips the customer's own slash 24, which the customer would reject anyway because its AS is already in the path, and the slash 25 stays in again: a customer is another AS too.

@13
The summary: the peer and ISP1 each received Meridian's block and the customer's, the customer everything but its own routes, and the no export slash 25 stayed inside AS 64500 without any prefix list naming it. Each route was classified once, and every export decision matched one tag. That is why almost every multi-homed network works this way: the exporting router has lost the context, a million routes become one class, a new customer changes one import filter, and the rules can be audited as a query over what each neighbour is sent. These exports are allow-lists on tags, which fail closed. The trap is a tag that never arrives, or a strip that removes too much or too little: the sessions stay up and the policy quietly does the wrong thing.
