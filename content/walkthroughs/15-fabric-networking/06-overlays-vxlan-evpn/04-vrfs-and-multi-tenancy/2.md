@0
The same two Acme hosts in the same small Amsterdam example: acme-web1, 10.1.1.10, in VLAN 100 and VNI 10100 behind leaf 1, and acme-db1, 10.1.2.20, in VLAN 200 and VNI 10101 behind leaf 3. The difference is in the table. Each leaf now carries only its local subnet, plus VRF ACME's L3VNI, 50001, a VXLAN segment used only for traffic routed between leaves. Hold onto one idea: the VNI that crosses the fabric names the tenant's routing table on the far side, not the destination subnet, so the packet is routed once at each end.

@1-2
The start is identical. 10.1.2.20 is off acme-web1's subnet, so it sends its TCP SYN to the gateway, 10.1.1.1, at the anycast gateway MAC ending in 01 01, with a time to live of 64. The frame enters leaf 1 in VLAN 100, addressed to a MAC that leaf 1 owns, so leaf 1 routes it.

@3
VLAN 100's gateway belongs to VRF ACME, so leaf 1 looks up 10.1.2.20 in that VRF's table. The table has a subnet route for 10.1.2.0 slash 24 from a type 5 route, but the longest match is the host route, 10.1.2.20 slash 32, installed from leaf 3's type 2 route. That route is on screen: its route distinguisher is leaf 3's loopback followed by 10101, its two labels are 10101 for bridging and 50001 for routing, and its Router MAC extended community carries leaf 3's router MAC, ending in 53 81. The next hop is the VTEP 10.255.1.13, over L3VNI 50001.

@4
leaf 1 routes. The time to live goes from 64 to 63 and the checksum is recomputed. Now the inner Ethernet header runs from leaf 1's own router MAC, ending in 53 80, to leaf 3's router MAC, ending in 53 81. On the L3VNI the inner frame is addressed to the egress leaf, not to acme-db1, so leaf 1 never needs an ARP entry for acme-db1.

@5
leaf 1 encapsulates in VNI 50001, Acme's L3VNI, not 10101. Outer UDP to port 4789 and an outer IP header from 10.255.1.11 to 10.255.1.13 follow, 50 bytes in all, and the underlay hash picks spine 1.

@6-7
To the underlay this is a 110 byte UDP packet with an outer time to live of 64. Spine 1 routes on the outer destination alone, drops the outer time to live to 63 and writes new outer MACs. The frame inside, in VNI 50001, is untouched; the spine holds no Acme routes.

@8
leaf 3 owns the outer destination, strips the 50 bytes and reads VNI 50001. Its VNI map ties 50001 to VRF ACME, not to a VLAN, and the inner destination MAC is its own router MAC, so it routes the packet again, in VRF ACME. There, 10.1.2.20 is a local host on VLAN 200, with acme-db1's MAC from leaf 3's own ARP table.

@9
leaf 3 decrements the time to live to 62, recomputes the checksum, and writes the last Ethernet header, from VLAN 200's gateway MAC, ending in 01 01, to acme-db1's MAC, ending in 53 14.

@10
acme-db1 receives the SYN with a time to live of 62. It was routed twice, once at each leaf, so a traceroute from acme-web1 shows two hops before acme-db1, leaf 1 and leaf 3, and never a spine. Both leaves answer from addresses in VRF ACME, often the anycast gateway, so the hop addresses alone may not say which leaf it was.

@11
That is symmetric IRB: routed at the ingress leaf, carried in L3VNI 50001, routed again at the egress leaf, and the reply does exactly the same in reverse, also in 50001. No leaf needs a remote subnet's VLAN, VNI or MAC table; it needs one L3VNI per tenant, host routes and router MACs. It does not win on speed, and it does not shrink the routing table, because remote host routes remain. It wins on state. In the lesson's example of 20 subnets over 16 leaves, each leaf holds 5 local subnets and 240 MAC entries instead of 960, the hosts in subnets it does not serve share one rewrite entry per remote leaf, and a new subnet is a change on its 4 leaves, not all 16. That is why large EVPN fabrics use it. The trap is the L3VNI itself: it must be the same on every leaf. A leaf that maps VRF ACME to a different VNI sends routed packets no other leaf can place, and they are dropped while every BGP session stays up.
