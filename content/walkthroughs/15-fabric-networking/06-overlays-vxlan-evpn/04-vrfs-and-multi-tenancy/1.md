@0
On screen is a small Amsterdam example with two spines. acme-web1, at 10.1.1.10 in VLAN 100, which Meridian carries as VNI 10100, sits behind leaf 1. acme-db1, at 10.1.2.20 in VLAN 200, which is VNI 10101, sits behind leaf 3. The two hosts are in different subnets, so some leaf must route between them. Hold onto one idea: in asymmetric IRB, every leaf with Acme hosts carries every Acme subnet, with its gateway and its VNI, so the ingress leaf can route straight into the destination's subnet and then simply bridge across. There is no L3VNI at all.

@1
10.1.2.20 is outside acme-web1's own subnet, so its routing table sends the TCP SYN to its default gateway, 10.1.1.1. Its ARP cache maps that gateway to the anycast gateway MAC, ending in 01 01, which every Acme leaf owns. The packet leaves with a time to live of 64.

@2
The frame enters leaf 1 on an access port in VLAN 100. Its destination MAC is one that leaf 1 owns itself, so leaf 1 routes the packet instead of bridging it.

@3
Here is the asymmetric part. leaf 1 has a gateway on 10.1.2.0 slash 24 as well, so in VRF ACME the destination is directly connected, on VLAN 200. Its ARP table already maps 10.1.2.20 to acme-db1's MAC, ending in 53 14, filled from leaf 3's type 2 route. So leaf 1 can route as if acme-db1 were plugged into one of its own ports.

@4
leaf 1 routes into VLAN 200. The time to live drops from 64 to 63 and the header checksum is recomputed. The new Ethernet header runs from the VLAN 200 gateway MAC to acme-db1's own MAC. From here on the packet is only bridged, so this is already the frame acme-db1 will receive.

@5
Now leaf 1 bridges in VNI 10101. Its MAC table for that segment puts acme-db1's MAC behind the VTEP 10.255.1.13, leaf 3's loopback, so it encapsulates in VXLAN with VNI 10101, the destination subnet's L2VNI. Outer UDP to port 4789 and an outer IP header from 10.255.1.11 to 10.255.1.13 follow, 50 bytes in all. IS-IS offers two equal next hops, and the underlay hash picks spine 1.

@6-7
To the underlay this is a 110 byte UDP packet from leaf 1 to leaf 3, with an outer time to live of 64. Spine 1 routes it on the outer destination alone, drops the outer time to live to 63 and rewrites the outer MACs. It holds no Acme routes and has no idea that VRF ACME exists.

@8
leaf 3 owns the outer destination, strips the 50 bytes and reads VNI 10101, which its VNI map ties to VLAN 200. The inner destination MAC, acme-db1's, is on a local port, so leaf 3 simply bridges the frame: no routing lookup and no change to the time to live.

@9
acme-db1 receives the SYN with a time to live of 63. It was routed once, at leaf 1, so a traceroute from acme-web1 shows leaf 1 and then acme-db1.

@10
That is asymmetric IRB: routed at the ingress, bridged in the destination's L2VNI. The reply is routed at leaf 3 into VLAN 100 and travels in VNI 10100, so the two directions use different VNIs, which is where the name comes from. It works and the path is direct, but look at what each leaf must hold: both VNIs, both gateways, and the MAC and ARP entry of every remote host. In the lesson's example of Acme with 20 subnets over 16 leaves, every leaf carries all 20 subnets and 960 MAC and ARP entries, and adding a subnet is a change on every tenant leaf. That state is what stops it scaling.
