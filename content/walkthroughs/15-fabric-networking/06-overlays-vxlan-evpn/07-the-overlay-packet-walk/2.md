@0
The failure version. Two tables for leaf 2 in Amsterdam, tenant Globex: what the source of truth says, and what is actually running. An engineer set up Globex by copying Acme's tenant config from leaf 1 and changed the VNIs to 10200 and 50002, but not the route distinguishers and route targets. So leaf 2 now uses leaf 1's route distinguishers, 10.255.1.11 colon 10100 and 10.255.1.11 colon 50001, and Acme's route targets, 65000 colon 10100 and 65000 colon 50001. Hold onto the rule this breaks: the route distinguisher must be unique per leaf per segment, and the route target unique per tenant segment. The distinguisher only keeps routes distinct. The target decides who imports them.

@1
Every health check is green. All BGP sessions are Established, IS-IS adjacencies are up, there are no interface errors, and the config push reported success. Nothing in that list looks at what the routes say.

@2
globex-app1, at 10.1.1.10, with the MAC ending in 53 1A, comes up on leaf 2 in Amsterdam. The leaf advertises a type 2 route with the copied distinguisher, 10.255.1.11 colon 10100, and Acme's route target, 65000 colon 10100, while label 1 correctly says VNI 10200. To every other router, this is an Acme route.

@3
The route reflector, rr1 in Amsterdam, reflects it like any other route. Reflectors check loops, not ownership. The duplicate distinguisher hides nothing yet, because a type 2 route's key includes the MAC, and acme-web1's MAC is different, so both routes travel on. The route target is what does the damage.

@4
Leaf 2 in Singapore imports the route, because 65000 colon 10100 is exactly the target its Acme VNI 10100 imports. Acme's segment now holds a Globex MAC, behind VTEP 10.255.1.12, with VNI 10200. Two MACs now claim 10.1.1.10 in one segment. The ARP suppression table keeps one binding, and here it is the newer one, globex-app1's.

@5
acme-web2 ARPs for its colleague acme-web1 at 10.1.1.10, and leaf 2's suppression table answers with Globex's MAC, ending in 53 1A. acme-web2 has no way to tell.

@6
acme-web2 sends its data to that MAC. Leaf 2 in Singapore tunnels it to VTEP 10.255.1.12 with VNI 10200, the VNI the leaked route carries in label 1. The packet crosses from Singapore to Amsterdam inside a perfectly valid VXLAN header. A platform that always uses its own VNI would send 10100 instead, which leaf 2 in Amsterdam does not have, and drop it: a black hole instead of a leak.

@7
Leaf 2 in Amsterdam decapsulates VNI 10200 into Globex's VLAN 100 and delivers the frame to globex-app1. Acme's traffic is now on another customer's server, and every BGP session is still Established.

@8
None of the usual alarms fire, so the platform has to look at route content. One IP with two MACs in one VNI. One route distinguisher arriving from two different next hops. A jump in a tenant's route count. A route whose VNI differs from the VNI importing it. And a running config that differs from the source of truth. Each of these is cheap to compute from data the fabric already exports.

@9
The duplicate distinguisher bites where the keys do collide. Both tenants announce 10.1.1.0 slash 24 as a type 5 route, and with the same distinguisher, 10.255.1.11 colon 50001, the reflector sees one route from two next hops. It picks one best path and reflects only that. Here the router ID step of best-path selection, the lower originator ID, 10.255.1.11, picks Acme's, and Globex's prefix silently disappears from every other leaf. A copied target leaks type 2 routes; a copied distinguisher hides type 5 routes.

@10
The checklist runs from the outside in. Confirm the scope: which tenant, which hosts, one site or several. Rule out the underlay. Read the EVPN routes for the affected IP and MAC: distinguisher, target, next hop and VNI label. Check the leaves' local tables. Then compare distinguisher, target and VNI with the source of truth. Restoring leaf 2's own distinguishers, 10.255.1.12 colon 10200 and 10.255.1.12 colon 50002, and Globex's route targets fixes it. Generating tenant config from intent, and rejecting a duplicate distinguisher or target before deployment, stops it from happening again. The general lesson: Established means routes can flow, not that they are right, so a platform has to read what the routes say.
