@0
This is the reason BUM traffic is small in an EVPN fabric. On screen, Meridian's overlay control plane in a small example: leaf1 and leaf2 in Amsterdam are clients of the route reflector rr1 Amsterdam, leaf2 in Singapore is a client of rr1 Singapore, and the two reflectors peer with each other. The dashed lines are iBGP sessions in AS 65000, on TCP port 179. leaf2 Amsterdam carries only Globex. Hold onto one idea: a MAC learned on one port becomes one BGP route, and every leaf that receives it can answer an ARP for that host without flooding anything.

@1
acme-web1, at 10.1.1.10, boots and ARPs for its gateway, 10.1.1.1. The broadcast's source MAC ends in 53 0A, and the ARP sender fields pair that MAC with 10.1.1.10.

@2
leaf1 Amsterdam learns two facts from that one frame: the MAC ending in 53 0A is on port 1 in VLAN 100, which is VNI 10100, and 10.1.1.10 belongs to that MAC. The gateway address is the anycast gateway every Acme leaf owns, so leaf1 answers the ARP itself, with the gateway MAC ending in 01 01.

@3
leaf1 turns what it learned into an EVPN type-2 route, a MAC and IP advertisement. Its route distinguisher is the leaf's loopback, 10.255.1.11, followed by the VNI, 10100, so the route is unique across the fabric. It carries the MAC, the IP 10.1.1.10, VNI 10100 for bridging, L3VNI 50001 for routing, and its own VTEP, 10.255.1.11, as next hop. Two route targets, 65000 with 10100 and 65000 with 50001, say which leaves should import it.

@4
The update goes to rr1 Amsterdam, and an identical copy to rr2. Without reflectors, leaf1 would need a session to every other leaf, because an iBGP speaker never passes on a route it learned from another iBGP peer.

@5
rr1 adds two attributes before reflecting: the originator ID, 10.255.1.11, who first sent it, and the cluster list, 10.255.1.101, its own cluster ID, so a copy that loops back is recognised and dropped. It leaves the next hop alone and installs nothing for forwarding. A reflector has no VNIs and sits outside the data path.

@6-7
It reflects the route to its other clients, leaf2 Amsterdam among them, and to its peer rr1 Singapore. Each receiver decides for itself whether the route concerns it. leaf2 Amsterdam imports only Globex's route targets, 65000 with 10200 and 65000 with 50002. Neither matches, so it discards the route: Globex never learns an Acme MAC, even though both tenants use 10.1.1.0 slash 24.

@8
rr1 Singapore learned the route from a non-client, so it reflects it only to its own clients, and prepends its cluster ID: the cluster list now reads 10.255.2.101, then 10.255.1.101. The next hop is still leaf1's 10.255.1.11. That session crosses the Amsterdam to Singapore circuit, about 52 and a half milliseconds one way.

@9
leaf2 Singapore imports it: 65000 with 10100 matches its VNI 10100, and the next hop resolves through the IS-IS underlay. It installs the MAC ending in 53 0A behind VTEP 10.255.1.11, and the host route 10.1.1.10 slash 32 in VRF ACME. Every other leaf with VNI 10100 does the same from its own copy.

@10
Later, acme-web2 wants to talk to 10.1.1.10 and broadcasts an ARP request for it. leaf2 Singapore intercepts it, because its ARP suppression table, filled from that same type-2 route, already pairs 10.1.1.10 with the MAC ending in 53 0A.

@11
leaf2 answers on acme-web1's behalf. The request is never replicated: no copy crosses the fabric or the long-haul circuit, and acme-web1 never sees it. Zero ARP requests were flooded into VNI 10100.

@12
acme-web2's first frame hits the installed entry and is tunnelled straight to VTEP 10.255.1.11. That is the pattern: one MAC learned on one port, one BGP route, and every leaf that needs it knows where the host lives before anyone sends it a frame. What is left to flood is the ARP for an address no leaf has learned yet, DHCP, gratuitous ARP, unknown unicast and real tenant multicast, and that is the traffic the rest of this lesson has to deliver.
