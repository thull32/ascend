@0
An illustrative MPLS backbone from Singapore to Amsterdam, the design a carrier would build; Meridian itself runs VXLAN. At each end is a provider edge router, a PE, holding VRF ACME. In the middle are two provider routers, the P routers, joined by the long-haul circuit, and all they do is switch labels. acme-web2, at 10.1.1.11, will send to acme-db1, at 10.1.2.20. Hold onto one idea: the edge puts two labels in front of the packet, one that says which PE to reach and one that says which tenant, and the core reads only the outer one.

@1
First the transport labels are agreed, and LDP hands them out from the destination back towards the sources. The Amsterdam PE tells the Amsterdam P router: for my loopback, 10.255.1.241 slash 32, use label 3. Label 3 is implicit null, a request to send such packets with no transport label at all, so the Amsterdam P router will pop it. That is penultimate-hop popping.

@2
The Amsterdam P router picks a local label of its own, 24031, for the same forwarding equivalence class, everything heading for that loopback, and advertises it upstream to the Singapore P router. Its entry reads: in 24031, pop, send to the Amsterdam PE.

@3
The Singapore P router does the same with label 24017: in 24017, swap to 24031. Each label means something only to the router that chose it, and the chain of swaps from one PE to the other is a label-switched path, an LSP.

@4
Separately, multiprotocol BGP carries the tenant route. The Amsterdam PE advertises a VPNv4 route for 10.1.2.0 slash 24, with route distinguisher 10.255.1.241 colon 50001, route target 65000 colon 50001, next hop its own loopback, and VPN label 92001, which it allocated to mean: look this up in VRF ACME. This is the ancestor of EVPN: the same route distinguisher and route target, with the label playing the part of the VNI.

@5
The Singapore PE imports the route into VRF ACME because of the route target, and resolves the next hop, 10.255.1.241, to the LSP towards Amsterdam. To reach 10.1.2.0 slash 24 it must push VPN label 92001 and, above it, transport label 24017.

@6
Now the data plane. acme-web2 sends a TCP SYN to 10.1.2.20, port 5432, with a time to live of 64, to its gateway on the Singapore PE: an ordinary IPv4 packet.

@7-8
The Singapore PE looks up 10.1.2.20 in VRF ACME, decrements the IP time to live to 63, and pushes two labels: VPN label 92001 at the bottom of the stack, with its bottom-of-stack bit set, and transport label 24017 on top. Both carry 63, copied from IP, which is the uniform model. The EtherType becomes 8847, MPLS, and the addition is 8 bytes, 4 per label. From here on, nothing in the core looks at 10.1.2.20 until the Amsterdam PE.

@9-10
The Singapore P router reads only the top label. It swaps 24017 for 24031, decrements the label's time to live to 62, and sends it across the long-haul circuit: about 10,500 kilometres of fibre, roughly 52.5 milliseconds one way. It never reads the VPN label or the IP header, so it needs no Acme routes, just as a spine needs none for VXLAN.

@11-12
The Amsterdam P router is the penultimate hop. The Amsterdam PE asked for implicit null, so instead of swapping, it pops the transport label and copies its time to live, 61, into the VPN label now on top. Only 92001 crosses the last link, its bottom-of-stack bit telling the PE that IP follows. That saves the PE a lookup.

@13-14
The Amsterdam PE finds 92001 in its own table: pop it, and route in VRF ACME, where 10.1.2.0 slash 24 is connected. The IP time to live ends at 60, one less per router, and a fresh Ethernet header carries the packet to acme-db1, EtherType 0800 again. With the uniform model a traceroute shows all four routers; many carriers switch to the pipe model to hide their core.

@15
Step back, and the two designs are one idea: the edge adds a tenant identifier and an address for the far edge, and the core forwards on the outer part alone. In MPLS those are VPN label 92001 and transport labels 24017 and 24031; in EVPN over VXLAN, the VNI and an outer IP header to the VTEP. The difference is cost and what the core must run: 8 bytes here against VXLAN's 50, but MPLS and LDP or RSVP-TE on every core router, where VXLAN needs plain IP routing. Backbones choose MPLS for the small overhead and traffic engineering; data centres choose VXLAN because any IP fabric and commodity switch can carry it, with ECMP entropy in the UDP source port. The trap: a VPN route is usable only while its next hop resolves to an LSP, so a missing label to the far loopback hides every route behind it.
