@0
Two Acme web servers, acme-web1 in Amsterdam and acme-web2 in Singapore, share one subnet, 10.1.1.0 slash 24, on VLAN 100, which Meridian carries as VXLAN segment 10100. Between them is a routed underlay: leaf 1 in Amsterdam, a spine in each site, about 10,500 kilometres of fibre, and leaf 2 in Singapore, in a small example with two spines per site. Hold onto one idea: the leaves wrap the tenant's frame in an ordinary UDP packet addressed from one leaf to the other, so every device in between routes on that outer address and never reads the frame inside.

@1
acme-web1 builds an ordinary Ethernet frame. Its destination is acme-web2's MAC address, ending in 53 0B. The two hosts share a subnet, so no router is involved and the time to live stays at 64. Inside is an IPv4 packet of 1,500 bytes carrying a TCP segment to port 443. It has no idea a tunnel exists.

@2
The frame enters leaf 1 in Amsterdam on an access port in VLAN 100, untagged, exactly as it would enter any switch.

@3
Leaf 1 maps VLAN 100 on this port to VNI 10100, and looks up the destination MAC in that segment's table. The table says acme-web2 lives behind a remote tunnel endpoint, a VTEP, at 10.255.2.12, which is the loopback of leaf 2 in Singapore. It learned that from a BGP EVPN type 2 route, the subject of the next lesson. So this frame must be tunnelled, not switched out of a local port.

@4
Encapsulation starts from the inside. Leaf 1 puts an 8 byte VXLAN header in front of the original frame. It holds a flags byte, zero eight, which says the VNI is valid, and the 24 bit VNI itself, 10100, which tells the far end which tenant segment this frame belongs to. Only the egress VTEP will ever read it. Overhead so far: 8 bytes.

@5
Next comes an 8 byte UDP header. The destination port is 4789, the port IANA assigned to VXLAN. The source port, 58479, is computed from a hash of the inner headers. Different inner flows get different source ports, which gives the underlay's ECMP hash something to spread on, while one flow keeps one port and one path. The UDP checksum is zero, unused. Sixteen bytes so far.

@6
Then a 20 byte outer IPv4 header, from leaf 1's VTEP address, 10.255.1.11, to leaf 2's, 10.255.2.12: protocol UDP, time to live 64, total length 1,550 bytes. Leaf 1's underlay route to 10.255.2.12 has two equal-cost next hops, spine 1 and spine 2 in Amsterdam, both from IS-IS, and the hash of the outer headers, source port 58479 included, picks spine 1. Thirty-six bytes so far.

@7
Last, a 14 byte outer Ethernet header addressed to spine 1's port MAC. Four headers now wrap the original frame: 14 plus 20 plus 8 plus 8, which is 50 bytes. That is the number to remember. A 1,500 byte tenant packet needs an underlay IP MTU of at least 1,550 bytes, which is why fabrics run jumbo MTUs; Meridian runs 9,216 bytes.

@8
The tunnelled packet leaves leaf 1. To the underlay it is just a UDP packet from 10.255.1.11 to 10.255.2.12, and the tenant's frame is payload.

@9
Spine 1 in Amsterdam reads only the outer IPv4 destination, 10.255.2.12, and its routing table sends that over the long-haul circuit to spine 1 in Singapore. Like any router it decrements the outer time to live, to 63, and rewrites the outer MACs. For VNI 10100 it holds no MACs and no tenant routes; the inner frame passes through unread.

@10
Across the circuit from Amsterdam to Singapore: about 10,500 kilometres of fibre at roughly 5 microseconds per kilometre, so about 52 and a half milliseconds one way. That is far more than all the switches on the path add together.

@11
Spine 1 in Singapore does the same. The outer destination is leaf 2's loopback, one hop down. The outer time to live becomes 62, the outer MACs are rewritten again, and the VXLAN header and the inner frame are exactly the bytes leaf 1 wrote.

@12
Down the last underlay link to leaf 2 in Singapore, the egress VTEP, which owns the outer destination address.

@13
Leaf 2 runs three checks, one per tunnel header. The outer destination, 10.255.2.12, is its own VTEP address. The UDP destination port is 4789, so this is VXLAN. The VNI is 10100, which it maps to its local Acme VLAN 100. Then it strips all 50 bytes, and its MAC table puts the original frame's destination, acme-web2, on a local port.

@14
Leaf 2 switches the frame out of acme-web2's port, still untagged, still carrying acme-web1's source MAC and a time to live of 64.

@15
acme-web2 receives, byte for byte, the frame acme-web1 sent, as if both sat on one switch. Fifty bytes were added at one leaf and removed at the other, the outer time to live went from 64 to 62 across two underlay routers, and the inner frame never changed. The spines never needed to know Acme, VLAN 100 or VNI 10100 exist, and that separation lets one routed fabric carry thousands of tenant segments. The traps live in the outer headers: one link below the 1,550 byte budget drops only large packets, and only on the flows hashed through it; and a VTEP that writes a constant source port collapses all its tunnelled traffic onto one spine.
