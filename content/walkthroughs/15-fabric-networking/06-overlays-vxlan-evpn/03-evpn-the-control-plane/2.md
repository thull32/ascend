@0
The same small Meridian overlay as before: two route reflectors, one per site, leaf 1 and the Globex-only leaf 2 in Amsterdam, and leaf 2 in Singapore. This run asks a harder question than where a MAC lives: who has the MAC now, after it moves? Here is the one idea that answers it. When a host moves, two routes for its MAC exist under different route distinguishers, so BGP's own best-path selection never compares them. A sequence number carried with the route does, and the highest one wins on every leaf.

@1-9
The first part repeats the earlier run. acme-web1, at 10.1.1.10 with the MAC ending in 53 0A, ARPs for its gateway; leaf 1 in Amsterdam learns the MAC on port 1 and answers as the anycast gateway. It advertises one type 2 route with route distinguisher 10.255.1.11 colon 10100 and next hop 10.255.1.11, the reflectors add their cluster IDs and pass it on, the Globex leaf discards it for lack of a matching route target, and leaf 2 in Singapore installs the MAC behind VTEP 10.255.1.11.

@10-12
acme-web2 then ARPs for 10.1.1.10, and leaf 2 in Singapore answers from its suppression table without flooding anything. Its MAC table now points the MAC ending in 53 0A at leaf 1. Notice that this first route carries no MAC Mobility community at all.

@13
Now acme-web1, a virtual machine, is live-migrated to a server behind leaf 4 in Amsterdam. After the move it announces itself with a gratuitous ARP, an unsolicited ARP for its own address; some hypervisors send RARP instead. Leaf 4 learns the MAC on a local port, yet it already holds a remote route for that MAC from leaf 1. That route has no sequence number, which counts as 0. Two sources, one MAC.

@14
Leaf 4 advertises its own type 2 route for the MAC: route distinguisher 10.255.1.14 colon 10100, next hop 10.255.1.14, and the MAC Mobility extended community with sequence number 1, one more than the highest it has seen. The two routes have different route distinguishers, so to BGP they are separate routes. The sequence number is what tells every leaf which one is current.

@15
Reflected the same way, the new route reaches leaf 2 in Singapore, which now holds two routes for the MAC: the old one from 10.255.1.11 with no sequence, and the new one from 10.255.1.14 with sequence 1. The higher sequence wins, so the leaf points the MAC at VTEP 10.255.1.14, and Acme's traffic follows the virtual machine without any flooding. The remote site converges about one long-haul delay plus BGP processing after the gratuitous ARP.

@16
Leaf 1 receives the sequence 1 route too, for a MAC it had learned locally. RFC 7432 makes the old owner give way: it flushes its local entry and withdraws its own type 2 route. Now only leaf 4's route remains, and it is best everywhere.

@17
Every further move raises the sequence number again. If one MAC moves 5 times within 180 seconds, the defaults RFC 7432 suggests, a leaf flags it as a duplicate and stops advertising it. A MAC that keeps bouncing between leaves is rarely a virtual machine on tour: usually two hosts share a MAC, such as a cloned virtual machine, or a layer 2 loop is reflecting one host's frames back into the fabric. So the general pattern is a version number on a fact that can change owner, with the newest version winning and the old owner retracting. And the number to put on a dashboard is the MAC move counter: one move after a migration is normal, a steady climb is the alarm.
