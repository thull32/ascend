@0
On screen, three switches wired in a triangle: leaf1, leaf2 and leaf3 in Amsterdam, every port in VLAN 100, one Acme host on each. Any one link can fail without cutting anyone off, the classic layer 2 design. Spanning tree is off, so all three links between the switches forward. Hold onto one fact, because it is the whole reason fabrics refuse to stretch layer 2: an Ethernet frame has no time to live, so nothing ever tells a switch that a frame has been round too many times.

@1
acme-web1 sends one broadcast, an ARP request for 10.1.1.17. Unlike an IP packet, which carries a TTL every router counts down, this frame has no hop count at all.

@2
leaf1 learns acme-web1 on port 1 and floods the broadcast out of every other port: one copy to leaf2 on port 49, one to leaf3 on port 50. Two copies are now in the loop.

@3
leaf2 floods its copy to acme-web3 and on to leaf3, and leaf3 floods its copy to acme-web4 and on to leaf2. Each host now has its ARP request, which is all that should ever have happened. But the two copies are crossing on the link between leaf2 and leaf3, in opposite directions.

@4
Each copy reaches the far switch and is flooded again. acme-web3 and acme-web4 get the same request a second time, and both copies head back to leaf1, still carrying acme-web1's source MAC, the one ending in 53 0A. leaf1 learns that address on port 49, then on port 50. The MAC flaps between ports, and unicast frames for acme-web1 are now sent the wrong way.

@5
leaf1 floods each returning copy out of every port except the one it came in on. One sets off round the triangle again in each direction, and acme-web1 receives its own broadcast back, twice. Two copies are in the loop, exactly as at the start, and nothing in the frame or in the switches will ever end the cycle.

@6
So the copies circle as fast as the switches can forward them. A lap through three switches takes about 3 microseconds, roughly 1 microsecond per switch, so every host receives this one ARP request about 670,000 times a second, and its CPU has to process every copy. The switches' own CPUs receive every broadcast too.

@7
It gets worse with every broadcast that follows. ARP, DHCP, service discovery: every host sends some, and each one adds two more copies that never die, one circling each way. Each copy crosses every link about 333,000 times a second, 2.2 percent of a 10 gigabit link in its direction, so after 45 broadcasts every link in the triangle is full both ways and real traffic is crowded out. Where a switch has three or more looped ports, copies multiply on every lap and the count grows exponentially.

@8
Now the classic cure. The same triangle runs again with the Rapid Spanning Tree Protocol, RSTP, turned on. The switches exchange BPDUs, bridge protocol data units, small messages sent every 2 seconds, and elect a root bridge: the one with the lowest bridge ID, which is its priority, 32768 by default on all three, followed by its MAC. leaf1's MAC ends in 53 80, the lowest, so leaf1 is the root.

@9
Every other switch picks its root port, the one with the lowest path cost to the root. A 10 gigabit link costs 2,000, the IEEE default, so for leaf2 and leaf3 the direct link to leaf1, at 2,000, beats the way round through the other switch, at 4,000. Port 49 becomes the root port on both.

@10
That leaves the link between leaf2 and leaf3, where both ends offer the same cost to the root, 2,000. The tie goes to the lower bridge ID, and leaf2's MAC ends in 81 against leaf3's 82. So leaf2's port 50 is designated and keeps forwarding, while leaf3's port 50 becomes an alternate port in the discarding state. It still receives BPDUs but forwards no data, and the loop is broken.

@11
acme-web1 sends the same broadcast again. leaf1 floods it to both neighbours, each delivers it to its host, and the copy leaf2 sends towards leaf3 dies at the discarding port. Every host receives it exactly once.

@12
Spanning tree buys that safety at a price. One of the three links carries no traffic at all. Recovery after a failure takes RSTP from under a second to a few seconds, where the original standard took 30 to 50 seconds. And a switch that stops sending BPDUs, because its CPU is overloaded or a link has failed in one direction only, makes its neighbour's alternate port start forwarding, and the storm comes back. That is why leaf-spine fabrics route between switches instead: every link forwards, and an IP packet caught in a loop dies when its TTL reaches zero. Tenants who need layer 2 get it from an overlay on that routed core, not from VLANs stretched across it.
