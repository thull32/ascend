@0
On screen, acme-web1 in Amsterdam and acme-web2 in Singapore. Both sit in Acme's subnet, 10.1.1.0 slash 24, on VLAN 100, which Meridian carries across the fabric as VXLAN segment VNI 10100. They expect to share one Ethernet segment, but between them is a routed underlay: leaf1 in Amsterdam, two spines, the 10,500 kilometre circuit, and leaf2 in Singapore, in a small example with two spines per site. Hold onto one idea, because it is the whole overlay: the tenant's frame is sealed at the first leaf and opened at the last, and everything in between reads only the outside.

@1
acme-web1 sends an ordinary Ethernet frame to acme-web2's MAC, the one ending in 53 0B. They are in the same subnet, so no router is involved and the TTL stays at 64. Inside is a 1,500 byte IPv4 packet carrying a TCP segment for port 443. acme-web1 has no idea any tunnel exists.

@2
The frame enters leaf1 on an access port in VLAN 100, untagged, exactly as it would enter any switch.

@3
leaf1 maps VLAN 100 on this port to VNI 10100 and looks up the destination MAC in that segment's table. The MAC ending in 0B lives behind a remote VTEP, 10.255.2.12, which is leaf2 in Singapore's loopback, learned from a BGP EVPN type 2 route. So the frame must be tunnelled, not switched out of a local port.

@4
Encapsulation starts from the inside. leaf1 puts an 8 byte VXLAN header in front of the original frame: a flags byte saying the VNI is valid, and the 24 bit VNI, 10100, which tells the far end which tenant segment this frame belongs to. The overhead counter reads 8 bytes.

@5
Next an 8 byte UDP header, 16 bytes so far. The destination port is 4789, the port IANA assigned to VXLAN. The source port, 58479, is computed from a hash of the inner headers. Different inner flows get different source ports, which gives the underlay's ECMP hash something to spread on, while one flow keeps one port and so one path.

@6
Then a 20 byte outer IPv4 header, from VTEP 10.255.1.11 to VTEP 10.255.2.12, protocol UDP, TTL 64, total length 1,550. The overhead is now 36 bytes. Here is the second lookup: leaf1's underlay route to 10.255.2.12 has two equal-cost next hops, spine1 and spine2, and the hash of the outer headers, source port included, picks spine1 in Amsterdam.

@7
Last, a 14 byte outer Ethernet header, addressed to spine1's port MAC. Four headers, 14 plus 20 plus 8 plus 8, make 50 bytes wrapped around the original frame. So a 1,500 byte tenant packet needs an underlay IP MTU of at least 1,550 bytes, which is why fabrics run jumbo MTUs. Meridian uses 9,216.

@8
The tunnelled packet leaves leaf1. To the underlay it is just a UDP packet from 10.255.1.11 to 10.255.2.12, and the tenant's frame is payload.

@9
spine1 in Amsterdam reads only the outer IPv4 destination, 10.255.2.12, and routes it towards spine1 in Singapore over the long-haul circuit. It decrements the outer TTL to 63 and rewrites the outer MACs. Look at what it knows about VNI 10100: no MACs and no tenant routes. The inner frame passes through unread.

@10
Across the Amsterdam to Singapore circuit: about 10,500 kilometres of fibre at roughly 5 microseconds per kilometre, so about 52.5 milliseconds one way, far more than all the switches on the path add together.

@11
spine1 in Singapore does the same. The outer destination is leaf2's loopback, one hop down. The outer TTL becomes 62, the outer MACs are rewritten again, and the VXLAN header and inner frame are exactly the bytes leaf1 wrote.

@12
Down the last underlay link to leaf2, the egress VTEP, which owns the outer destination address 10.255.2.12.

@13
leaf2 runs three checks. The outer destination is its own VTEP address, the UDP destination port is 4789, so this is VXLAN, and VNI 10100 maps to its local Acme VLAN 100. It strips all 50 bytes of outer headers, and the original frame comes out, with a destination MAC that is on a local port.

@14
leaf2 switches the frame out of acme-web2's port, still untagged, still carrying acme-web1's source MAC and an inner TTL of 64.

@15
acme-web2 receives, byte for byte, the frame acme-web1 sent, as if both sat on one switch. The outer TTL went from 64 to 62 across two underlay routers, and the inner frame never changed. That is the parcel in a box: the depots, the leaves, seal and open it, and the sorting centres, the spines, read only the label. The spines never needed to know that Acme, VLAN 100 or VNI 10100 exist, and that separation is what lets one routed fabric carry thousands of tenant segments. The price is a 50 byte box on every frame, and the overlay is only as healthy as the underlay route to the far VTEP.
