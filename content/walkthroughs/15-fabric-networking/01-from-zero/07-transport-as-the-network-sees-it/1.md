@0
On screen is Meridian's Amsterdam fabric: four spines, and two of its sixteen leaves, leaf1 and leaf3, with Acme's servers behind each. Meridian really carries this traffic inside VXLAN tunnels, and we get there at the end; first, picture leaf1 routing it as plain IP, so every packet towards 10.1.2.0 slash 24 behind leaf3 has four equal-cost uplinks, one per spine. The idea to hold onto: the switch never tracks flows. It reads five fields that every packet of a connection shares, hashes them, and lets the number choose the uplink.

@1
The forwarding table entry for 10.1.2.0 slash 24 points at an ECMP group with four members, numbered 0 to 3, one per spine. For every packet the switching chip must pick a member, and it must pick the same one for every packet of a flow.

@2
Flow A arrives: a PostgreSQL connection from acme-web1, 10.1.1.10 with source port 51517, to acme-db1, 10.1.2.20 on port 5432. Five fields light up: the two addresses, the protocol, 6 for TCP, and the two ports. That is the 5-tuple, the flow's name, and every packet of this connection carries exactly those five values.

@3
The chip feeds the five fields through a hash and gets a 32-bit number, written in hex as B 6 A F 1 3 1 B. Real chips use their vendor's own hash; this one stands in for them, and what matters is shared: the same five values always give the same number, and changing any one of them gives a different number.

@4
That number modulo 4 is 3, which with four members is just its lowest two bits, so flow A goes up member 3, the uplink to spine4. Every later packet of flow A hashes the same way and follows it, so the flow stays on one path and in order.

@5
Flow B is a second connection between the same two servers, and only its source port differs: 51518 instead of 51517. Its hash, 1 5 1 F D D C 6, looks nothing like A's; modulo 4 it gives 2, and B takes spine3. Change one port by one and the flow lands on what is, in effect, a random uplink.

@6
Three more flows: C, a TLS connection; D, another database client; and E, a DNS query over UDP, protocol 17. They land on members 0, 3 and 1. Five flows now sit on four uplinks as one, one, one and two, with spine4 carrying two. The hash spreads flows evenly only on average. It balances flows, not bytes, and it never looks at how busy an uplink already is.

@7
Now the same connections A and B as Meridian really sends them, across the VXLAN overlay. leaf1, the tunnel endpoint at 10.255.1.11, routes each packet into Acme's VNI 50001 and wraps it in outer headers addressed to leaf3's endpoint, 10.255.1.13. The uplink is now chosen by the underlay route to that loopback, and the hash reads only the outer headers. The inner 5-tuple, with its ports, is just payload.

@8
Suppose leaf1 used one fixed outer source port. Every tunnelled flow to leaf3 would then have the same outer 5-tuple: the two loopbacks, UDP, and port 4789 on both sides. Same tuple, same hash, 7 7 7 D 5 5 C B, and the same uplink, spine4, so all of it rides one of four links.

@9
What tunnel endpoints actually do, as the VXLAN standard recommends: leaf1 hashes the inner headers and writes the result into the outer UDP source port, kept in the range 49152 to 65535. Flow A gets 54043, flow B gets 56774. The destination port stays 4789, which is how leaf3 still recognises the packet as VXLAN.

@10
The two outer 5-tuples now differ in one field, so their hashes differ, and the connections take spine1 and spine4. Every packet of flow A still gets source port 54043, so the tunnel keeps each inner flow on one path and in order.

@11
That is the whole trade. Hashing the 5-tuple needs no coordination and keeps every flow in order, but it spreads flows rather than bytes, so two large flows can share a link while another sits idle. And it needs entropy: many distinct tuples. A tunnel must copy the inner flow's entropy into the one field it controls, its outer source port, or everything it carries collapses onto one path. RoCE version 2, on UDP port 4791, uses its source port the same way, which is why AI fabrics care how their NICs choose it.
