@0
On screen are a sender and a receiver. The network never acknowledges or retransmits anything; it forwards, queues, drops and marks, and only the endpoints learn that a packet was lost. The rule that makes fast recovery possible is simple: the receiver always acknowledges the last byte it holds in order, so a hole in the stream makes it repeat itself.

@1
The sender sends four segments of a thousand bytes each, starting at bytes 1, 1001, 2001 and 3001. The one starting at 1001 is lost on the way.

@2
The first segment arrives, and the receiver acknowledges 1001: the next byte it expects.

@3
The segment starting at 2001 arrives out of order. The receiver buffers it, but its acknowledgement is cumulative, so it still says 1001. That is duplicate ACK number one.

@4
The segment at 3001 arrives, and the receiver says 1001 again: duplicate number two. With selective acknowledgements it would also report that it already holds bytes 2001 to 4000.

@5
Duplicate number three. Later segments are still getting through, so the sender infers a single loss, not a collapsed path.

@6
It resends the segment at 1001 at once, without waiting for its timer. It halves its congestion window rather than resetting it to one segment; that halving is classic Reno, and Linux's default, CUBIC, cuts it to 70 percent. The retransmission timer has not expired.

@7
The hole is filled, and the cumulative acknowledgement jumps to 4001, covering the buffered segments too. Recovery cost about one round trip.

@8
The alternative is the timeout: at least 200 milliseconds on Linux, doubling on each failure, and a congestion window back to one segment. Inside a site, where a round trip is tens of microseconds, 200 milliseconds is ten thousand round trips of idleness, and it shows in the application's tail latency, not in any network graph. Fast retransmit needs later segments to produce the duplicates, which is why losing the last segments of a burst is worse, and why Linux sends a tail loss probe. For your tooling, the signal is retransmitted segments per second on the hosts: grouped by destination leaf, it often points at a lossy link that no switch counter shows.
