@0
The same cable as before: acme-web1 on port 1 of leaf1, in Meridian's Amsterdam site, over a full duplex link where both ends send at once and nothing collides. This time the frame carries a jumbo payload of 9,000 bytes, as if acme-web1 ran a 9,000 byte MTU. In practice it keeps 1,500; Meridian's GPU and storage servers are the ones that run 9,000. One idea makes jumbo frames worth the trouble: the overhead of a frame is fixed, so a bigger payload spreads it thinner, and the same bytes travel in far fewer frames.

@1-4
The front of the frame is identical to a standard one. First 8 bytes of preamble and start frame delimiter, for the receiver's PHY to lock onto the bit timing, never counted in the frame size. Then the 14 byte Ethernet header: the destination MAC, the gateway's anycast address ending in 01 01, placed first so the switch can pick a port early; the source MAC, acme-web1's own card, ending in 53 0A; and the EtherType, 0800 in hex, meaning IPv4.

@5
Then the payload: a 9,000 byte IPv4 packet, a jumbo frame. Here is the condition. It only works if every device on the link has an MTU, the largest payload allowed, of at least 9,000 bytes. The standard MTU is 1,500, and Meridian's fabric links run 9,216. The frame is now 9,014 bytes.

@6
The 4 byte frame check sequence closes it: a CRC-32 over every byte from the destination MAC to the end of the payload, recomputed by leaf1 as the frame arrives. If even one bit differs, the frame is dropped and counted as an FCS error.

@7
14 bytes of header, 9,000 of payload and 4 of check make 9,018 bytes, the size counters report. With the 8 byte preamble and the 12 byte interframe gap, it costs 9,038 bytes of wire time, 72,304 bits, and 99.6 percent of that is payload, against 97.5 percent for a standard 1,518 byte frame.

@8
At 1 gigabit per second, one bit per nanosecond, the 72,304 bits take 72.304 microseconds to serialise, and the first bit is already 14.46 kilometres down the fibre when the last one leaves. That port can send only 13,830 of these frames a second.

@9-10
At 10 gigabits per second, 7.2304 microseconds, the frame stretching over 1.45 kilometres of fibre, and 138,304 frames a second. At 100 gigabits per second, 723.04 nanoseconds, and up to 1,383,049 frames a second. Serialisation time falls in exact proportion to link speed.

@11
At 400 gigabits per second, the speed of Meridian's fabric links, 180.76 nanoseconds, with the whole frame in 36.15 metres of fibre, and a ceiling of 5,532,197 frames a second. A standard 1,518 byte frame allowed about 32.5 million. That is the real gain: the same bandwidth in about a sixth of the frames, and every frame costs a lookup in the switch, and interrupts and kernel work on the host.

@12
Propagation ignores all of this: about 5 nanoseconds per metre at any speed, 500 nanoseconds for a 100 metre run in a data hall, about 52.5 milliseconds for the 10,500 kilometres from Amsterdam to Singapore. On the 100 metre link, serialisation dominates at 1 gigabit, 72.304 microseconds against 500 nanoseconds. At 400 gigabits even this large frame takes only 180.76 nanoseconds, so propagation dominates again.

@13
leaf1's port counters show 1 packet in and 9,018 bytes in, counted from destination MAC to FCS, where a Linux host would usually leave the 4 byte FCS out. A second frame with one bit flipped by a dirty connector fails its CRC, and is dropped and counted as an FCS error.

So the gain from jumbo frames is not efficiency, which moves by only a few percent. It is the packet rate: about six times fewer frames to carry the same bytes. RoCE fabrics run them end to end. The trap is the condition from the payload step. Every device on the link has to accept the size, because a receiver drops a frame above its MTU as oversize and tells the sender nothing.
