@0
On screen is one cable: acme-web1, Acme's web server, plugged into port 1 of leaf1, the top of rack switch in Meridian's Amsterdam site. The link is full duplex, two independent one way channels, so both ends send at full speed at once and nothing collides. Hold onto one idea while it is built: a receiver reads a frame in the order its bits arrive, so each field sits exactly where its reader needs it, and every byte, counted or not, costs time on the wire.

@1
Before the frame proper come 8 bytes. Seven bytes of alternating ones and zeros, 55 in hex, let the receiver's physical layer, the PHY, lock onto the bit timing. One more byte, the start frame delimiter, D5 in hex, marks where the frame begins. They use wire time, yet the frame size still reads zero: they are never counted in it.

@2
First comes the destination MAC, 6 bytes: leaf1's gateway address, the anycast gateway MAC ending in 01 01. It goes first so a switch can choose the output port while the rest of the frame is still arriving. Its first three bytes, 00 00 5E, are the OUI, the block IANA owns, and the lowest bit of the first byte is zero, so it is unicast. An odd first byte would mean multicast, and all ones is broadcast.

@3
Then the source MAC, 6 more bytes, ending in 53 0A: acme-web1's own card. A source MAC is always unicast, and it is the field switches learn from, the next lesson's subject.

@4
Then 2 bytes of EtherType, 0800 in hex, meaning the payload is an IPv4 packet. 86DD would mean IPv6, 0806 ARP, and 8100 a VLAN tag. That completes the Ethernet header at 14 bytes: two addresses that only mean something on this one link, and a type. There is no hop count, and nothing about the final destination.

@5
Next, the payload: a 1,500 byte IPv4 packet, the standard maximum, which is exactly what an MTU of 1,500 means. The frame is now 1,514 bytes. A payload shorter than 46 bytes is padded up to 46, so no frame is ever under 64 bytes.

@6
Last, the 4 byte frame check sequence, the FCS: a CRC-32 the card computes over every byte from the destination MAC to the end of the payload. leaf1 recomputes it as the frame arrives, and if the two differ by even one bit, the frame is dropped and counted as an FCS error.

@7
14 bytes of header, 1,500 of payload and 4 of check make 1,518 bytes, the size interface counters report. Add the 8 byte preamble and a mandatory 12 byte idle gap before the next frame, the interframe gap, and the frame costs 1,538 bytes of wire time: 12,304 bits, of which 97.5 percent is payload.

@8
Now it is sent. At 1 gigabit per second the card puts one bit on the wire every nanosecond, so the 12,304 bits take 12.304 microseconds. That is serialisation delay. Light in fibre covers about 200 metres per microsecond, so the first bit is 2.46 kilometres away when the last one leaves. At this size the port can send at most 81,274 frames a second.

@9
At 10 gigabits per second the same frame takes 1.2304 microseconds, ten times less, and occupies 246 metres of fibre. The port's ceiling rises to 812,743 frames a second.

@10
At 100 gigabits per second, 123.04 nanoseconds, and up to about 8.1 million frames a second. Serialisation time falls in exact proportion to link speed.

@11
At 400 gigabits per second, the speed of Meridian's fabric links, 30.76 nanoseconds, and the whole frame fits in 6.15 metres of fibre. A faster link does not make bits travel any faster; it makes each frame shorter in time.

@12
Propagation is the other half, about 5 nanoseconds per metre of fibre at any speed. A 100 metre run inside a data hall adds 500 nanoseconds. The 10,500 kilometres from Amsterdam to Singapore add about 52.5 milliseconds. On that 100 metre link, serialisation dominates at 1 gigabit, 12.304 microseconds against 500 nanoseconds, while at 400 gigabits the frame takes only 30.76 nanoseconds and propagation dominates. That is why long distance latency is a geography problem that faster links cannot fix.

@13
Finally, the counters on leaf1's port 1. The good frame counted 1 packet in and 1,518 bytes in: switches count from destination MAC to FCS, while a Linux host usually leaves the 4 byte FCS out. Then a second frame arrives with one bit flipped by a dirty connector. Its CRC does not match, so leaf1 drops it and counts an FCS error, and the sender is never told.

That is the pattern to keep. Every field is placed for the reader who needs it first, the bytes nobody counts still cost wire time, and serialisation shrinks with speed while propagation never does. When a cable or optic starts to fail, a rising FCS error counter on the receiving port is where it shows first.
