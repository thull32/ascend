@0
On screen, leaf1 is playing a classic learning switch for VLAN 100, with four Acme servers on ports 1 to 4: acme-web1, acme-app1, acme-app2 and acme-app3. Its MAC address table is empty, and nobody will ever configure it. Hold onto three rules, because they are the whole algorithm: learn from the source, forward to the known port or flood the unknown, and forget whatever falls silent.

@1
acme-web1 sends a frame to acme-app2. The header stack shows the destination MAC, ending in 53 0E, and the source, acme-web1's own MAC ending in 53 0A. It enters the switch on port 1.

@2
Before it forwards anything, the switch reads the source and writes its first entry: the MAC ending in 0A is behind port 1, age 0 seconds. Learning only ever uses the source address, because a frame proves where its sender is, never where its destination is.

@3
Now the destination lookup. The MAC ending in 0E is not in the table: an unknown unicast. So the switch floods, one copy out of every other port in the VLAN, ports 2, 3 and 4, and never back out of port 1, where the frame came in.

@4
acme-app1 and acme-app3 receive a frame that is not for them, see a destination MAC that is not their own, and discard it. Only acme-app2 accepts it. Flooding always works, but every host in the VLAN pays for every unknown frame.

@5
acme-app2 replies, arriving on port 3, and the switch learns its second entry from the reply's source: the MAC ending in 0E is on port 3. The table now holds two entries.

@6
This time the destination, acme-web1, is in the table on port 1, so the reply leaves through port 1 only. acme-app1 and acme-app3 never see it.

@7
One second later, acme-web1 sends again. The destination is known, so the frame goes out of port 3 alone. And because the switch has just seen acme-web1 as a source again, its age resets to 0, while acme-app2's entry reads 1 second.

@8
At 5 seconds, acme-app3 sends a broadcast, an ARP request to the all-ones address. The switch learns a third entry, the MAC ending in 53 10 on port 4, and floods the frame to ports 1, 2 and 3, as it always does for a broadcast. Every port in VLAN 100 is one broadcast domain.

@9
Jump to 290 seconds. acme-web1 and acme-app2 are still talking, so their ages keep resetting to 0. acme-app3 has said nothing since 5 seconds, and its entry is 285 seconds old, close to the aging time of 300 seconds, the IEEE default.

@10
At 306 seconds the entry passes 300 seconds without a refresh and the switch removes it. Two entries are left. Aging exists because the table cannot tell when a host leaves: it keeps the table from filling with addresses that are gone, and lets a host that moved to another port be learned afresh.

@11
At 340 seconds acme-web1 sends to acme-app3, whose MAC is unknown again, so the frame is flooded to ports 2, 3 and 4 until acme-app3 answers and is relearned. A trickle of this is normal. A steady stream usually means something is still sending to an address the switch has aged out, the classic case being a router that keeps ARP entries for hours while the switch forgets MACs after five minutes.

@12
And acme-app1 has sent nothing at all, so it was never learned, and every frame for it would be flooded. That is the whole switch: learn from the source, forward or flood by the destination, forget the silent. One more thing to watch for: a MAC that keeps appearing on two ports in turn, a MAC flap, is the classic sign of a loop, and the broadcast storm later in the lesson shows exactly why.
