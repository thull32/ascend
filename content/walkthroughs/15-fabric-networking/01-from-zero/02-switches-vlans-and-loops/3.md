@0
Three switches, leaf1, leaf2 and leaf3, wired in a triangle so that any one link can fail without cutting anyone off: a classic layer 2 design, not Meridian's. Every port is in VLAN 100, one Acme host sits on each switch, and spanning tree is off, so all three links forward. Hold onto one fact, because it is the whole outage: a switch floods a broadcast out of every port but the one it came in on, and nothing in the frame says it has travelled too far.

@1
acme-web1 sends one broadcast, an ARP request for 10.1.1.17. An IP packet carries a TTL that routers count down, but the Ethernet header has no TTL and no hop count at all.

@2
leaf1 learns acme-web1 on port 1 and floods the broadcast out of every other port: one copy to leaf2 on port 49, and one to leaf3 on port 50. The counter reads two copies in the loop.

@3
leaf2 floods its copy to acme-web3 and on to leaf3. leaf3 floods its copy to acme-web4 and on to leaf2. Each host has its ARP request, which is all that should have happened. But the two copies are crossing on the leaf2 to leaf3 link in opposite directions.

@4
Each copy reaches the far switch and is flooded again. acme-web3 and acme-web4 get the same request a second time, and both copies head back to leaf1, still carrying acme-web1's source MAC. Watch leaf1's MAC table: it relearns acme-web1 on port 49, then on port 50. The address flaps between ports, and real unicast frames for acme-web1 now go the wrong way.

@5
leaf1 floods each returning copy out of every port except the one it arrived on. One copy sets off round the triangle again in each direction, and acme-web1 receives its own broadcast back, twice. Two copies are in the loop, exactly as at the start, and nothing will ever end the cycle.

@6
The copies circle as fast as the switches can forward them. A lap through three switches takes about 3 microseconds, roughly 1 microsecond per switch, so every host receives this one ARP request about 670,000 times a second, and its CPU has to process every copy. The switches' own CPUs receive every broadcast too.

@7
And it gets worse with every broadcast hosts send anyway: ARP, DHCP, service discovery. Each one adds two more copies that never die, one circling each way. A single copy crossing a link about 333,000 times a second uses 2.2 percent of a 10 gigabit per second link in its direction. The chart climbs in a straight line, 22 percent at 10 broadcasts, 67 at 30, and at 45 broadcasts every link in the triangle is full both ways, with real traffic crowded out. Where switches have three or more looped ports, every copy is copied again on each lap and the count grows exponentially.

@8
Spanning tree prevents this. Run the same triangle again with the Rapid Spanning Tree Protocol on. The switches exchange BPDUs, small messages sent every 2 seconds, and elect a root bridge: the lowest bridge ID, which is the priority, by default 32768, followed by the MAC. All three priorities are equal, so the lowest MAC decides, and leaf1, ending in 53 80, is the root.

@9
Every other switch picks its root port, the one with the lowest path cost to the root. A 10 gigabit link costs 2,000 by the IEEE default, so for leaf2 and leaf3 the direct link to leaf1, at 2,000, beats the way round through the other switch, at 4,000. Port 49 becomes the root port on both.

@10
That leaves the link between leaf2 and leaf3, where both ends offer the same cost to the root, 2,000. The tie goes to the lower bridge ID, so leaf2's port 50 is designated and keeps forwarding, while leaf3's port 50 becomes an alternate port in the discarding state. It still receives BPDUs but forwards no data, so no frame can go all the way round.

@11
acme-web1 sends the same broadcast again. leaf1 floods it to both neighbours, each delivers it to its host, and the copy leaf2 sends towards leaf3 is discarded at the alternate port. Every host receives it exactly once, acme-web1 gets nothing back, and the copies die.

@12
Spanning tree buys safety at a price. One of the three links carries no traffic. Recovery takes rapid spanning tree from under a second to a few seconds, where the original version took 30 to 50. And it fails open: a switch that stops sending BPDUs, because its CPU is overloaded or a link fails in one direction only, makes its neighbour's alternate port start forwarding, and the storm comes back. That is why leaf-spine fabrics route between switches instead: every link forwards, flows are spread across all of them with ECMP, and an IP packet caught in a loop dies when its TTL reaches zero.
