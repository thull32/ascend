@0
On screen is the inside of one switch, leaf1.ams: a forwarding pipeline from port 1 to port 49, and below it a dashed path to control-plane policing and the CPU. Hold onto one sentence, because it is the whole picture: a switch is two machines in one box. The ASIC, one chip forwarding 51.2 terabits per second on widely deployed parts, moves every transit packet using its tables. A separate CPU running the network operating system decides what goes into those tables, over a PCIe link with a tiny fraction of that bandwidth. Management has its own out-of-band port, 172.16.1.11.

@1
The first packet is acme-web1's TCP SYN to acme-db1, arriving on port 1. The port checks the frame check sequence first, and this one is good: 1 packet in, 0 FCS errors. A bad frame would be dropped right here, before any lookup, and counted.

@2
The parser copies what later stages need into a header vector: the destination MAC, the anycast gateway ending in 01 01; EtherType hex 0800, meaning IPv4; 10.1.1.10 to 10.1.2.20; a TTL of 64; DSCP 0; protocol 6, which is TCP; and ports 51514 to 5432. From here on every stage reads this vector, while the packet's bytes wait in the buffer.

@3
The destination MAC is leaf1's own gateway MAC, so the packet is routed. In the hardware FIB the longest match for 10.1.2.20 is 10.1.2.0 slash 24, an ECMP group of two next hops, and the flow hash picks 10.254.1.0, spine1, on port 49, in a fixed number of clock cycles. Notice one more row: 10.255.1.11 slash 32, marked receive, pointing at the CPU. It matters later.

@4
The ingress ACL lives in TCAM, a memory that compares the header vector with every rule at once, each rule a value and a mask, and the lowest-numbered match wins. Rule 10 denies telnet, TCP port 23: no match. Rule 20 denies tenant addresses reaching the fabric's loopback range, 10.255.0.0 slash 16: no match. Rule 30 permits anything, and its hit counter reads 1.

@5
The packet is queued in the shared buffer on port 49, traffic class 0, chosen from its DSCP of 0: best effort. The queues for RoCE and for network control are empty. The buffer is small for the speed: even 64 megabytes drains in 10 microseconds at 51.2 terabits per second. When a queue's share is used up, new packets are tail-dropped and the discard counter rises. Here it reads 0.

@6
At the head of the queue the egress pipeline rewrites the packet. The TTL goes from 64 to 63, the IP checksum from E 9 7 D to E A 7 D in hex, and the adjacency entry for 10.254.1.0 supplies a new Ethernet header: destination spine1's port MAC, ending in 53 81, source leaf1's, ending in 53 80.

@7
The packet leaves on port 49 towards spine1, typically under a microsecond to a few microseconds after it arrived. Look at the counters: port 49 has sent 1 packet, and packets seen by the CPU is still 0. Transit traffic is forwarded entirely in hardware.

@8
Now a different packet arrives on port 49: a BGP KEEPALIVE from the route reflector rr1.ams, 10.255.1.101, to leaf1's own loopback, 10.255.1.11, on TCP port 179, marked DSCP 48, network control. Same parser.

@9
This time the longest match is that receive entry, leaf1's own slash 32. The packet is for the switch, not through it, so instead of choosing an egress port the chip traps it towards the CPU. That is a punt.

@10
Punted packets pass control-plane policing: a queue and a rate limiter per class, because the CPU handles a tiny fraction of what the ports can deliver. BGP and IS-IS each have a protected queue, ARP a low rate, TTL-expired packets a very low rate. A flood of ARP is dropped at its own policer and cannot starve the routing protocols. The KEEPALIVE passes, and the BGP class counts 1.

@11
Over PCIe, the kernel's TCP stack delivers it to bgpd, the BGP daemon in FRRouting, and bgpd resets the session's hold timer. Route changes travel the other way: bgpd updates the RIB, and the network operating system programs the ASIC's FIB through the chip vendor's SDK over the same link.

@12
The last table says which signal proves what. An Established session proves the punt path, policing, the CPU and bgpd work. A route in the RIB proves the control plane chose it. An entry in the hardware FIB proves the ASIC can forward it. Drop counters show what the ASIC actually did. The SYN depended only on tables the CPU had written; the KEEPALIVE depended on the CPU itself. So a failed FIB write, a full hardware table or a wrong ACL drops traffic while every protocol looks healthy. That is why tooling compares the RIB with the hardware FIB and watches drop counters, and why a ping to a switch's loopback measures the punt path and its policer, not the path your traffic takes.
