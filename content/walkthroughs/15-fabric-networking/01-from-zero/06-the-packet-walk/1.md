@0
On screen is a small slice of Meridian's Amsterdam fabric. Acme's web server, acme-web1 at 10.1.1.10, sits behind leaf1. Acme's database, acme-db1 at 10.1.2.20, sits behind leaf3. Spine1 is in the middle, and spine2 is greyed out. For this walk, treat the leaves and the spine as plain IP routers. Hold onto one sentence, because it is the whole picture: the Ethernet header is a label for one link and is rebuilt at every router, while the IP header is the end-to-end label that every router reads and almost never changes.

@1
acme-web1 decides first. 10.1.2.20 is not inside its own subnet, 10.1.1.0 slash 24, so the default route sends the packet to the gateway, 10.1.1.1. The gateway's MAC address, the anycast gateway MAC ending in 01 01, is already in its ARP cache, so nothing has to wait.

@2
Now it builds the packet, and the header stack shows three layers. TCP, from port 51514 to PostgreSQL's port 5432, with the SYN flag set. IPv4, from 10.1.1.10 to 10.1.2.20, with a TTL of 64. And Ethernet, addressed to the gateway's MAC, not to the database's. A destination MAC only ever names the next device on this link.

@3
The frame crosses the first link: destination the gateway MAC, source acme-web1's own MAC ending in 53 0A, and a TTL of 64 inside.

@4
leaf1 accepts the frame because the destination MAC is its own gateway MAC. It throws the Ethernet header away, which is why the stack now starts at IPv4, and looks up 10.1.2.20 in its forwarding table. The longest match is 10.1.2.0 slash 24, with two equal-cost next hops, one per spine. The hash of this flow picks spine1, at 10.254.1.0.

@5
Then the rewrite, and the two fields that light up are the only ones that change in the IP header. The TTL drops from 64 to 63. The header checksum covers the TTL, so it changes too, from E 9 7 D to E A 7 D in hex: the router patches it with one small addition instead of summing the whole header again. The neighbour table maps 10.254.1.0 to spine1's MAC, so leaf1 writes a brand-new Ethernet header, from its own uplink port to spine1's port.

@6
The packet crosses the leaf1 to spine1 link, a slash 31, inside that new Ethernet header. Source and destination IP are exactly what acme-web1 wrote.

@7
spine1 accepts the frame because it is addressed to its own MAC, strips the Ethernet header, and looks up the same destination. A spine learns every leaf's subnets from the underlay routing protocol, and here 10.1.2.0 slash 24 has a single next hop: leaf3, at 10.254.1.5.

@8
Same rewrite, one hop later. The TTL goes to 62, the checksum is updated again, and spine1 writes another new Ethernet header, from its port facing leaf3 to leaf3's uplink MAC, which its neighbour table holds for 10.254.1.5.

@9
Down to leaf3 over the 10.254.1.4 link. Two routers done, two Ethernet headers replaced, and the IP addresses still untouched.

@10
On leaf3 the longest match for 10.1.2.20 is 10.1.2.0 slash 24, directly connected on VLAN 200. This is the last router, so the next hop is not another router: it is the destination itself, and leaf3 already has acme-db1's MAC in its ARP table.

@11
leaf3 takes the TTL to 61, updates the checksum, and writes the last Ethernet header. The destination is acme-db1's MAC, ending in 53 14. The source is the gateway MAC ending in 01 01, the very same address that was the destination on the first link, because every leaf answers as the same anycast gateway.

@12
The frame reaches acme-db1. It accepts it because the destination MAC is its own, the destination IP is its own, and PostgreSQL is listening on port 5432.

@13
The summary table says it all. Three routers, three new Ethernet headers, and a TTL three lower, 64 down to 61. The IP addresses, the TCP ports and the payload never changed. That is why a network needs both labels: the MAC pair gets the frame across one link and then its job ends, and the IP pair is what every router reads to choose the next link. Two consequences to keep. Because each frame is new, a CRC error belongs to one link and nothing else. And because each router lowers the TTL, a routing loop is bounded, and traceroute can make every router on the path identify itself, which is the next picture.
