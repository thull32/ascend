@0
The question is how RoCE handles congestion, and this run backs the answer with numbers. DCQCN, RoCE's standard congestion control, has three roles: rail1-leaf1 in San Jose is the congestion point that marks packets, srv3's NIC is the notification point that reports the marks, and the NICs on srv1 and srv2 are reaction points that set their own rates. The parameters are the ones published with DCQCN, on 400 gigabit links; production values are tuned per platform. The one idea: the switch marks instead of dropping, and the sender's NIC cuts its own rate when the receiver reports the marks.

@1
For the first 20 microseconds only srv1 sends, at the full 400 gigabits per second that srv3's port can carry, so the queue stays empty. Each packet's two-bit ECN field is set to one zero, ECT zero, which tells the switches that this sender reacts to marks.

@2
At 20 microseconds srv2 joins, straight at line rate, because DCQCN has no slow start. 800 gigabits per second now arrives for a 400 gigabit port, and the queue grows by 50 kilobytes every microsecond.

@3
The queue passes Kmin, 5 kilobytes, almost at once, and Kmax, 200 kilobytes, at 24 microseconds. Between the two, at most 1 percent of packets are marked, so a mark is unlikely in that short window. Above Kmax the switch marks every packet.

@4
rail1-leaf1 sets the ECN field to one one, congestion experienced, and still forwards the packet: marking replaces dropping. It marks as the packet joins the queue, so the mark waits 4 microseconds behind 200 kilobytes before srv3 sees it. That wait is part of the control loop.

@5
At 28 microseconds srv3's NIC receives marked packets on both connections and answers each sender with a CNP, a congestion notification packet, addressed to the sender's queue pair and sent in a strict-priority queue, so it overtakes the queued data. It sends at most one per queue pair every 50 microseconds, however many marks arrive.

@6
The CNPs land at 29 microseconds and each sender halves its rate, from 400 to 200 gigabits per second. Together they now send exactly 400, so the queue stops growing, but it is already 450 kilobytes deep.

@7
400 in and 400 out, so the 450 kilobyte queue stands still, adding 9 microseconds to every packet, and every packet is marked. srv3 stays silent until 50 microseconds have passed since its last CNP. Each sender's share of the queue stays below the switch's PFC threshold, so no pause is sent.

@8
At 78 microseconds srv3 may send again, and at 79 both senders cut a second time, to 100 gigabits per second each. Now 200 arrives for a 400 gigabit port, and the queue drains at 25 kilobytes per microsecond.

@9
The queue is empty at 97 microseconds, and no third CNP follows. The link now runs half idle until the senders climb back: DCQCN trades a short spell of under-use for an empty queue.

@10-11
55 microseconds after the last CNP, with no new one, fast recovery begins: each sender moves halfway back to its target of 200, first to 150 gigabits per second. Every further 55 microseconds without a CNP halves the remaining gap: 175, 187.5, 193.75, then 196.875 at 354 microseconds. The pair is back to 98.4 percent of srv3's port, and the queue is still empty.

@12
Then additive increase takes over: the target rises by 40 megabits per second, and each sender's rate moves halfway towards it, to about 198.5 gigabits per second.

@13
From here the senders creep up until their total passes 400, a mark produces a CNP, and the cycle of cut and recovery repeats with the queue mostly near empty, so PFC rarely has to fire. That answers the follow-up, isn't PFC enough on its own? PFC is flow control: it stops a link, not the source. ECN with DCQCN slows the source and keeps queues short.

To see this loop in production, watch both halves: the switch's ECN marks and PFC pauses per queue, next to the NIC's marked packets received and CNPs sent at the receiver, and CNPs handled at the senders.
