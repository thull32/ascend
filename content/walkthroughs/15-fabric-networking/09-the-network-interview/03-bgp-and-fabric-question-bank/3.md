@0
The question is how you detect a link failure fast, and this run shows the failure that makes it hard. Amsterdam to Singapore, small example: leaf1 in Amsterdam sends 80 gigabits per second towards leaf2 in Singapore, split by ECMP over the two long-haul circuits, about 40 each. Circuit 1 is delivered through a carrier's optical network, so the light that spine1 in Amsterdam receives comes from the carrier's equipment, not from the far spine. The one idea: a port only sees its own light, and BFD exists for the failures the port cannot see.

@1
The two spine1 switches, one in each site, run a BFD session, bidirectional forwarding detection, across the circuit. Each sends a small UDP packet every 300 milliseconds and declares the session down after 3 intervals without one: 900 milliseconds. IS-IS is registered as a BFD client on this link, because its own hellos, every 10 seconds with a 30 second hold time, are far too slow.

@2
At time zero a fibre breaks inside the carrier's network. The carrier's equipment keeps sending both spines light, so their ports stay up and neither notices. Every packet hashed onto circuit 1, half of leaf1's Singapore traffic, is now being lost.

@3
spine1 in Amsterdam expects a BFD packet every 300 milliseconds, and none arrives, so its count of missed intervals climbs towards 3. IS-IS, left to its own timer, would still call the adjacency up.

@4
At 900 milliseconds spine1 declares the BFD session down and tells IS-IS at once, which drops the adjacency. The Singapore spine reaches the same verdict independently. BFD has done its whole job, and its job is only detection: it reroutes nothing itself, its client protocols act.

@5
Now the follow-up: detection takes 900 milliseconds, so where does the rest go? spine1 waits its initial LSP generation delay, 50 milliseconds, in case more changes arrive to batch, then floods a new link-state packet without circuit 1. It reaches the Amsterdam leaves about 2 milliseconds later, and Singapore does the same.

@6
leaf1 waits its SPF delay, another 50 milliseconds, then runs SPF in about 1 millisecond. Its route to leaf2's loopback, 10.255.2.12, now has a single next hop, spine2. Until every leaf has updated, a few packets bounce between spine1 and a leaf still pointing at it: a microloop.

@7
The new route is written into the forwarding ASIC in about 15 milliseconds, because every affected prefix shares one ECMP group and only the group is rewritten. From here every flow to Singapore leaves through spine2 and circuit 2, and traffic is flowing again.

@8
In total, about 1,018 milliseconds from cut to recovered traffic: 900 of detection, and 118 for generation, flooding, SPF and the FIB write. At 40 gigabits per second that is about 5.1 gigabytes lost on circuit 1. TCP retransmits and connections survive, but the affected flows stall for at least that long.

@9
Without BFD the only detector is IS-IS itself. With 10 second hellos and a 30 second hold time, the adjacency dies up to 30 seconds after the cut, and the same 118 milliseconds follow: about 30.1 seconds and 150.6 gigabytes lost. Even hellos tuned to 1 second are 3.3 times slower than BFD here.

@10
Had the fibre run straight between the two spines, loss of light would have taken the port down within milliseconds, BFD or not. The next rung asks why not 10 milliseconds, three missed. False positives: a BFD process on a busy CPU misses its own packets and declares a healthy link dead, so timers far below 100 milliseconds usually need BFD in the line card or ASIC.

That is the shape of the whole answer. Detection dominates convergence, so it is the number to shrink, within what the platform runs safely. And what to watch is BFD flaps against CPU load, and the links that should have a BFD session but do not.
