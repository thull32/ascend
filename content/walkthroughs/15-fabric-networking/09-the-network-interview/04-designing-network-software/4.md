@0
Meridian's Amsterdam fabric, a small example with 4 spines and 4 leaves on 400 gigabit links; spine1 and spine2 also carry the two 100 gigabit circuits to Singapore. Tonight's change upgrades spine2.ams's operating system, which means a reboot, and the goal is to do it without dropping a single packet. The idea that makes it work: an upgrade is a drain. Move the traffic away first, and the reboot becomes an ordinary spine failure that nothing notices.

@1
The pre-check does the arithmetic before anything changes. With one of four spines drained, each leaf spreads its traffic over three uplinks, so each one carries four thirds of its load. At two in the afternoon, leaf3's 270 gigabits per second per uplink would become 360, which is 90 percent of 400, and all 90 gigabits per second to Singapore would land on spine1's single 100 gigabit circuit. Both are above Meridian's 80 percent limit, so the tool refuses to start.

@2
In the two in the morning window, the same check passes. leaf3 goes from 165 to 220 gigabits per second per uplink, 55 percent, and the Singapore circuits go from 35 each to 70 on one, 70 percent. The state checks pass too: all four spines are in service, because draining one while another is already out would halve the fabric; 18 IS-IS adjacencies are up; and every leaf has four equal-cost paths.

@3
The drain. spine2 raises the IS-IS metric on all five of its links, normally 10 on the four leaf links and 52,500 on the Singapore circuit, to 16,777,214, the largest value that still leaves a link usable as a last resort, and floods a new link-state packet saying so. Every leaf reruns its shortest path calculation. Through spine2 a path now costs 16,777,224, against 20 through any other spine, so spine2 leaves every equal-cost group. Setting the IS-IS overload bit is the other common way, with one difference: overload forbids transit through spine2 even when it is the only path left, while the maximum metric keeps it as a last resort.

@4
Traffic moves off spine2 as each leaf updates its forwarding table. leaf3's uplinks go from four at 165 to three at 220 gigabits per second, and Singapore traffic shifts onto spine1's circuit, 70 of its 100. No packet needs to be lost, because spine2 keeps forwarding whatever still reaches it until every leaf has moved. Flows that change spine may see a few packets reordered, once.

@5
Before touching spine2, verify it is empty. Its five ports carry 10 to 20 kilobits per second each, just the IS-IS hellos, BFD and LLDP that keep its adjacencies up. No fabric uplink is above 55 percent, the Singapore circuit runs at the predicted 70 percent, and there are no discards. The drain did what the pre-check said it would.

@6
Now the work. spine2 reboots into the new release. Its links go dark for several minutes and the leaves see five adjacencies drop, which change-aware monitoring expects for this change, so it pages nobody. No traffic notices, because none was using those links.

@7
spine2 comes back on the new release, and because the high metric is part of its saved configuration, it returns still costed out: adjacencies form, but it attracts no traffic. That is the moment to check it, before it carries anything: five adjacencies up, the same 52 routes as spine1, every optic lane in range and zero errors.

@8
Restore. spine2 sets its metrics back to 10 on the leaf links and 52,500 on the circuit, floods a new link-state packet, and every leaf puts it back into its equal-cost groups. leaf3's uplinks return to four at 165 gigabits per second, and the two Singapore circuits to 35 each.

@9
The final check compares against the pre-check snapshot: four spines carrying equal shares again, 18 adjacencies, zero discards and clean probes across every spine. Both snapshots and every step's timestamp go on the change record, which feeds the visibility platform's question of what changed. Now scale it to the fleet. At about 45 minutes a spine, Amsterdam's four take three hours in series, and sites can only upgrade in parallel where blast radius allows: spine1.ams and spine2.sin out together would cut both Amsterdam to Singapore circuits. So the scheduler runs blast radius on every set of devices it plans to have out at once, and the pre-check you just watched becomes one gate in a fleet-wide plan.
