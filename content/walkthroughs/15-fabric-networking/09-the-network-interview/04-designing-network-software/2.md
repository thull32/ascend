@0
Acme reports that some requests from its web servers behind leaf1.ams to its databases behind leaf3.ams are slow, intermittently, while others are fine. On screen, Amsterdam with all four spines, and a probe agent behind each leaf: 10.250.11.10 behind leaf1, and 10.250.13.10 behind leaf3. The one idea to hold: counters describe devices, users experience paths, and a probe only tests the path its five-tuple hashes onto.

@1
First, the tool everyone reaches for. A ping between the two agents: 100 replies out of 100, zero percent loss. But ICMP has no ports, so to the hash every echo between these two addresses looks the same. leaf1 sends all of them through one spine, here spine1, and ping has tested one path out of four.

@2
So the agent runs TWAMP Light, the test half of RFC 5357: 16 UDP streams to port 862, 10 packets a second each, identical except for the source port, 50001 to 50016. The reflector behind leaf3 timestamps each packet and returns it with its own sequence number, so loss can be split by direction. A different source port is a different five-tuple, so leaf1's equal-cost hash spreads the streams across the spines.

@3
To learn which spine each stream uses, the agent sends one packet with that stream's exact five-tuple but a TTL of 2. It expires at the spine, and the time exceeded message comes back from that spine's address on the link, 10.254.3.0 for spine3. The hash ignores the TTL, so this packet takes exactly the stream's path. The count: 4, 5, 3 and 4 streams on spine1 to spine4.

@4
Five minutes later, 3,000 packets per stream. Three streams lost about 4 percent from leaf1 to leaf3, while their 99th percentile round trip stayed at 61 microseconds like everyone else's, and the rest lost nothing apart from one packet of noise. Averaged over all 16 streams, the loss is 0.7 percent. That average is the number a single fabric-wide dashboard would show, and it hides a path that is badly broken.

@5
Group the results by spine and the picture snaps into place: every degraded stream crosses spine3, and no stream through another spine is degraded. Acme's own connections hash the same way, so about a quarter of them cross spine3 and suffer retransmissions. That is why the complaint was sometimes slow, rather than down.

@6
Is it spine3 itself, or one of its links? Add the agent behind leaf2 and you get two more pairs. Its 5 streams through spine3 to leaf3 also lose about 4 percent, while 5 streams from leaf1 to leaf2 through spine3 lose nothing. The only element every bad path shares, and the good path lacks, is the link from spine3 down to leaf3.

@7
The counters at the two ends agree. spine3 sent 3 billion frames on that link in five minutes; leaf3 received 2.883 billion intact and discarded 117 million, 3.9 percent, that failed the frame check. One receive lane of leaf3's optic reads minus 8.6 decibel milliwatts against about minus 2 on the others, too weak for forward error correction to repair every error. Yet the link stays up, so ECMP keeps sending it a quarter of the traffic bound for leaf3.

@8
The fix: Meridian costs out that one link, raising its IS-IS metric at both ends so no shortest path uses it, because a metric only governs traffic leaving its own end and the damaged direction is spine3 to leaf3. leaf1's next hops for leaf3's prefixes drop to three spines. The same 16 streams rehash over spine1, spine2 and spine4 and lose nothing, which confirms the diagnosis while the optic replacement is scheduled.

@9
Two things made this work: enough distinct five-tuples to cover every path, and grouping results by the path each probe took, which is why the prober records its path mapping in the graph. Even with 16 random source ports over 4 spines, a given spine still has a 1 percent chance of getting no stream at all, so real probing systems map their paths and fill the gaps deliberately. And the alerting rule: alert on the worst path between a pair, never on the mean. Ping, with its single five-tuple, would still be saying zero percent loss.
