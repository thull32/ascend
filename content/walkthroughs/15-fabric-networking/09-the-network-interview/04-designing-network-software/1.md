@0
On screen, a small slice of Amsterdam: two spines, two leaves, and two ways of watching leaf2.ams. The collector, 10.250.1.10 behind leaf4, holds a gNMI subscription, and leaf2 streams its interface counters to it every 10 seconds over the data network, through spine1. Off to the side, an out-of-band monitor reaches leaf2's management port, 172.16.1.12, through a separate management switch. Hold onto the rule this design is built on: bulk data in band, heartbeats and the alert path out of band. Watch why it needs both halves.

@1
At five seconds past ten, a Globex batch job starts offering about 1.1 terabits per second towards the spines, more than leaf2's 800 gigabits per second of uplink. The telemetry is marked DSCP zero, so it waits in the same best effort queue as the job and is tail dropped along with it. TCP backs off after each loss, and the stream stalls. The thing being watched carries the watching, and it has just blinded you.

@2
At thirty seconds past ten, the collector has missed three samples in a row and raises an absence of data alert. That is the only alert that fires. There is no bad value to alert on, and without it the dashboard would keep showing leaf2's numbers from ten o'clock as if they were current. This is why absence of data has to be a first-class signal.

@3
Now the question that matters: is leaf2 dead, or alive and unable to get its data out? The gNMI session's own keepalive, an HTTP 2 ping, goes unanswered either way, because it travels the same path as the data. A signal that shares the failed path cannot tell you anything about that path.

@4
So ask over a different one. The out-of-band monitor pings the management port, 172.16.1.12, over the management network the batch job never touches, and the reply comes back in 0.6 milliseconds. leaf2 is powered, its operating system is running, and its CPU answers.

@5
Then ask the neighbours, whose own telemetry still flows. spine1 and spine2 report their links to leaf2 up, their IS-IS adjacencies to it up, and 399 gigabits per second arriving from it on each 400 gigabit port. The routing hellos still get through because switches send routing protocol packets in a network control queue, ahead of user traffic.

@6
Verdict, at thirty-five seconds past ten: alive and congested, not dead. The platform shows leaf2 as telemetry blind, neither down nor green, and raises the real problem, saturated uplinks, from the spines' counters. The device's own telemetry stopped exactly when it had the most important thing to say, and two sources that do not share the failed path, the heartbeat and the neighbours, said it instead.

@7
At four minutes and five seconds past ten, the job finishes. The old session did not survive four minutes of unanswered keepalives, so the collector closed it and kept re-dialling; now a new subscription comes up and the samples flow again. The ones it never received are stored as a gap from a device known to be alive, never as zeros, so no graph shows a fake dip to idle in the middle of the busiest four minutes.

@8
The follow-up change marks telemetry DSCP 16, the CS2 class set aside for operations and management traffic, and gives that class its own queue with a guaranteed share, so the next saturation cannot silence it. Now generalise it to the whole platform: design every health signal so that some second source can confirm it over a different path. A visibility platform breaks by going blind with the fabric it rides, and the signal that shows it is exactly what you just watched, an absence of data alert, settled by witnesses on other paths, so the platform can say "I am blind here" instead of guessing.
