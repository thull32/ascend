@0
This is drill six, two ISPs. Meridian, AS 64500, announcing 203.0.113.0 slash 24, has gw1 in Amsterdam on ISP1, AS 64501, and gw2 on ISP2, AS 64502. The goal: use ISP1 in both directions and fall back to ISP2 only if ISP1 fails, shown here for traffic to and from a content network, AS 64511. Hold onto one distinction, because the whole answer turns on it: outbound, Meridian decides; inbound, other networks decide.

@1
Start with no policy. gw2 has two paths to the content network's prefix: its own, through ISP2, and one learned over iBGP from gw1, through ISP1. They tie on local preference, 100 each, and on AS path length, 2 each. MED is not compared, because the paths come from different neighbouring ASes. The next step, eBGP over iBGP, makes each gateway keep its own ISP, so outbound traffic leaves by whichever gateway is closer.

@2-3
The outbound fix is one line of import policy on gw1: every route learned from ISP1 gets local preference 200. Local preference travels to every router in AS 64500 over iBGP and is compared before path length. Now gw2 sees 200 against its own 100 and prefers the path through gw1 before the eBGP step is ever reached. Every router in Meridian exits via ISP1. Outbound is fully under control because Meridian makes the decision.

@4
Inbound is decided elsewhere. Meridian announces its slash 24 with AS path 64500 to both ISPs, so AS 64511 sees two paths of length 2, and its own tie-breakers happen to pick ISP2. Traffic leaves by ISP1 and returns by ISP2.

@5-6
First tool, prepending. gw2 adds its own AS twice more to what it sends ISP2, so the content network now compares length 2 through ISP1 with length 4 through ISP2, and moves to ISP1. The prepended path is still valid; it just loses wherever path length is compared. MED could not have done this: it only chooses between two links to one ISP.

@7
Prepending has a limit, and this is the step most answers miss. ISP2 gives routes from its paying customers local preference 200 and routes from peers 100, and local preference comes before path length. So ISP2 keeps the direct path to Meridian, prepended or not, and everything ISP2's own customers send still arrives via gw2.

@8
Second tool, the upstream's own community. ISP2 publishes that a customer route tagged 64502 colon 70 gets local preference 70 inside ISP2. Meridian tags its announcement, 70 is now below ISP2's peer value of 100, and ISP2 itself sends traffic for Meridian over its peering with ISP1.

@9
Third tool, the strongest: more-specifics. gw1 also announces the two halves, slash 25s, to ISP1 only, and the slash 24 still goes to both. Every network that accepts them sends Meridian's traffic via ISP1 by longest-prefix match, whatever its local preference or path lengths say. Longer prefix, more specific, wins. On the real internet most networks drop IPv4 routes longer than slash 24, so in production this is two slash 24s split from a slash 23.

@10
Now the failure the design is for: the fibre between gw1 and ISP1 is cut. Both ends see loss of light and drop the eBGP session at once. A failure that leaves the light on would be noticed only by BFD or by the hold timer, 90 seconds as the BGP standard suggests.

@11
Outbound: gw1 withdraws its ISP1 routes over iBGP, the local preference 200 paths disappear, and gw2's own route through ISP2 becomes best. Outbound traffic moves within seconds.

@12
Inbound: ISP1 withdraws the slash 25s and the slash 24, and every network falls back to the prepended slash 24 via ISP2. Prepending and local preference 70 make a path less preferred, never unusable, so the backup carries everything once it is the only path. Those withdrawals take seconds to minutes to spread across the internet.

@13
The general rule: outbound, you choose your exit with local preference and the choice is exact. Inbound, every other network chooses with its own policy, so you can only make one path look worse or more specific. Say influence, not control, and then say how you would measure where traffic actually arrives, and how long the last failover test took.
