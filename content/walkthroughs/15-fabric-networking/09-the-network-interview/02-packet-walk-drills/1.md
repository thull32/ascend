@0
This is drill two, the VXLAN walk, played out on Meridian. On the left, acme-web1 at 10.1.1.10 behind leaf1 in Amsterdam; on the right, acme-web2 at 10.1.1.11 behind leaf2 in Singapore. Both sit in the same Acme subnet, VLAN 100, which Meridian carries as VNI 10100. Between them is a routed underlay: two spines and a 10,500 kilometre circuit. Hold onto one sentence, because it is the whole answer: the ingress leaf wraps the tenant's frame in 50 bytes of headers, every device in the middle reads only the outer header, and the egress leaf unwraps it.

@1-2
acme-web1 sends an ordinary Ethernet frame. Same subnet, so the destination MAC is acme-web2's own, ending in 53 0B, and no router is involved: the inner TTL is 64 and stays 64. Inside is a 1,500 byte IP packet carrying TCP to port 443. The frame enters leaf1 untagged on an access port, exactly as it would enter any switch. The server has no idea a tunnel exists.

@3
Layer 2, at the ingress VTEP. leaf1 maps VLAN 100 on this port to VNI 10100 and looks the destination MAC up in that segment's table. The table says it lives behind VTEP 10.255.2.12, leaf2's loopback, learned from an EVPN type-2 route. So the frame cannot be switched out of a local port; it must be tunnelled.

@4-5
Encapsulation builds from the inside out. First 8 bytes of VXLAN: a flags byte saying the VNI is valid, and VNI 10100, which tells the far end which tenant segment this is. Then 8 bytes of UDP: destination port 4789, the VXLAN port, and source port 58479, a hash of the inner headers. One flow keeps one source port and so one path; different flows get different ports for the underlay's ECMP hash to spread.

@6-7
Next 20 bytes of outer IPv4, from VTEP 10.255.1.11 to VTEP 10.255.2.12, protocol UDP, TTL 64, total length 1,550. The underlay route to that loopback has two equal-cost next hops, and the hash picks spine1. Last, 14 bytes of outer Ethernet to spine1's port MAC. 14 plus 20 plus 8 plus 8 is 50 bytes, so a 1,500 byte inner packet needs an underlay IP MTU of at least 1,550. That is why fabrics run jumbo frames; Meridian uses 9,216.

@8
The packet leaves leaf1. To the underlay it is just a UDP packet from one loopback to another; the tenant's frame is payload.

@9
Layer 3, at spine1 in Amsterdam. It reads only the outer destination, 10.255.2.12, finds the route towards spine1 in Singapore over the long-haul circuit, decrements the outer TTL to 63 and rewrites the outer MACs. Look at what it knows about VNI 10100: no MACs and no tenant routes. The inner frame passes through unread.

@10
Across the circuit: about 10,500 kilometres of fibre at roughly 5 microseconds per kilometre, so about 52 and a half milliseconds one way, far more than every switch on the path adds together.

@11-12
spine1 in Singapore does the same thing. The outer destination is leaf2's loopback, one hop down, so the outer TTL becomes 62 and the outer MACs are rewritten again. The VXLAN header and the inner frame are still exactly the bytes leaf1 wrote.

@13
At leaf2, the egress VTEP, three checks: the outer destination is its own VTEP address, the UDP port is 4789, and VNI 10100 maps to its local Acme VLAN 100. It strips all 50 bytes, and the original frame's destination MAC is on a local port.

@14-15
leaf2 switches the frame out to acme-web2, untagged, with acme-web1's source MAC and the inner TTL still 64. acme-web2 receives byte for byte what was sent, as if both servers sat on one switch.

The pattern to say in the interview: EVPN was used once, at the ingress leaf, to find the far VTEP, and IS-IS carried the packet at every hop. The outer TTL went from 64 to 62; the inner one never moved. The slips follow from the picture: the spines never run EVPN or learn tenant MACs, VXLAN is UDP and not TCP, and one underlay link left at 1,500 bytes drops every full-size tunnelled packet while small pings pass.
