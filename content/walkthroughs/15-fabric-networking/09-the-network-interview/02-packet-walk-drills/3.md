@0
This is the localisation step of drill four, the latency that jumped. Acme reports that some requests from its web servers behind leaf1 to its databases behind leaf3 are slow, intermittently, while others are fine. On screen is Meridian's Amsterdam fabric with all four spines, and a probe agent behind each leaf. Hold onto one idea, because it is how you search tens of thousands of links: use enough different five-tuples to cover every path, and group the results by the path each probe took.

@1
Start with what most people try first. A ping between the two agents: 100 sent, 100 received, zero loss. But ICMP has no ports, so to the ECMP hash every echo between these two addresses looks the same. leaf1 sends all of them through one spine, here spine1, and the ping tested one path out of four.

@2
So the agent runs TWAMP Light: timestamped UDP packets bounced off a reflector behind leaf3. Sixteen streams to port 862, identical except for the source port, 50001 to 50016. A different source port is a different five-tuple, so leaf1's hash spreads the streams across the spines.

@3
To learn which spine each stream crossed, the agent sends one packet with the stream's exact five-tuple but a TTL of 2, which expires at the spine. The Time Exceeded reply comes from that spine's address on the link, 10.254.3.0 for spine3. The hash ignores the TTL, so this packet takes exactly the stream's path. The count: 4 streams on spine1, 5 on spine2, 3 on spine3 and 4 on spine4.

@4
After five minutes, the results. Three streams lost about 4 percent, while their 99th percentile round trip stayed at 61 microseconds like everyone else's. The rest lost nothing but one packet of noise. Averaged over all 16 streams the loss is 0.7 percent, which is why a single fabric-wide number hides a path that is badly broken.

@5
Grouped by spine, the picture is plain: every degraded stream crosses spine3, and no stream through another spine is degraded. Acme's own connections hash the same way, so about a quarter of them cross spine3 and suffer retransmissions. That is why the complaint is sometimes slow rather than down: the customer's latency is TCP recovering from loss.

@6
Is it spine3 itself, or one of its links? Add the agent behind leaf2. Its 5 streams through spine3 to leaf3 also lose about 4 percent, while 5 streams from leaf1 to leaf2 through spine3 lose nothing. The only element every bad path shares, and the good path lacks, is the link from spine3 down to leaf3.

@7
Now read that link at both ends. In five minutes spine3 sent 3 billion frames; leaf3 received about 2.88 billion intact and discarded 117 million, 3.9 percent, failing the frame check. One receive lane of leaf3's optic reads minus 8.6 dBm against about minus 2 on the others, too weak for forward error correction to repair every error. And both ends still say up, so ECMP keeps sending that link its share of the traffic.

@8
The fix: Meridian costs out that one link, raising its IS-IS metric at both ends so no shortest path uses it. leaf1's next hops for leaf3's prefixes drop to three spines, the same 16 streams rehash over spine1, spine2 and spine4, and every one shows zero loss. That confirms the diagnosis while the optic replacement is scheduled.

@9
The general lesson: frames sent at one end against frames received at the other turn somewhere into this link, but only after hashed probes have named the suspects. Coverage is not free, either: with 16 random source ports and four spines, a given spine still has about a 1 percent chance of getting no stream at all, so real probing systems map their paths and fill the gaps on purpose. And the slip to avoid in the interview: pinging once and declaring the network clean.
