@0
This is the picture behind the second part of the two-minute answer, the intra-site fabric. On screen is Meridian's Amsterdam fabric in its small form: four spines along the top, four leaves below, and Acme servers behind leaf 1 and leaf 3. Every leaf has exactly one link to every spine, sixteen links in all, and no link joins two leaves or two spines. That one wiring rule is what the sentence in your answer rests on: two hops, four paths, a quarter per spine.

@1
Here are the two hops. Any leaf reaches any other leaf by going up to a spine and down again. From leaf 1 to leaf 3 there are four such paths, one through each spine, all the same length, so every pair of leaves sees the same latency and the same number of paths.

@2
The underlay, IS-IS, gives every link a metric of 10, so each path costs 20. Four equal costs, so leaf 1 installs all four next hops for leaf 3's subnet, 10.1.2.0 slash 24, at once. That is equal-cost multipath, ECMP: the forwarding table holds a group of four next hops instead of one.

@3
Flow A arrives at leaf 1: from 10.1.1.10 to 10.1.2.20, port 5432, over TCP. The forwarding chip hashes the 5-tuple, the addresses, protocol and ports, takes the result modulo 4 and gets 0, so flow A goes up to spine 1. Every later packet of flow A hashes the same way, so the flow stays on one path and in order.

@4
spine 1 has one route to 10.1.2.0 slash 24, via leaf 3, so the second hop has no choice to make, and flow A is delivered in two hops. One honest caveat, which the caption makes too: this run routes Acme's subnet directly to keep the hashing visible. In Meridian the leaf hashes VXLAN packets addressed to leaf 3's loopback, and the spines never hold the tenant prefix.

@5-7
Flow B differs from A in its destination and ports, so its hash is unrelated: modulo 4 it gives 1 and picks spine 2. Flow C hashes to 2 and goes to spine 3, and flow D hashes to 3 and goes to spine 4.

@8-9
Then the lumps. Flow E hashes to 1 and joins B on spine 2, and flow F hashes to 2 and joins C on spine 3. The hash spreads flows, not bytes, and never looks at how busy a spine already is.

@10
All six together: one, two, two and one over spine 1 to spine 4. With a handful of flows the split is lumpy; with thousands of flows of similar size it evens out. That is the bet ECMP makes.

@11
leaf 1's four uplinks of 400 gigabits per second give it 1,600 towards the spines, and each spine carries about a quarter. So losing one spine costs a quarter of the capacity, not connectivity, and adding one adds a path without redesigning anything. That is the number the panel listens for. It also sets up the follow-up: a link that is up but corrupting packets still receives its quarter of the flows, so one connection in four crawls while a ping looks clean. The signal is CRC errors on one port, and probes with varied 5-tuples that fail only through one spine.
