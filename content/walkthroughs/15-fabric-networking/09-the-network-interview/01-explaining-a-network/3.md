@0
This is the packet part of the one story, with every lookup visible, because a panel may stop you at any hop and ask what that device reads. acme-web1 in Amsterdam sends to acme-web2 in Singapore, both in Acme's 10.1.1.0 slash 24 on VLAN 100, VXLAN segment 10100. The path runs through leaf 1 in Amsterdam, a spine in each site, the long-haul circuit, and leaf 2 in Singapore. Hold onto the sentence that explains every hop: BGP says which leaf, IS-IS says how to get there.

@1
Lookup 1 is on acme-web1 itself. 10.1.1.11 is inside its own subnet, so no router is involved, and the frame must carry acme-web2's own MAC address. Its ARP cache has only the gateway, so it has to ask.

@2-3
It broadcasts an ARP request, and leaf 1 receives it. Lookup 2: leaf 1's ARP suppression table already pairs 10.1.1.11 with acme-web2's MAC, learned from leaf 2's EVPN route, so leaf 1 answers locally and nothing is flooded to Singapore.

@4
acme-web1 caches the answer and sends its data: a frame to acme-web2's MAC carrying a 1,500 byte packet, TTL 64, with a TCP segment for port 443.

@5
Lookups 3 and 4 are on leaf 1. The access port in VLAN 100 maps to segment 10100, and in that segment's MAC table acme-web2 sits behind a remote tunnel endpoint, 10.255.2.12, leaf 2's loopback. That is the which-leaf half, and it came from BGP.

@6
Lookup 5 is the how-to-get-there half. 10.255.2.12 is a BGP next hop, so leaf 1 resolves it in its underlay table, where IS-IS gives two equal paths of metric 1,020, one through each spine. It wraps the frame in VXLAN, inside UDP to port 4789, with an outer IP header from its own loopback, 10.255.1.11, to 10.255.2.12, and the hash of those outer headers picks spine 1.

@7
The tunnel packet is now 1,550 bytes, and from here on only the outer headers are read. Every underlay link must carry it, which is why fabrics run a jumbo MTU; at the default of 1,500 this packet would be dropped.

@8
Lookup 6, on spine 1 in Amsterdam: the outer destination matches the IS-IS route over its own circuit to spine 1 in Singapore, metric 1,010. It lowers the outer TTL to 63 and rewrites the outer MACs, nothing else. It has no idea that Acme exists.

@9
Across the circuit: 10,500 kilometres at about 5 microseconds per kilometre, roughly 52 and a half milliseconds one way, more than every lookup on the path put together.

@10-11
Lookup 7, on spine 1 in Singapore: 10.255.2.12 is one hop away, metric 10. Outer TTL 62, outer MACs rewritten again, and the VXLAN header and the inner frame are still exactly the bytes leaf 1 wrote. Then down the last link to leaf 2, which owns that address.

@12
Lookup 8, on leaf 2: the outer destination is its own tunnel endpoint, the UDP port is 4789, and segment 10100 maps to its local VLAN 100. It strips all 50 bytes of outer headers and the original frame comes out.

@13-14
Lookup 9: acme-web2's MAC is on a local port. leaf 2 does not learn acme-web1's MAC from this tunnelled frame, because EVPN already told it where acme-web1 lives. It bridges the frame out to acme-web2, still with acme-web1's source MAC and TTL 64, byte for byte what was sent.

@15
Nine lookups on five devices. The host and the two leaves read tenant state: routing table, ARP, segment and MAC tables learned through EVPN. The two spines read only the outer IP destination in an IS-IS table. That is why the spines need no BGP: encapsulation addresses the packet to something IS-IS already knows. When this path breaks, each lookup is a place to look, and the platform you describe as your part should be able to show every one of them.
