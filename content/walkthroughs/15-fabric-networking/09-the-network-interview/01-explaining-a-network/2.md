@0
The third part of the answer, the inter-site design, is really a question about cost: do you know why the backbone looks different from the fabric? On screen are Amsterdam and Singapore, in the small example with two spines and two leaves each, joined spine to spine by two leased circuits of 100 gigabits per second. Spines inside a site are not wired to each other, so the other two sides of this square run from a spine down through a leaf and up to its sister spine. The idea to hold: the square survives one cut only because either circuit alone can carry everything.

@1
Inside a site, links are cheap cables, so you build every path. Between sites each link is a lease, a separate contract, route and failure point. A full mesh of spines would need four circuits; the square gets the same reachability from two by reusing links Meridian already owns, and pays by losing half its capacity when one fails.

@2
To IS-IS a circuit is just a link with a high metric, 1,000, against 10 inside a site. So leaf 1 in Amsterdam reaches leaf 2 in Singapore, loopback 10.255.2.12, over two equal paths of 1,020, one through each Amsterdam spine and its circuit.

@3
Amsterdam sends 80 gigabits per second to Singapore. ECMP at every leaf splits it over both spines, so each circuit carries about 40, which is 40 percent.

@4
Here is the first number that makes the answer credible: latency is distance. About 10,500 kilometres of fibre at roughly 5 microseconds per kilometre is 52 and a half milliseconds one way, at least 105 milliseconds round trip. The switches add only microseconds.

@5
A ship's anchor cuts the subsea cable carrying circuit 1. The carrier's equipment passes the failure on, so the spines' ports facing it go down within milliseconds, and the 40 gigabits per second riding circuit 1, with the packets in flight, has lost its path.

@6
spine 1 in Amsterdam reruns its shortest path calculation first. Its best way to Singapore is now down through a leaf, up to spine 2 and over circuit 2, metric 1,030. Packets the leaves are still sending it take that detour, two extra hops.

@7
Once the leaves rerun theirs, typically well under a second, the path through spine 1 costs 1,040 against 1,020 through spine 2, so every leaf sends its Singapore traffic straight to spine 2. Leaf to leaf, the path is no longer than before.

@8
And here is the second number: capacity on failure is half. All 80 gigabits per second now rides circuit 2, 80 percent of its 100. Half the inter-site capacity is gone, but the traffic fits. The signal is circuit 1's ports down and a step from 40 to 80 percent on circuit 2.

@9
That gives the planning rule you say in the answer: keep each circuit under half full at the busiest hour, so either one can carry both shares. And the two circuits must not share a cable, duct or landing station, or one anchor cuts both.

@10
The cable is repaired, the two equal paths return, and the load splits 40 and 40 again. This is the door the sentence opens: expect to be asked about latency, about capacity after a cut, and about shared risk. The signals to name are each circuit's busy-hour utilisation against 50 percent, and a shared-risk record per circuit.
