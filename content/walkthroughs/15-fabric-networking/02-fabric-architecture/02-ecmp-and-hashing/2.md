@0
A smaller slice of Meridian's Amsterdam fabric this time: two spines, leaf1 and leaf3, and the border gateways gw1 and gw2 above the spines. In this example leaf1 has two uplinks of 100 gigabits a second each, 200 in all, and hashes every flow towards leaf3 onto one of them. The idea to hold onto: the hash counts flows, and a flow can be a few kilobytes or 60 gigabits a second.

@1
Most flows are mice: thousands of short RPCs and web requests of a few kilobytes each, about 20 gigabits a second in total. With that many small flows the hash's randomness averages out, and the chart shows each uplink carrying about 10 gigabits a second, a tenth of its capacity.

@2
Then an elephant. A backup server starts writing a large dataset to an NFS storage server: flow E1, from port 875 to the NFS port, 2049. It is a single TCP connection that a tuned 100-gigabit host can drive at about 60 gigabits a second. Its hash is even, so modulo 2 it gives 0, and the whole elephant rides the uplink to spine1, which now carries 70.

@3
A second backup, E2, from a different server to a different storage server. Its hash is also even, so it gives 0 again and joins E1 on the same uplink: 130 gigabits a second offered to a 100-gigabit link, while the uplink to spine2 still carries 10. Overall leaf1 needs only 140 of its 200, but the hash never looks at load, so the spare capacity on the other uplink cannot be used.

@4
leaf1's egress queue towards spine1 fills and drops packets, and TCP congestion control backs both elephants off until they share what the mice leave: about 45 gigabits a second each, instead of 60. The mice that hashed onto this uplink now wait behind a full queue and lose packets too, while the link to spine2 sits at 10 percent.

@5
How often does this happen? With two uplinks, two elephants share one half the time; with four spines, a quarter of the time; and with many elephants on few links, a collision is close to certain. The fixes change what gets balanced: split a transfer into several parallel flows, let a flow change uplink only at an idle gap between its bursts, which is flowlet switching, or route on measured load, which is adaptive routing.

@6
Now the second half: polarisation. Meridian's web servers behind leaf1, at 203.0.113.10 and 11, answer clients on the internet with plain routed IP. leaf1 has a default route over both spines, and each spine has a default route over both gateways. So every packet passes two ECMP decisions in a row, and the table on screen lists both.

@7
leaf1 hashes eight such flows, P1 to P8, and splits them four and four across the spines: a fair split. The detail that matters is in the setup. Every switch here runs the same chip hash with the same factory-default seed, 0.

@8
Each spine hashes the same five fields with the same function and the same seed, so it computes exactly the number leaf1 computed, and modulo 2 gives the same answer again. Every flow that reached spine1 had an even hash, so all of them go to gw1, and every flow on spine2 goes to gw2. The links from spine1 to gw2 and from spine2 to gw1 carry nothing: half the spine-to-gateway capacity sits idle, with every link up and every route correct. Per-link utilisation shows it as two links stuck at zero.

@9
The fix is to make the tiers disagree. Each device gets its own hash seed, here derived from its loopback, so a spine's hash of a flow is unrelated to the leaf's. Now all four spine-to-gateway links carry two flows each. leaf1's own choices were reshuffled as well, and they still split four and four.

@10
Two lessons, and they are different. Seeds cure polarisation, but not elephants: a new seed only reshuffles which flows collide, and two 60-gigabit elephants still share an uplink half the time. What a platform should show is the per-member utilisation of every ECMP group, the busiest member against the average, and the top flows on a hot link from sFlow samples, because an interface total alone cannot tell one elephant from a crowd of mice.
