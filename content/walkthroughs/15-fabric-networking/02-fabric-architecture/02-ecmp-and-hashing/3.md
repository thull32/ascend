@0
leaf1 in Meridian's Amsterdam fabric has four 400-gigabit uplinks, one per spine. It sends about 1,000 gigabits a second towards the other leaves, and ECMP gives each uplink a quarter of the flows, about 250. The idea to hold onto: routing removes a link only when it looks down, and a gray failure is a link that is sick but never looks down.

@1
Errors are counted where a frame is received, so the counters that matter for this direction are on spine3's port facing leaf1. At week 0 the light arriving is minus 1.8 d B m per lane, and the bit error rate before FEC is about one in a hundred million, normal for 100-gigabit-per-lane PAM4 signalling, which always needs some correction. Forward error correction, the code known as KP4, repairs those few errors, about 4,250 a second, so no frame fails its FCS check.

@2
Over two weeks the receive power falls to minus 4.6 d B m, as it does when a connector is contaminated or a fibre is bent too tightly in a tray, and the error rate before FEC rises three hundredfold, to three in a million. FEC still repairs every codeword: zero FCS errors, zero drops, and nothing at the IP layer has noticed.

@3
At week 4 the power is minus 5.9 d B m, and the error rate before FEC is nine in a hundred thousand, within a factor of three of the highest rate at which KP4 is specified to deliver a practically error-free link. 38 million bit errors are being repaired every second. This is the earliest strong warning, yet the link still looks perfectly healthy.

@4
Week 5, minus 6.8 d B m. The error rate is now one and a half in a thousand, six times the FEC limit, and FEC fails abruptly rather than gradually: about one codeword in a hundred has more errors than it can repair. spine3 drops the 2 percent of leaf1's frames that then fail their FCS check, the CRC-32 at the end of each frame: about 625,000 frames a second. And leaf1's own counters look perfect, because a sender never learns that the receiver threw its frames away.

@5
Nothing routes around it. The optic still delivers light, so the port stays up, and LLDP, IS-IS hellos and BFD packets get through at least 98 percent of the time, short packets more often still, since they have fewer bits to hit. BFD declares a link down only after three consecutive misses, which at 2 percent loss happens with a probability of about 8 in a million per window. So spine3 stays in the ECMP group and keeps receiving its 25 percent.

@6
Here is the shape users see. One in four of leaf1's flows crosses this link and loses 2 percent of its packets, and TCP throughput falls roughly with one over the square root of the loss rate, so those flows slow to a fraction of their normal speed while the other three quarters are untouched. Averaged over leaf1's uplinks the loss is only half a percent, small enough to disappear on a dashboard of totals. Yet one slow request in four is exactly what users report.

@7
The alerts that would have fired weeks earlier watch the error rate before FEC and the receive power against this link's own baseline: both have trended the wrong way since week 2. The module's own low-power warning, set by its vendor at minus 8.9 d B m, never fired at all. Now, FCS errors on spine3's receive side, plus probes spread over every uplink, of which only those through spine3 lose packets, pin the fault to this one link.

@8
The operator costs the link out, raising its IS-IS metric at both ends far above any real path. leaf1 drops spine3 from its ECMP group, and three uplinks carry the 1,000 gigabits a second at 83 percent utilisation: tight, but loss-free. Every other leaf also stops sending leaf1-bound traffic through spine3, because without this link spine3's path to leaf1 is a long detour.

@9
A technician cleans the connector, or swaps the optic. Receive power returns to minus 1.9 d B m and the error rate to its baseline, and after a soak period with zero FCS errors the link's metric is restored. leaf1's group is back to four members at 25 percent each, and 62.5 percent utilisation.

@10
To generalise: a gray failure is a component that is neither working nor down, and ECMP makes it worse by feeding the sick member its full share for as long as routing says it is up. Watch errors and light against their baseline, not only link state, and probe with many different 5-tuples so every member is exercised. Some operators automate the response, costing out any link whose error rate crosses a threshold, provided the remaining uplinks have the headroom to take its traffic.
