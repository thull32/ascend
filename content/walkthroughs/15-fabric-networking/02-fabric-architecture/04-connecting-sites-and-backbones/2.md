@0-2
The same square: Amsterdam and Singapore, two spines and two leaves each, two 100 gigabit circuits joining spine 1 to spine 1 and spine 2 to spine 2. Two leases instead of a mesh of four, and IS-IS sees two equal paths from leaf 1 in Amsterdam to leaf 2 in Singapore, each of metric 1,020. Only one thing changes in this run: the load. Watch for the moment the survivor is asked to carry what two circuits carried.

@3
Amsterdam now sends 140 gigabits per second to Singapore at the busy hour. ECMP splits it over both spines, so each circuit carries about 70 gigabits per second, 70 percent of its capacity. On a daily dashboard this looks healthy, with 30 percent free on every circuit.

@4
The latency is unchanged: about 10,500 kilometres of fibre, 52 and a half milliseconds one way and 105 round trip. Load does not move that floor while the queues stay short.

@5
The anchor cuts circuit 1 again. Both spines' ports facing it go down within milliseconds, the packets in flight are lost, and the 70 gigabits per second that rode circuit 1 has lost its path.

@6
Spine 1 in Amsterdam reruns its shortest path calculation first and finds the detour through a leaf to spine 2, metric 1,030. Leaves that have not caught up still send it half their Singapore flows, which take the two extra hops, and a packet can bounce in a microloop until the leaves converge.

@7
The leaves converge: 1,040 through spine 1 against 1,020 through spine 2, so every leaf sends all of its Singapore traffic to spine 2. Routing has done its job perfectly. The problem is arithmetic: circuit 2 is now offered 140 gigabits per second, on a 100 gigabit circuit.

@8
That is 140 percent of its capacity. The queue on spine 2 towards the circuit overflows, the excess is dropped, and TCP slows every flow between the sites, far more than the drop rate suggests, because each loss costs a long recovery at 105 milliseconds. The signals are circuit 1's ports down, circuit 2 pinned at 100 percent, and output drops on spine 2's port facing it.

@9
The planning rule this run broke: keep each circuit below 50 percent at the busiest hour, so either can carry both shares alone. At 70 percent per circuit, the square was already broken; it just had not failed yet. And the two circuits must not share a cable or landing station, or one anchor cuts both.

@10
When the cable is repaired, IS-IS restores both paths and the load splits 70 and 70 again, which only puts the square back into the same unsafe state. The general lesson: judge a redundant pair by what the survivor would carry, not by today's utilisation. A platform should show each site pair's load after its worst single failure, so the 70 percent that looks fine on every dashboard raises an alert, with time to order capacity before the cut that proves it.
