@0
Meridian's Amsterdam and Singapore sites, in the small example with two spines and two leaves each. Two leased long-haul circuits of 100 gigabits per second join them spine to spine: spine 1 in Amsterdam to spine 1 in Singapore, and spine 2 to spine 2. Spines inside a site are not wired to each other, so the other two sides of this square run from a spine down through any leaf and up to its sister spine. Hold onto one idea: the square survives a cut only because either circuit alone can carry everything.

@1
Why two circuits and not four? Between sites every link is a lease: the most expensive links Meridian has, each a separate contract, route and failure point. A full mesh would join every Amsterdam spine to both Singapore spines, four circuits. The square buys the same reachability with two by reusing the links inside each site, and pays by losing half its capacity when one fails. A mesh of four 50 gigabit circuits keeps 150 of its 200 on paper, but plain ECMP still sends half the traffic to the spine that lost a circuit, so it too fills at 100 gigabits per second.

@2
To IS-IS a circuit is a link with a high metric, 1,000, against 10 for a link inside a site. So leaf 1 in Amsterdam reaches leaf 2 in Singapore, loopback 10.255.2.12, over two equal paths: 10, plus 1,000, plus 10, which is 1,020, one through each Amsterdam spine and its circuit.

@3
At the busy hour Amsterdam sends 80 gigabits per second to Singapore. Every Amsterdam leaf hashes its flows across both spines, so each circuit carries about 40 gigabits per second, 40 percent of its capacity.

@4
The latency comes from distance, not hardware. About 10,500 kilometres of fibre at roughly 5 microseconds per kilometre is 52 and a half milliseconds one way and 105 milliseconds round trip. Each of the four switches on the path adds only microseconds. The two circuits follow different routes of different lengths, so latency can change after a failover.

@5
A ship's anchor cuts the subsea cable carrying circuit 1. The carrier's equipment at each end passes the failure on, by signalling a fault or switching its laser off, so both spines' ports facing the circuit go down within milliseconds. The 40 gigabits per second that rode circuit 1 has lost its path, and the packets in flight on it are gone. On the chart, circuit 1 drops to zero.

@6
Spine 1 in Amsterdam reruns its shortest path calculation first. Its best way to Singapore is now down through an Amsterdam leaf, up to spine 2, and over circuit 2: metric 1,030. Leaf 1 has not caught up and still sends half its Singapore flows to spine 1, so those packets take the detour, two extra hops. Until every leaf has rerun its own calculation, a packet can even bounce between spine 1 and a leaf that still points at it: a microloop.

@7
The leaves converge, well under a second after the cut. Through spine 1 now costs 1,040, detour included, against 1,020 through spine 2, so every Amsterdam leaf drops spine 1 from its Singapore route and sends everything to spine 2. Leaf to leaf, the path is no longer than before. Only traffic that starts at spine 1 itself, such as its own probes, still shows the extra hops.

@8
All 80 gigabits per second now rides circuit 2: 80 percent of its 100. Half the inter-site capacity is gone, and nothing is broken. The signals are both ports of circuit 1 down, and a step from 40 to 80 percent on circuit 2 and on the leaf links into spine 2.

@9
That is the square's planning rule: keep each circuit below 50 percent at the busiest hour, so either one can carry both shares when the other fails, and order capacity before growth crosses that line. Just as important, the two circuits must not share a cable, duct or landing station, a shared-risk link group, or one anchor cuts both and the square is gone.

@10
Weeks later the cable is repaired. Circuit 1 comes back, IS-IS restores the two equal paths at 1,020, and the load splits 40 and 40 again. The general rule: with k equal circuits, losing one leaves k minus 1 over k of the capacity, so each must run below that share at peak, 50 percent for two and 75 percent for four. A platform should model the site pair rather than two independent links, and alert whenever the survivor of a single failure could not carry the pair's peak, before a failure proves it.
