@0
On screen is Meridian's Amsterdam fabric in its small form: four spines along the top, four leaves below them, and Acme servers behind leaf1 and leaf3. Every leaf has exactly one link to every spine, sixteen links in all, and no link joins two leaves or two spines. Hold onto that one rule, because the whole design follows from it: every leaf connects to every spine, so every path between two leaves is one link up and one link down.

@1
leaf1 and leaf3 light up, with their links to all four spines. Any leaf reaches any other leaf in exactly two hops, up to a spine and down again. From leaf1 to leaf3 there are four such paths, one through each spine, all the same length, so every pair of leaves sees the same latency and the same number of paths.

@2
The underlay, IS-IS, gives every link a metric of 10, so each of the four paths costs 20: two links of 10. With four equal costs, leaf1 installs all four next hops for leaf3's subnet, 10.1.2.0 slash 24, at once, one per spine. That is equal-cost multipath, ECMP: the forwarding table holds a group of four next hops instead of one.

@3
Flow A arrives at leaf1: from 10.1.1.10, port 51517, to 10.1.2.20, port 5432, over TCP. The forwarding chip hashes those five fields, the 5-tuple, takes the result modulo 4 and gets 0, so flow A goes up to spine1. Every later packet of flow A hashes the same way and takes the same path, which keeps the flow in order.

@4
spine1 has a single route to 10.1.2.0 slash 24, via leaf3, so the second hop has no choice to make. Flow A is delivered in two hops.

@5
Flow B differs from flow A in its destination address and both ports, so its hash is unrelated to A's. Modulo 4 it gives 1, which picks spine2.

@6-7
Flow C hashes to 2 and goes up to spine3, and flow D hashes to 3 and goes up to spine4. Each is the first flow on its spine.

@8-9
Then the lumps. Flow E hashes to 1 and joins flow B on spine2, and flow F hashes to 2 and joins flow C on spine3. The hash spreads flows, not bytes, and it never looks at how busy a spine already is.

@10
All six flows together: one on spine1, two on spine2, two on spine3, one on spine4. Each flow stays pinned to one spine, so with a handful of flows the split is lumpy. With thousands of flows of similar size it evens out. That is the bet ECMP makes, and the lesson on ECMP and hashing shows when it loses.

@11
leaf1's four uplinks of 400 gigabits per second give it 1,600 gigabits per second towards the spines, and across many flows each spine carries about a quarter of its traffic. So the pattern: in a leaf-spine fabric the number of spines is the number of paths. Adding a spine adds a path and capacity without redesigning anything, and losing one costs a quarter of the capacity rather than connectivity. The signal to watch is the ECMP group width on every leaf, which should equal the spine count, and uplinks whose utilisation drifts apart from their siblings.
