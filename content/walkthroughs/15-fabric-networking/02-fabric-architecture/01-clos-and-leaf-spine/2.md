@0
The same small Amsterdam fabric, built with only two spines this time: four leaves, each with one link to each spine, eight links in all. The idea to hold is simple arithmetic: losing one spine costs every leaf the share of its uplink that went through that spine, and the survivors carry everything that is left. With two spines, that share is half.

@1-2
leaf1 reaches leaf3 in two hops, through either spine, so there are two equal paths. Each costs 20, two links of IS-IS metric 10, and leaf1 installs both next hops for 10.1.2.0 slash 24 as an ECMP group of two.

@3-4
Flow A, from 10.1.1.10 to 10.1.2.20 over TCP, is hashed on its 5-tuple and taken modulo 2. The result is 0, so it goes up to spine1, whose single route sends it down to leaf3. Two hops, and every packet of flow A follows it.

@5-9
The other five flows follow the same rule. Flow B goes to spine2. Flow C lands on spine1 beside A. Flows D and E both go to spine2, joining B. And flow F lands on spine1 with A and C.

@10
Three flows on each spine. With only two choices the split happens to be even this time, but each flow is still pinned to one spine.

@11
Before any failure, leaf1 sends 500 gigabits per second towards the spines over 800 gigabits per second of uplink: 62.5 percent utilisation, spread over both spines.

@12
spine2 loses power, and every leaf's link to it goes dark at once. Each leaf sees loss of signal on its own port within milliseconds, and can take that next hop out of its ECMP group locally, before any routing protocol message arrives. A spine that stays powered but stops forwarding gives no such signal; that one needs BFD, from the lesson on convergence and BFD.

@13
The ECMP group for 10.1.2.0 slash 24 shrinks from two next hops to one. Every leaf still reaches every other leaf in two hops, but only through spine1, so there is no redundancy left until spine2 returns.

@14
With one next hop left, every flow now hashes to spine1. Flows B, D and E had to move, because they were on spine2. Packets that were in flight on spine2, or sent to it before the leaf noticed, are lost, and TCP retransmits them. No connection breaks.

@15
Now the capacity. leaf1 has one uplink of 400 gigabits per second left: losing one spine cost half the capacity, not the fabric. But the same 500 gigabits per second of traffic now runs at 125 percent utilisation, more than the uplinks can carry, so queues fill and packets are dropped. The lesson on redundancy and failure domains sizes headroom so that a fabric survives losing a spine.

@16
When spine2 is repaired, its links come back, each leaf adds it to the ECMP group again, and flows rehash modulo 2. For planned work, the safe way to take a spine out is to drain it first, so traffic moves off gradually, as the lesson on safe change and maintenance shows. The general rule: with N spines, losing one multiplies uplink utilisation by N over N minus 1. With two spines that doubles it, which is why two big spines are a worse design than four or eight smaller ones.
