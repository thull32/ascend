@0
Meridian's small Amsterdam fabric again, this time with three spines, a size small sites often start at, and four leaves. Every leaf has one link to every spine, twelve links in all, and there are no leaf-to-leaf or spine-to-spine links. leaf 1 has three uplinks of 400 gigabits per second, 1,200 in all. The one idea to hold: losing one of n spines costs each leaf one nth of its uplink, and the same load lands on what is left. With three spines, that is a third.

@1-2
leaf 1 reaches leaf 3 in exactly two hops, up to any spine and down again, so there are three equal paths. Each costs 20, two links of IS-IS metric 10, and leaf 1 installs all three next hops for 10.1.2.0 slash 24 as one ECMP group of three.

@3-4
Flow A, from 10.1.1.10 to 10.1.2.20 on port 5432 over TCP, arrives at leaf 1. The forwarding chip hashes its 5-tuple, takes the result modulo 3 and gets 0, so it sends the flow up to spine 1, which has a single route down to leaf 3. Two hops, and every packet of flow A follows the same path.

@5-9
The other five flows follow the same rule. Flow B hashes to 2 and goes to spine 3. Flow C hashes to 1 and goes to spine 2. Then flow D joins B on spine 3, flow E joins C on spine 2, and flow F joins A on spine 1. The hash spreads flows, not bytes, and never looks at how busy a spine already is.

@10
Six flows, two on each spine. With three spines this run happens to split evenly, but each flow is still pinned to its spine.

@11
Before any failure, leaf 1 sends 750 gigabits per second towards the spines over 1,200 of uplink: 62.5 percent utilisation, the same starting point as the two-spine and four-spine runs in the lesson on Clos and leaf-spine.

@12
spine 2 loses power, and every leaf's link to it goes dark at once. Each leaf sees loss of signal on its own port within milliseconds and removes that next hop from its ECMP group locally, before any routing message arrives. A spine that stays powered but stops forwarding gives no such signal; catching that one needs BFD.

@13
The ECMP group for 10.1.2.0 slash 24 shrinks from three next hops to two. Every leaf still reaches every other leaf in two hops, through spine 1 and spine 3.

@14
Flows are rehashed modulo 2 over the two survivors. Flows C and E had to move, because they were on spine 2: C goes to spine 1 and E to spine 3, while A, B, D and F keep their spines. Packets in flight on spine 2, or sent to it before the leaf noticed, are lost, and TCP retransmits them. No connection breaks, and three flows now sit on each survivor.

@15
Now the number this lesson is about. leaf 1 has two uplinks of 400 left, 800 gigabits per second: losing one spine cost a third of the capacity, not the fabric. On the chart the capacity line steps down from 1,200 to 800 while the offered load stays at 750. That is 93.8 percent utilisation. Nothing is overloaded, and the fabric is still in trouble: at that level every burst queues, and the next failure overloads it.

@16
When spine 2 is repaired, its links come back, every leaf adds it to the ECMP group again, and flows rehash modulo 3; planned work drains a spine first instead of letting it fail like this. Now turn the rule around. After losing k of n members, the load is the utilisation times n over n minus k. So to stay under Meridian's 80 percent target after losing one of three spines, the busiest hour may run at no more than about 53 percent. With two spines that ceiling is 40 percent, with four it is 60, and with sixteen, 75. That is N plus 1: one spare member, sized so the target still holds when any one fails. And because maintenance is a failure you schedule, one spine drained while another fails is two losses, which with three spines means a ceiling of 27 percent. The signal to surface, for every leaf and every pair, is today's peak against the capacity left after its worst single loss.
