@0
Meridian's small Amsterdam fabric: four spines and four leaves on links of 400 gigabits per second, with spine 1 and spine 2 also carrying the two 100 gigabit circuits to Singapore. Tonight's change upgrades spine 2's operating system, which means a reboot, and the goal is to do it without dropping a single packet. The idea that makes it work: a drain is a spine failure you schedule, with the traffic moved out of the way first. So the first step is not a command but arithmetic.

@1
The pre-check asks what the fabric would look like with spine 2 gone. Each leaf's traffic would spread over three uplinks instead of four, so each would carry four thirds of its current load. At two in the afternoon, leaf 3 carries 270 gigabits per second per uplink, which would become 360, 90 percent of the link. And the 90 gigabits per second to Singapore, now 45 on each circuit, would all land on spine 1's circuit: 90 percent again. Both are above Meridian's 80 percent limit, so the tool refuses to start.

@2
In the window at two in the morning, the same check passes. leaf 3 goes from 165 to 220 gigabits per second per uplink, 55 percent, and the circuits from 35 each to 70 on one, 70 percent. The state checks pass too: all four spines in service, because draining one while another is already out would halve the fabric, all 18 IS-IS adjacencies up, four equal-cost paths on every leaf, and clean probes.

@3
The drain itself. spine 2 raises the IS-IS metric on all five of its links, from 10 on the four leaf links and 52,500 on the Singapore circuit, to 16,777,214: the largest value that still leaves a link usable as a last resort. It floods a new link-state packet saying so, and every leaf reruns its shortest-path calculation. Through spine 2, the path to leaf 1's loopback now costs 16,777,224, against 20 through any other spine, so spine 2 drops out of every ECMP group. Setting the IS-IS overload bit is the other common way. The difference: overload forbids transit through spine 2 even when it is the only path left, while the maximum metric keeps it as a last resort.

@4
Traffic moves off as each leaf updates its forwarding table. On the chart, leaf 3's uplinks go from four at 165 to three at 220 gigabits per second, and the Singapore traffic shifts onto spine 1's circuit, 70 of its 100. No packet needs to be lost, because spine 2 keeps forwarding whatever still reaches it until every leaf has moved. A flow that changes spine may see a few packets reordered, once.

@5
Before touching spine 2, verify that it is empty. Its five ports carry 10 to 20 kilobits per second each: only the IS-IS hellos, BFD and LLDP that keep its adjacencies up. The busiest remaining uplink is at 55 percent, the Singapore circuit is at the predicted 70, and no fabric port shows a discard. The drain did what the pre-check said it would.

@6
Now the work: spine 2 reboots into the new release. Its links go dark for several minutes, and its five adjacencies drop. The change-aware monitoring expects that for this change and pages nobody. Packets lost: zero. Probes failed: zero. Every leaf still has three equal-cost paths, as it has had since the drain. It is an ordinary spine failure, with the traffic moved out of the way first.

@7
spine 2 comes back on the new release, and because the high metric is part of its saved configuration, it returns still costed out: its adjacencies form, but it attracts no traffic. That is the moment to check it, while a mistake costs nothing: five adjacencies up, the same 52 routes as spine 1, every optic lane in range and zero interface errors. A spine that came back at normal metrics would attract traffic the moment its adjacencies formed, before its forwarding table held every route, and black-hole a share of flows.

@8
Restore: spine 2 sets its metrics back to 10 on the leaf links and 52,500 on the circuit, floods a new link-state packet, and every leaf puts it back into its ECMP groups. leaf 3's uplinks return to four at 165 gigabits per second, and each Singapore circuit to 35.

@9
The final check compares against the pre-check snapshot: four spines carrying equal shares, 18 adjacencies, zero discards, clean probes, and zero packets lost during the change. The tool attaches both snapshots and every step's timestamp to the change record, so the audit trail shows the change was hitless and the next engineer can repeat it. It only works because the fabric was designed for it: headroom sized for the drain and not only the failure, devices that come up costed out, and never two members of the same pair out at once. The signal that a drain worked is the drained device's ports falling to the control-plane trickle, and the ECMP width on every leaf matching the plan.
