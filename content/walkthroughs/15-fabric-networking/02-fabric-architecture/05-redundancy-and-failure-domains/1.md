@0
On screen, acme-web2, an Acme server, is bonded to leaf 1 and leaf 2 in Singapore with one LACP bond. Both leaves configure the same ethernet segment identifier, the ESI, on their bond port, and send the same LACP system ID, so the server sees a single partner. Far away, leaf 1 in Amsterdam, with acme-web1 behind it, is a remote VTEP in VNI 10100. The dashed lines are BGP sessions through the route reflector. Hold onto one idea: the two Singapore leaves have no peer link between them. Everything they agree on travels as ordinary BGP routes.

@1
First the leaves find each other. Each advertises a type-4 route, the ethernet segment route, for the ESI. Its import route target is derived from the ESI itself, so only leaves attached to that same segment import it. The two Singapore leaves discover each other, and leaf 1 in Amsterdam ignores both routes.

@2
Then the election. After a 3-second wait for every peer's type-4 route, both leaves run the same calculation: list the segment's leaves by address, 10.255.2.11 then 10.255.2.12, and take the service's VLAN, 100, or its VNI, 10100, both even, modulo 2, the number of leaves. The answer is 0, the first in the list, so leaf 1 in Singapore becomes the designated forwarder: the only leaf allowed to send broadcast, unknown unicast and multicast traffic from the fabric down to the server.

@3
Each leaf also advertises two type-1 routes, for Ethernet auto-discovery: one per segment, flagged all-active, for mass withdrawal, and one carrying VNI 10100, for aliasing. Both reach every leaf in VNI 10100, Amsterdam included.

@4
Now traffic. acme-web2 sends its first frame, and the server's own LACP hash happens to put it on the link to leaf 2. Leaf 2 learns the server's MAC address and advertises a type-2 route whose ESI field is the segment's identifier, not zero. Leaf 1 in Singapore has not seen a single frame from that MAC.

@5
It does not need to. The type-2 route reaches leaf 1 in Singapore too; it finds its own ESI in it and installs the MAC on its local bond port.

@6
Leaf 1 in Amsterdam received the MAC from leaf 2 only. But the per-instance type-1 routes say that both Singapore leaves reach that segment in VNI 10100. So it combines the three routes and installs the MAC behind both VTEPs, 10.255.2.11 and 10.255.2.12. That is aliasing.

@7
acme-web1's flows to acme-web2 are now hashed over both VTEPs, flow 1 to leaf 1 and flow 2 to leaf 2, so both bond links carry traffic. Without aliasing every flow would go to leaf 2, the leaf that happened to learn the MAC, and the link to leaf 1 would sit idle.

@8
Flooded traffic has the opposite problem: it must arrive once, not twice. A broadcast from acme-web1 is copied to every VTEP in VNI 10100 by ingress replication, so both Singapore leaves receive it. Only leaf 1, the designated forwarder, sends it down the bond, and leaf 2 drops its copy for that segment. acme-web2 receives the broadcast exactly once.

@9
In the other direction, a broadcast from acme-web2 lands on leaf 2, which floods it to every VTEP, leaf 1 in Singapore included. Leaf 1 sees the outer source address, 10.255.2.12, a leaf on the same segment, so it never sends that copy back down to the server, even though it is the designated forwarder. This split-horizon rule for VXLAN is called local bias.

@10
Now the failure. The link between acme-web2 and leaf 2 fails. The server's LACP drops that member and sends everything to leaf 1, and leaf 2 has lost its only port on the segment. One of two bond members is up.

@11
Leaf 2 withdraws its per-segment type-1 route, a single message, and every remote leaf removes 10.255.2.12 from the next hops of every MAC on that segment at once. If 500 MACs sat behind the bond, say a hypervisor's virtual machines, this one withdrawal would do the work of 500 type-2 withdrawals arriving one MAC at a time. That is mass withdrawal. Leaf 2 also withdraws its type-4 route, which leaves leaf 1 as the segment's only member and its designated forwarder for every VLAN.

@12
Both flows now hash to 10.255.2.11 and reach acme-web2 through leaf 1, after one BGP withdrawal instead of one per MAC. Leaf 2 also withdraws its type-2 route for the MAC, and leaf 1, which now receives all of the server's frames, advertises the MAC itself.

@13
EVPN multihoming does with standard BGP routes what MLAG does with a vendor protocol over a peer link: no peer link to size or to split, two or more leaves per segment, and remote leaves that see two VTEPs rather than one shared address. And because the failure is announced in BGP, it is visible fabric-wide, where with MLAG it stays hidden inside the pair. The signals worth surfacing are the designated forwarder per segment, both members' type-1 routes at a remote leaf, and a bond with one member down while its leaf is still up: a pair quietly running as one.
