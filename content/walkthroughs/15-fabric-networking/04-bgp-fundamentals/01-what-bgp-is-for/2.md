@0
A small Amsterdam fabric, two spines and four leaves, built the R F C 7938 way, with BGP as the only routing protocol and no IS-IS. Both spines share AS 65100, and each leaf has its own private AS, 65101 to 65104. Every leaf-to-spine link carries one external BGP session between its two slash 31 addresses, eight sessions in all. The idea to hold: this is the same loop check gateway 2 applied at the internet edge, and giving both spines one AS number turns it into a rule that no path may ever run through two spines.

@1
Leaf 1 announces its loopback, 10.255.1.11 slash 32, to spine 1, in an UPDATE over their TCP session on port 179. The AS path holds only its own AS, 65101, and the next hop is its own address on that link, 10.254.1.1. The packets carry a TTL of 1, because these peers are directly connected and nothing should ever forward them.

@2
The same UPDATE goes to spine 2, with next hop 10.254.2.1. Each spine looks for its own AS, 65100, in the path, does not find it, and installs the route: one path, straight to leaf 1.

@3
Spine 1 passes the route on to leaves 2, 3 and 4. It prepends its own AS, so the path reads 65100, 65101, and sets the next hop to its own address on each link. The copy to leaf 3 says next hop 10.254.1.4.

@4
Spine 2 does the same, so leaf 3 receives a second copy with the same AS path, 65100, 65101, and next hop 10.254.2.4.

@5
Leaf 3 now holds two paths with identical AS paths, so neither wins on length, and it installs both: an ECMP route to leaf 1's loopback through both spines, the same forwarding result IS-IS would compute. This is the first reason the spines share one AS. With different spine numbers the two paths would differ, and most implementations would use only one of them.

@6
Leaf 3 advertises its best path on to its other peer, spine 2, prepending its own AS: the path becomes 65103, 65100, 65101, with next hop 10.254.2.5. To spine 2, this is a way to leaf 1 that goes down to leaf 3, up to spine 1 and down again.

@7
Spine 2 finds its own AS, 65100, in that path and discards it. Spine 2 never saw this route before, but spine 1 shares its number, so the check works across the whole spine layer. No spine ever accepts a path through another spine, and traffic can never take a spine, leaf, spine detour.

@8
Now the link between leaf 1 and spine 2 fails, and the session over it drops as soon as loss of light or B F D reports it. Spine 2 has lost its only usable path, because the looped ones were never accepted, so it sends a withdrawal of leaf 1's loopback to leaves 2, 3 and 4.

@9
Leaf 3 drops the path through spine 2 and keeps the one through spine 1, so traffic to leaf 1's loopback now leaves only through spine 1. IS-IS would reach the same result by flooding and a shortest-path run; BGP reaches it by an explicit withdrawal between neighbours. And spine 2 itself can no longer reach leaf 1 at all, where an IS-IS spine would detour through a leaf.

@10
That is the appeal of a BGP underlay: one protocol and one set of tools everywhere, policy at every hop, and failures handled by withdrawals between neighbours instead of flooding to every router. The price is a session on every link and an AS plan to manage. And the pattern to carry away is that one rule, reject your own AS number, does different work depending on how you assign the numbers: at the internet edge it stops announcements circling the world, and inside a fabric it stops routes turning back through a second spine.
