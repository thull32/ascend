@0
The same three routers as in the broken design: R1 is leaf1, R2 is spine1, R3 is leaf3, IS-IS on all three and BGP only on the two leaves. This time the design is Meridian's real one: the leaves are VXLAN tunnel endpoints, and Acme's subnets live in VRF ACME, a separate routing table, carried by BGP EVPN. The one idea: change the question the spine is asked. Instead of "where is the customer's address?", ask it "where is leaf3's loopback?", which IS-IS already answers.

@1
IS-IS is up on both links, and spine1's table holds the same four routes as before: the two leaf loopbacks, 10.255.1.11 and 10.255.1.13, and the two slash 31 link subnets. Still no Acme subnet.

@2
leaf1 opens the BGP session exactly as before: a TCP SYN from its loopback to leaf3's loopback, port 179, forwarded by spine1 on its IS-IS route.

@3
The session reaches Established in AS 65000, and this time the OPEN messages also negotiated the EVPN address family besides plain IPv4. The dashed line is still a TCP connection whose packets travel through spine1.

@4
leaf3 advertises 10.1.2.0 slash 24 as an EVPN type 5 route, an IP prefix route. It carries a route distinguisher, 10.255.1.13 colon 50001; a route target, 65000 colon 50001, which means import into VRF ACME; the next hop, leaf3's loopback; and VNI 50001, Acme's routing VNI. The UPDATE crosses spine1 as plain TCP that the spine never reads, just as before.

@5
leaf1 imports the route into VRF ACME, because the route target matches. The route now says: send to the tunnel endpoint 10.255.1.13, with VNI 50001. And leaf1's underlay route to 10.255.1.13 goes through spine1. This time the packet will be addressed to a place the spine knows.

@6
acme-web1 sends the same SYN to 10.1.2.20, to its gateway with TTL 64. Nothing on the host changes; the fix is entirely inside the fabric.

@7
leaf1 routes the packet in VRF ACME, inner TTL down to 63, and wraps it: a VXLAN header with VNI 50001, UDP to port 4789, and an outer IP header from leaf1's loopback to leaf3's, 10.255.1.13. That wrapper is 50 bytes, which is why the underlay MTU must exceed the tenant's by at least 50.

@8
The tunnelled packet goes up to spine1. To the spine it is just a UDP packet between two loopbacks. 10.1.2.20 is buried in the payload.

@9
spine1 looks up the outer destination, 10.255.1.13, matches its IS-IS route via 10.254.1.5, decrements the outer TTL to 63, and forwards. It has exactly the same four routes as in the broken design, still no route for 10.1.2.0 slash 24, and it no longer needs one.

@10-11
The packet reaches leaf3, the owner of the outer address. leaf3 strips the outer headers, maps VNI 50001 to VRF ACME, and looks up 10.1.2.20 there: connected, on VLAN 200. It decrements the inner TTL to 62 and writes a new Ethernet header, from the subnet's gateway MAC to acme-db1's MAC, ending in 53 14. The VNI is what picks the table, so Globex, with its identical 10.1.1.0 slash 24, travels in VNI 50002 to a different table, a separation the spines never see.

@12
acme-db1 receives the SYN and answers. Same three routers, same path as the broken design. The only change is what spine1 was asked to look up.

@13
Encapsulation turned "find 10.1.2.20" into "find 10.255.1.13", a question the IGP answers on every router. MPLS does the same with a label bound to leaf3's loopback. Either way the transit routers stay free of customer routes, which is why Meridian's spines run IS-IS and nothing else. The trade is that the black holes move into the underlay: a tunnel endpoint's loopback missing from IS-IS, or an MTU without room for the extra 50 bytes, where small packets pass and full-size ones vanish. So the signals to watch are every endpoint's loopback present on every router, and large-packet probes across the underlay.
