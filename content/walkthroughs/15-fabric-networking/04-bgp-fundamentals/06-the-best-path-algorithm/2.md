@0
The same three paths on gw1 in Amsterdam, to 192.0.2.0 slash 24: A from its own eBGP session to ISP1, B over iBGP from gw2 through ISP2 in Amsterdam, and C over iBGP from the Singapore gateway through ISP2 there. Left alone, B wins at step 9 on IGP cost. This time, hold onto one idea: local preference is the operator's deliberate choice of exit, it sits third on the ladder, and whatever wins there never meets the steps below it.

@1
Meridian changes one thing. The import policy on gw1's session to ISP1, say because ISP1 is the cheaper contract, now gives ISP1's routes a local preference of 200 instead of 100. The routes themselves have not changed. gw1 re-applies the policy to its stored copy of what ISP1 sent, or asks ISP1 to resend it with a route refresh, and A's local preference in the table becomes 200. B and C stay at 100.

@2-3
Step 1 passes all three, since every next hop still resolves and no AS path holds Meridian's own 64500. Step 2, weight, is 0 on all three, because Meridian sets none, so it is a tie.

@4
Step 3, local preference: 200 against 100 and 100. A wins outright, and B and C are out, although A's AS path is one network longer. The AS path step that would have removed A is never reached.

@5
So steps 4 to 13 grey out as not reached. Locally originated, AS path length, origin, MED, eBGP over iBGP, IGP cost, multipath and the tie-breakers are never consulted for this prefix on this router.

@6
gw1 installs A, and its forwarding table sends 192.0.2.0 slash 24 straight out of the port to ISP1, next hop 198.51.100.2. Because its best path is now one it learned over eBGP, gw1 advertises A to gw2 and to the Singapore gateway over iBGP, and iBGP carries the local preference of 200 with it.

@7
Now the ripple. gw2 and the Singapore gateway each compare A, at 200, with their own ISP2 path, at 100, and pick A at step 3. Their best path is now learned over iBGP, so they may no longer advertise their own, and they withdraw B and C. Both now exit through gw1 to ISP1. One policy change on one router moved the whole AS's exit.

That is the strength and the cost of local preference. It is the strongest standard lever for outbound traffic, and it works AS-wide because iBGP carries it, where weight would have stayed on one router. But look at gw1's table: it holds a single path. If ISP1 fails, the other gateways must re-select and re-advertise their ISP2 paths before gw1 has an alternative: a round of updates, not a pre-installed backup. And a session that misses its policy falls back to 100 and quietly moves egress, so the signal to watch is each provider's share of outbound traffic against intent.
