@0
Leaf 1 in Amsterdam, loopback 10.255.1.11, and its route reflector, reflector 1, loopback 10.255.1.101, both in AS 65000: an internal BGP session between loopbacks, carried over TCP on routes IS-IS provides. Reflector 1 has just rebooted, and it never opens connections itself. Hold onto one idea. Each state is a checkpoint, and nothing moves on until the layer below is proven: TCP, then the two OPENs, then a keepalive accepting them, and only then routes. After that, the session lives only as long as BGP messages keep arriving, not TCP acknowledgements.

@1
An operator enables the neighbour. Leaf 1 leaves Idle for Connect, starts its connect retry timer, 10 seconds on Meridian, and sends a TCP SYN to the reflector's port 179.

@2
Nothing on reflector 1 is listening on port 179 yet, so its kernel answers with a TCP reset. Leaf 1 moves to Active, which, despite the name, means there is no TCP connection. It listens on 179 and waits for the retry timer.

@3
Ten seconds later the timer fires. Leaf 1 goes back to Connect and sends a fresh SYN, from port 40123. The reflector's BGP process is up now, waiting in Active with a socket listening on 179.

@4
The reflector answers with a SYN-ACK. Both sides advertise a maximum segment size of 9,176 bytes: the fabric's 9,216-byte MTU minus 20 bytes of IPv4 header and 20 of TCP.

@5
The ACK completes the handshake, and leaf 1 immediately writes its 57-byte OPEN into the connection: version 4, AS 65000, hold time 9, BGP identifier 10.255.1.11, and capabilities for IPv4 unicast, E V P N, route refresh and four-byte AS numbers. Leaf 1 is now in Open Sent, its hold timer at a large 240 seconds until the peer's OPEN arrives.

@6
The reflector moved from Active to Open Sent when the connection arrived, and sends its own OPEN: hold time 90, identifier 10.255.1.101, and one more capability, IPv6 unicast, which makes it 65 bytes. The two OPENs cross in flight.

@7
Leaf 1 checks the reflector's OPEN: version 4, the expected AS, an identifier different from its own. Both sides use the smaller hold time proposed, so the hold time is 9 seconds, with a keepalive every third of that, 3 seconds. The session keeps only the families both offered: IPv4 unicast and E V P N, not IPv6. Leaf 1 sends a 19-byte keepalive and enters Open Confirm.

@8
The reflector runs the same checks on leaf 1's OPEN, reaches the same 9 seconds, sends its own keepalive and enters Open Confirm. A keepalive at this point means "I accept your OPEN". A peer that disliked it would send a NOTIFICATION instead.

@9
The reflector's keepalive reaches leaf 1 at 10.0015 seconds, and it moves to Established. The second attempt went from SYN to Established in 1.1 milliseconds; the 10 seconds before it were spent waiting out the retry timer.

@10
Only now may routes flow. The reflector starts the table transfer with a 4,049-byte UPDATE of E V P N routes, close to BGP's 4,096-byte message limit. Every UPDATE received restarts leaf 1's hold timer, just as a keepalive does.

@11
Each family's transfer ends with End of RIB, an UPDATE that carries no routes: 29 bytes for E V P N, as shown, and a bare 23 bytes for IPv4 unicast. It is how a tool tells a session still loading from one that is complete.

@12
From here a quiet session is just keepalives, every 3 seconds each way. Each one resets leaf 1's 9-second hold timer, so on the chart it falls for 3 seconds and jumps back to 9, never near zero while the reflector is healthy.

@13
At 598.0015 seconds the reflector's last keepalive arrives. Just after it, the reflector's BGP process hangs. Its kernel is fine, so TCP stays open and still acknowledges every byte leaf 1 sends.

@14
Leaf 1 keeps sending keepalives, at 601 and 604 seconds, and gets TCP acknowledgements back, but no BGP message, so nothing resets its hold timer: 6 seconds left, then 3. By TCP's standards this connection is healthy.

@15
At 607.0015 seconds, 9 seconds after the last message, the hold timer reaches zero. Leaf 1 sends a 21-byte NOTIFICATION with error code 4, hold timer expired, subcode 0, closes the TCP connection, drops to Idle and removes every route it learned from reflector 1.

@16
Leaf 1 loses no reachability, because it holds the same routes from reflector 2: that is why reflectors come in pairs. A monitoring tool should record the last NOTIFICATION, which side sent it and when, not just the current state. The general lesson: a fault pins the session in the state just below the broken layer. Stuck in Connect or Active, TCP will not come up. Stuck in Open Sent, the peer never sends an OPEN. Looping back to Idle, an OPEN was refused. And the hold timer is a trade: 9 seconds catches a hung peer fast, but can flap a session on a brief outage, which is why sub-second detection goes to B F D.
