@0
On screen are Amsterdam's two border gateways, gw1 with loopback 10.255.1.201 and gw2 with 10.255.1.202, both in AS 64500, running iBGP between their loopbacks. Between them sit spine1 and spine2, and IS-IS carries the loopbacks and the fabric's links. It does not carry the links to the ISPs, so gw1's routing table has nothing for gw2's link to ISP2. Hold onto one sentence, because it is the whole mechanism: BGP says which exit, and the IGP says how to get there. A BGP path is only usable if its next hop can be found in the routing table.

@1
ISP2 announces 192.0.2.0 slash 24 to gw2, with next hop 198.51.100.0, ISP2's own address on their shared link. For gw2 that is trivial to resolve: the address is on a directly connected link.

@2
gw2 passes the route to gw1 over iBGP. By default iBGP leaves the next hop unchanged, so gw1 is told to send traffic for 192.0.2.0 slash 24 to 198.51.100.0, an address on a link gw1 is not attached to.

@3
gw1 looks up 198.51.100.0 in its routing table. It holds gw2's loopback through both spines, spine1's loopback, the two links to the spines and its own link to ISP1, and nothing that matches. So the path is kept in the BGP table, marked unusable, and excluded from best-path selection. gw1 stays on ISP1's path, one AS longer. The session is Established and the prefix was received; only the next-hop status shows why it is not used.

@4
The usual fix is next-hop-self. gw2 rewrites the next hop to its own loopback, 10.255.1.202, on routes it sends to iBGP peers, and sends the route again. The alternative, carrying the ISP links in IS-IS, would put another company's addresses into the IGP.

@5
Now the lookup succeeds. 10.255.1.202 matches the IS-IS route 10.255.1.202 slash 32, metric 20, through spine1 and spine2. The path is usable, and with an AS path of two ASes, 64502 then 64511, against ISP1's three, it becomes gw1's best path. ISP1's path drops to valid, kept as the fallback.

@6
Recursion ends in the forwarding table. The BGP route points at 10.255.1.202, which IS-IS reaches through two equal-cost spines, so the forwarding chip gets 192.0.2.0 slash 24 with two physical next hops, 10.254.1.200 and 10.254.2.200, and hashes flows over both. BGP chose the exit, gw2. IS-IS chose the way to it, either spine. Neither knows the other's half.

@7
A packet for 192.0.2.10 heads to spine1. Resolution only picked this first hop. spine1 must also forward the packet on towards gw2, which works only if spine1 holds that route itself or the packet is tunnelled to gw2's loopback. The BGP black hole lesson shows what happens when neither is true.

@8
Now the link between gw2 and spine1 fails. IS-IS recomputes, and gw2's loopback is now reached through spine2 alone, metric still 20, so gw1's forwarding entry for 192.0.2.0 slash 24 drops to one next hop, 10.254.2.200. BGP sent nothing and changed nothing. The exit is the same; only the way there moved.

@9
Then gw2 loses power. Within about a second, IS-IS removes 10.255.1.202 slash 32 from gw1's routing table, and the lookup finds no match. Next-hop tracking, which watches the routing table for exactly this, marks every BGP path with that next hop unusable within moments, and 192.0.2.0 slash 24 falls back to ISP1. Look at the session to gw2: it still shows Established.

@10
Only when the hold timer expires, up to 9 seconds later with the fabric's 9 second hold time, does the session go to Idle and gw2's paths disappear from gw1's tables. Traffic had moved long before. Next-hop tracking, not the BGP session, decided how quickly gw1 stopped sending towards a dead exit.

@11
Generalise it. Every BGP route stands on an IGP route under its next hop, so IGP changes reach every BGP route without a single BGP message, and a next hop the IGP does not carry makes a received route silently useless. For tooling, show next-hop resolvability per route, not just session state. An Established session whose prefixes are all unusable is the classic missing next-hop-self, and a next hop that resolves through an unexpected path is how traffic quietly takes a detour.
