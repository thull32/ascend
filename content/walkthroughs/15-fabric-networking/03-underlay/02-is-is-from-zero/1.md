@0
On screen is Meridian's Amsterdam underlay, in a small example with two spines and four leaves: one IS-IS level-2 area, 49.0001, with an adjacency on every leaf-spine link, eight in all. Every router holds the same database of six LSPs, one per router, each describing that router's neighbours and prefixes. Hold onto one rule, because it is the whole of flooding: a router passes an LSP on only when it is newer than the copy it already holds, and the sequence number decides which is newer. Below the picture, leaf1's route to leaf3's loopback, 10.255.1.13, has two next hops at metric 20, one through each spine.

@1
This is leaf3's LSP. Its ID starts with leaf3's system ID, each octet of its loopback padded to three digits and the twelve digits regrouped in fours: 0 1 0 2, 5 5 0 0, 1 0 1 3, followed by a pseudonode number and a fragment number, both zero. Its sequence number is 2 A in hex, and it has 1,043 seconds of lifetime left. Inside, its TLVs list two neighbours, spine1 and spine2 at metric 10 each, and three prefixes: the loopback and the two point-to-point links.

@2
Now the fibre between leaf3 and spine2 is cut, both ends lose the adjacency, and the count drops to seven. leaf3 builds a new LSP: sequence 2 B, a fresh lifetime of 1,200 seconds, and only spine1 as a neighbour. spine2 does the same with its own LSP, from 51 to 52 in hex, dropping leaf3.

@3
leaf3 floods the new LSP to its only remaining neighbour, spine1. IS-IS packets ride directly inside Ethernet frames, not inside IP, so flooding works even when no IP route exists yet.

@4
spine1 compares sequence numbers. 2 B is higher than the 2 A it holds, so the copy is newer: spine1 replaces its entry and acknowledges with a PSNP, a partial sequence number PDU, naming the LSP and 2 B. Without that acknowledgement, leaf3 would resend the LSP every 5 seconds, the usual retransmit interval on point-to-point links.

@5
spine1 floods the LSP on every other adjacency, to leaf1, leaf2 and leaf4, but not back to leaf3, which sent it. Each leaf finds 2 B newer than its 2 A, installs it, and acknowledges with its own PSNP.

@6
Each of those leaves in turn floods it to its other neighbour, spine2, so three copies of the same LSP head for spine2 within about a millisecond of each other.

@7
spine2 installs the first copy and acknowledges all three, but the second and third carry a sequence number it already holds, so they go no further. That is the rule from the start, and it is how flooding ends with no loops and no hop count.

@8
Every router's copy of leaf3's LSP is now 2 B, and spine2's new LSP, 52, has flooded the same way from the other side: six identical databases again. The whole exchange takes tens of milliseconds, and most of that is the deliberate wait, often 50 milliseconds, that a router allows itself before generating an LSP, in case more changes follow.

@9
Now every router runs SPF, Dijkstra's shortest-path algorithm, over the same database. A link counts only if the LSPs at both ends list each other, and neither leaf3 nor spine2 lists the other any more. leaf1's route to 10.255.1.13 keeps metric 20, but now has a single next hop, spine1, at 10.254.1.0.

@10
From the same database, spine2 still reaches leaf3, at metric 30: down to any of the other three leaves, up to spine1, and down again, so it has three equal-cost next hops. To IS-IS a leaf is just another router that can carry transit; the eBGP underlay of the next lesson forbids exactly this kind of path.

@11
The fibre is repaired. leaf3 and spine2 bring the adjacency up with a three-way hello handshake, Down, Initializing, Up, and the count is back to eight. Each then sends a CSNP, a complete sequence number PDU, listing every LSP it holds with its sequence number. Flooding through spine1 kept both databases current, so the lists match and nothing needs to be sent.

@12
Both routers then originate LSPs that include the restored adjacency, leaf3 at 2 C and spine2 at 53, which flood exactly as before. SPF everywhere gives leaf1 two equal-cost next hops to 10.255.1.13 again. Had either router missed an update, the CSNP would have shown it an older sequence number, and it would have asked for the newer copy with a PSNP.

@13
Last, every LSP ages. Each router counts its copy's remaining lifetime down, and the originator refreshes it with a higher sequence number about every 15 minutes, before its 1,200 seconds run out. 900 seconds on, every copy has 300 seconds left, and leaf3 is about to refresh it. An LSP that does reach zero is purged from every database, which a later lesson in this module shows going badly wrong. To generalise: a sequence number to say which copy is newer, a lifetime so that nothing lives forever, acknowledgements so that nothing is lost, and CSNPs as the safety net when a link returns. So the signal worth building is each router's list of LSP IDs and sequence numbers compared across the fabric, plus retransmission counters and how fast each originator's sequence number climbs.
