@0
The same picture as before: leaf 1 in Amsterdam sending 80 gigabits per second to leaf 2 in Singapore, 40 on each circuit, and circuit 1 delivered through a carrier's optical network. One thing changes, and it is the idea to hold onto: the BFD timers. Detection was 900 of the 1,018 milliseconds last time, so detection is where the time is won.

@1
The two spine 1s now send each other a BFD control packet every 50 milliseconds, and the multiplier is still 3, so the session goes down after 150 milliseconds of silence. The packet's interval fields now read 50,000 microseconds. IS-IS is still the client, its own 30 second hold time still far too slow.

@2-3
At time zero the fibre breaks inside the carrier's network, and the ports stay up because the carrier keeps sending light. Half of leaf 1's Singapore traffic is now lost. Spine 1 in Amsterdam expects a BFD packet every 50 milliseconds, and the missed intervals start to climb.

@4
At 150 milliseconds, three intervals after the last packet, again the worst case, spine 1 declares the session down and IS-IS drops the adjacency. Spine 1 in Singapore reaches the same verdict on its own, within one interval.

@5-7
Everything after detection is unchanged, so watch the timeline instead of the steps. The 50 millisecond LSP generation wait brings it to 200 milliseconds, and flooding to the Amsterdam leaves to 202. Leaf 1's 50 millisecond SPF wait and its 1 millisecond run bring it to 253, with spine 2 as the only next hop, and the same brief microloop through spine 1 while the leaves catch up. The 15 millisecond write of the shared ECMP group lands at 268 milliseconds, and all 80 gigabits per second flow through circuit 2.

@8
From cut to recovered traffic, 268 milliseconds, 150 of them detection, and about 1.3 gigabytes lost on circuit 1, a quarter of what the slower timers cost. On the 105 millisecond round trip to Singapore, TCP's first retransmission leaves at about 305 milliseconds and already finds the path repaired.

@9
Without BFD nothing improves: the 30 second hold timer still detects the cut, recovery takes about 30.1 seconds, and about 150.6 gigabytes are lost. Even 1 second hellos with a 3 second hold detect 20 times more slowly than BFD at 50 milliseconds times 3.

@10
The general lesson: cutting detection from 900 to 150 milliseconds cut recovery almost four times, and what remains, 118 milliseconds, is mostly the two deliberate 50 millisecond waits. Faster timers are not free, though. Timers much below 100 milliseconds times 3 usually need BFD in the line card or ASIC, not on the CPU, or a busy control plane misses its own packets and declares a healthy link dead. That is why Meridian runs its circuits at 300 milliseconds on purpose: a protected carrier circuit switches over in about 50 milliseconds, and BFD should not trigger an intercontinental reroute for it.
