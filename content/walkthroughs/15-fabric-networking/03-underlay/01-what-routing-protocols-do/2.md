@0
On screen is Meridian's Amsterdam underlay as a small example, two spines and four leaves, in one IS-IS level 2 area, 49.0001. There are eight adjacencies, one per leaf to spine link. Every router holds the same link-state database of six LSPs, one per router, each describing that router's neighbours and prefixes. Hold onto one rule, because it is the whole of flooding: a router passes an LSP on only when it is newer than its own copy, and the sequence number decides what newer means.

@1
This is leaf3's LSP. Its ID is leaf3's system ID, derived from its loopback 10.255.1.13, plus pseudonode and fragment numbers. Its sequence number is 2 A in hex, with 1,043 seconds of lifetime left, and every router's copy says the same. Inside, it lists two neighbours, spine1 and spine2, at metric 10, and three prefixes: its loopback and its two slash 31 links. From that database, leaf1 reaches 10.255.1.13 at metric 20 with two next hops, spine1 and spine2.

@2
The fibre between leaf3 and spine2 is cut, and both ends lose the adjacency. leaf3 builds a new LSP: the sequence number raised to 2 B, a fresh lifetime of 1,200 seconds, and only spine1 as a neighbour. spine2 does the same with its own LSP, from 51 to 52 in hex, dropping leaf3.

@3
leaf3 floods the new LSP to the one neighbour it has left, spine1. IS-IS packets travel directly inside Ethernet frames rather than inside IP, so flooding works even when no IP route exists yet.

@4
spine1 compares sequence numbers. 2 B is higher than the 2 A it holds, so this copy is newer: spine1 replaces its entry and acknowledges with a PSNP, a partial sequence number PDU, naming the LSP and 2 B. Without that acknowledgement, leaf3 would resend the LSP every 5 seconds, the usual retransmit interval on point to point links.

@5
spine1 floods the LSP on every other adjacency, to leaf1, leaf2 and leaf4, but not back to leaf3, which sent it. Each leaf finds 2 B newer than its 2 A, installs it, and acknowledges with its own PSNP.

@6
Each of those leaves floods it on in turn, to its other neighbour, spine2. So three copies head for spine2 within about a millisecond.

@7
spine2 installs the first copy and acknowledges all three. The second and third carry a sequence number it already holds, so they go no further. That is how flooding ends with no loops and no hop count: the rule from the start, pass it on only if it is newer than mine.

@8
Every router's copy of leaf3's LSP is now 2 B, and spine2's new LSP, 52, has flooded the same way from the other side: six identical databases again. The whole exchange takes tens of milliseconds, mostly the deliberate wait, often 50 milliseconds, before a router generates an LSP, in case more changes follow.

@9
Now every router runs SPF, Dijkstra's shortest path algorithm, over the same database. A link is used only if the LSPs at both ends list each other, and neither leaf3 nor spine2 lists the other any more. leaf1's route to 10.255.1.13 keeps metric 20, but now has a single next hop, spine1.

@10
From the same database, spine2 works out that it can still reach leaf3 at metric 30: down to any of the other three leaves, up to spine1 and down again, so it has three equal-cost next hops, via leaf1, leaf2 and leaf4. To IS-IS a leaf is just another router. The eBGP underlay, in the lesson on BGP as the underlay, forbids exactly this kind of path.

@11
The fibre is repaired. leaf3 and spine2 bring the adjacency up with a three-way hello handshake: down, initializing, up. Each then sends a CSNP, a complete sequence number PDU, listing every LSP it holds with its sequence number. Flooding through spine1 kept both databases current, so all six entries match, leaf3's 2 B and spine2's 52 among them, and nothing needs to be sent.

@12
Both routers then originate LSPs that include the restored adjacency, leaf3 at 2 C and spine2 at 53, which flood exactly as before. SPF everywhere gives leaf1 two equal-cost next hops to 10.255.1.13 again. Had a router missed an update, the CSNP would have shown it an older sequence number, and it would have asked for the newer copy with a PSNP.

@13
Every LSP also ages. Each router counts its copy's lifetime down, and the originator refreshes it with a higher sequence number about every 15 minutes, well before the 1,200 seconds run out. Here, 900 seconds on, every copy has 300 seconds left and leaf3 is about to refresh. An LSP that reaches zero is purged everywhere, which the lesson on underlay failures in production shows going badly wrong. The pattern to carry away: identical databases, built by flooding and sequence numbers, are what make every router's SPF agree. And notice what the cut cost leaf1: not reachability, but one of its two next hops. In a Clos a missing adjacency costs capacity and nothing else alarms, so a platform has to count it.
