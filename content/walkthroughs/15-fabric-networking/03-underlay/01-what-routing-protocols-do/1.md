@0
On screen is Amsterdam joined to Singapore by two long-haul circuits, in a small example. leaf1 in Amsterdam is sending 80 gigabits per second to leaf2 in Singapore, and ECMP splits it over both circuits, about 40 each. Its route to leaf2's loopback, 10.255.2.12, has two next hops, spine1 and spine2, both at metric 1,020. Circuit 1 runs through a carrier's optical network, so the light spine1 receives comes from the carrier's equipment in Amsterdam, not from Singapore. Hold onto one idea, because it is the whole story: convergence time is mostly detection, and the algorithm is the cheap part.

@1
spine1 in Amsterdam and spine1 in Singapore run a BFD session, Bidirectional Forwarding Detection, across the circuit. Each sends a small UDP control packet every 300 milliseconds, and the header shows the two fields that matter: a desired transmit interval of 300 milliseconds and a detect multiplier of 3. Three missed intervals, 900 milliseconds, and the session is down. IS-IS is registered as a client of BFD on this link, because its own hellos, every 10 seconds with a 30 second hold time, are far too slow to rely on.

@2
At time zero a fibre breaks inside the carrier's network, between its own transponders. This service does not pass the failure on, so the carrier keeps sending both spines light and their ports stay up. Neither spine notices anything, and the 40 gigabits per second hashed onto circuit 1, half of leaf1's Singapore traffic, is now being lost.

@3
What spine1 can notice is silence. It expects a BFD packet from Singapore every 300 milliseconds, none arrives, and its count of missed intervals climbs towards 3. IS-IS on its own, waiting on its 30 second hold timer, would still call the adjacency up.

@4
At 900 milliseconds, three intervals after the last packet, which here arrived just before the cut, the worst case, spine1 declares the session down and tells IS-IS at once, which drops the adjacency. spine1 in Singapore reaches the same verdict independently, within one interval. That is BFD's whole job: detection.

@5
Now propagation. spine1 waits its initial LSP generation delay of 50 milliseconds, in case more changes arrive to batch, then floods a new LSP, its list of neighbours and prefixes, without circuit 1. It reaches leaf1 and the other Amsterdam leaves about 2 milliseconds later, 952 milliseconds after the cut. In Singapore, spine1 does the same for the leaves there.

@6
Then computation. leaf1 waits its own initial SPF delay, another 50 milliseconds, and runs SPF in about 1 millisecond, at 1,003 milliseconds. The route to 10.255.2.12 now has a single next hop, spine2. Meanwhile spine1 already sends Singapore traffic down through the leaves, and a leaf that has not yet updated hashes some of it back up, so until every leaf catches up a few packets bounce between them: a microloop.

@7
Last, FIB programming. The new route is written into the forwarding chip in about 15 milliseconds, because every affected prefix shares one ECMP group and only that group is rewritten. A box that rewrites each prefix separately can take far longer. Now all 80 gigabits per second leave through spine2 and circuit 2, and traffic flows again.

@8
In total, about 1,018 milliseconds from cut to recovered traffic, and 900 of them were detection. At 40 gigabits per second that is about 5.1 gigabytes of packets lost on circuit 1. TCP retransmits them and connections survive, but the affected flows stall for at least that long, and longer where TCP's retransmission timer has backed off, which latency-sensitive services notice.

@9
The second timeline is the same failure without BFD. The only detector left is IS-IS itself: with the common default of 10 second hellos and a multiplier of 3, the adjacency dies when its 30 second hold timer runs out. The remaining steps add the same 118 milliseconds, so recovery takes about 30.1 seconds and about 150.6 gigabytes are lost. Even hellos tuned to 1 second, with a 3 second hold, detect 3.3 times more slowly than BFD at 300 milliseconds times 3.

@10
To generalise. BFD exists for failures the port cannot see, like this one: had the fibre run straight between the two spines, loss of light would have taken the port down within a few milliseconds without it. And it has a floor: timers much below 100 milliseconds times 3 usually need BFD to run in the line card or the chip rather than on the CPU, or a busy control plane misses its own packets and declares a healthy link dead. So when someone asks why a network took 30 seconds to heal, start with detection. Here it was 88 percent of the tuned total and almost all of the untuned one, while the algorithm took about a millisecond.
