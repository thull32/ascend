@0
On screen is Meridian's Amsterdam fabric in its small form: four spines and four leaves on 400 gigabit links, with spine1 and spine2 also carrying the two 100 gigabit circuits to Singapore. Tonight's change upgrades spine2's operating system, which means a reboot, and the goal is a hitless change: not one packet lost. Hold onto one idea, because the whole procedure follows from it: move the traffic off while the spine still forwards, and let a measurement, not a timer, open every gate. The shape is pre-check, drain, verify, work, restore, verify.

@1
The pre-check does the arithmetic before anything is touched. With one of four spines out, each leaf spreads its traffic over three uplinks, so each carries four thirds of its load. At 14:00, leaf3's 270 gigabits per second per uplink would become 360, which is 90 percent of the link. And the two circuits' 45 gigabits each would all land on spine1's circuit: 90 gigabits, also 90 percent. Both are over Meridian's 80 percent ceiling, so the tool refuses to start. Nothing has been touched, so there is nothing to undo.

@2
In the 02:00 window the same check passes. leaf3 goes from 165 to 220 gigabits per second per uplink, 55 percent, and the circuits from two times 35 to 70 gigabits on one, 70 percent. The state checks pass too: four of four spines in service, all 18 IS-IS adjacencies up, sixteen leaf to spine links plus the two circuits, every leaf with four equal-cost paths, no open alarms and clean probes. The tool saves this as the pre-check snapshot, the definition of back to normal.

@3
Now the drain. spine2 raises the IS-IS metric on all five of its links, 10 on the leaf links and 52,500 on the circuit, to 16,777,214, the largest metric the shortest-path calculation still uses, and floods a new link-state packet saying so. Every leaf reruns its calculation. In leaf3's table for leaf1's loopback, the path through spine2 now costs 16,777,224, against 20 through spine1, spine3 or spine4, so spine2 drops out of the equal-cost group. The other common way is the IS-IS overload bit, which tells other routers never to route through the device, while a maximum metric keeps it as a last resort.

@4
Traffic moves off as each leaf updates its forwarding table. On the chart, leaf3's uplinks go from four times 165 to three times 220 gigabits per second, and Singapore traffic shifts onto spine1's circuit, 70 of its 100 gigabits. No packet needs to be lost, because spine2 keeps forwarding whatever still reaches it until every leaf has moved: make before break. A flow that changes spine may see a few packets reordered, once.

@5
Before touching spine2, prove it is empty, port by port. Each of its five ports carries 10 to 20 kilobits per second: just the IS-IS hellos, BFD and LLDP that keep its adjacencies up. The busiest remaining uplink is at 220 gigabits, 55 percent, as predicted, no fabric port counts a discard, and probes through spine1, spine3 and spine4 are clean.

@6
Now the work. spine2 reboots into the new release, its links go dark, and the leaves see five adjacencies drop. The change-aware monitoring expects exactly that for this change, so nobody is paged, and nothing else notices: zero packets lost, zero probes failed, three equal-cost paths per leaf, unchanged since the drain. It is an ordinary spine failure, with the traffic moved away first.

@7
spine2 comes back, and because the high metric is part of its saved configuration, it boots still costed out. Its adjacencies form but it attracts no traffic, so this is when it gets checked: the new release, five of five adjacencies up, 52 routes, the same as spine1, every optic lane in range and zero interface errors. A drain held only in the running configuration would have vanished at the reboot.

@8
Restore. spine2 sets its metrics back to 10 on the leaf links and 52,500 on the circuit and floods a new link-state packet. In leaf3's table every spine costs 20 again, so all four are back in the equal-cost group. leaf3's uplinks return to four times 165 gigabits per second, and each Singapore circuit to 35.

@9
The final check compares against the pre-check snapshot: four times 165 on leaf3 before and after, 18 adjacencies, zero discards, clean probes across every spine, and zero packets lost during the change. Both snapshots and every step's timestamp go into the change record: the audit trail. The pattern generalises to any device: compute whether the rest can carry its load, cost it out, prove per port that it is empty, work on it, check it while it still carries nothing, then restore and compare. Each gate is a measured value against a threshold, and each undo is cheapest before the point of no return, the moment the work begins.
