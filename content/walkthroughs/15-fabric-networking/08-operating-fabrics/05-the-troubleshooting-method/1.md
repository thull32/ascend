@0
On screen is Meridian's Amsterdam fabric with all four spines, and a probe agent behind the leaves: 10.250.11.10 behind leaf1 and 10.250.13.10 behind leaf3. The complaint, already scoped: Acme's web servers behind leaf1 talk to its databases behind leaf3, and some requests are slow, now and then, while others are fine. Intermittent, with retries that work, is the scope answer that points at some ECMP paths and not others. So hold onto one idea: in this fabric a hash of the five-tuple picks the path, so you test paths by varying the five-tuple, and you read every result by the path it took.

@1
Reachability first, with ping. 100 sent, 100 received, 0 percent loss, an average round trip of 52 microseconds. ICMP has no ports, so every echo between these two addresses hashes the same way, and leaf1 sends them all through one spine, here spine1. One path out of four is tested; three are not.

@2
So the agent runs probes that can cover every path: TWAMP Light, 16 UDP streams to the reflector's port 862, 10 packets a second each, identical except for the source port, 50001 to 50016. A different source port is a different five-tuple, so leaf1's hash spreads the streams over the spines. The reflector timestamps each packet and sends it back with its own sequence number, so loss can be split by direction. For now the spine column holds only question marks.

@3
To fill it in, the agent sends one packet per stream with that stream's exact five-tuple and a TTL of 2. It expires at the spine, which answers with an ICMP time exceeded from its own address on the link, 10.254.3.0 for spine3. The hash ignores the TTL, so this packet follows the stream's path exactly. The map: 4 streams on spine1, 5 on spine2, 3 on spine3 and 4 on spine4.

@4
Five minutes later, 3,000 packets per stream. Three streams lost about 4 percent from leaf1 to leaf3: 3.9, 4.1 and 3.8 percent. The rest are clean, apart from one lost packet of noise on a stream through spine1. The 99th percentile round trip is 61 microseconds on every stream, the lossy ones included: a latency graph shows nothing. Averaged over all 16 streams, the loss is 0.7 percent: a fabric-wide number that hides a path that is badly broken.

@5
This is the capacity step done per path, not across the fabric. Grouped by spine, spine3 carries all three bad streams and loses 3.93 percent; spine1 shows 0.01 percent, and spines 2 and 4 lose nothing. Acme's own connections hash the same way, so about a quarter of them cross spine3 and suffer retransmissions. That is why the report said sometimes slow, rather than down.

@6
Is it spine3 itself, or one of its links? Bring in the agent behind leaf2 and you get two more pairs through spine3. leaf2 to leaf3: 5 streams, about 4 percent loss. leaf1 to leaf2: 5 streams, nothing lost. That healthy pair clears spine3 as a whole, and the link from leaf1 up to it. The only element every bad path shares and the good path lacks is the link from spine3 down to leaf3.

@7
Now read the counters at both ends of that one link, and they agree. spine3 sent 3.000 billion frames on it in five minutes. leaf3 received 2.883 billion intact and discarded 117 million, 3.9 percent, that failed the frame check. Its uncorrectable forward error correction codewords are rising, and lane 3 of the optic receives minus 8.6 dBm against about minus 2 on the other three lanes, too weak for error correction to repair every error. Both ends still say the link is up, so ECMP keeps sending it a quarter of the traffic bound for leaf3. This is the silent drop that is counted but not watched: the counter climbed the whole time, and nobody compared it with the link's own baseline.

@8
The fix is to cost out that one link, raising its IS-IS metric at both ends so no shortest path uses it. leaf1's next hops for leaf3's prefixes drop to three: 10.254.1.0, 10.254.2.0 and 10.254.4.0, which are spines 1, 2 and 4. The same 16 streams rehash, modulo 3 now, and every one of them is clean. That confirms the diagnosis while the optic replacement is scheduled.

@9
Two things made this work: enough distinct five-tuples to cover every path, and results grouped by the path each probe took. Even 16 random source ports leave each spine a 1 percent chance of getting no stream at all, so real probing systems map their paths and fill the gaps on purpose. Run continuously between every pair of racks, as large operators do, that probing turns the counter nobody watched into an alert. And ping, with its single five-tuple, would still be saying 0 percent loss.
