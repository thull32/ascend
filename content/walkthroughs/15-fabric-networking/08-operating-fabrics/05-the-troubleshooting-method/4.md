@0
On screen are eight GPUs of Meridian's San Jose cluster: GPU0 of servers srv001 to srv008, each with its own 400 gigabit per second ConnectX-7 NIC, all on the rail 1 leaf, rail1-leaf1. They train one 1 billion parameter model with data parallelism: every GPU holds a full copy of the model and works on a different slice of each batch. Hold onto the fact that makes RoCE troubleshooting different: training is synchronous, so every GPU waits at each collective for the slowest transfer.

@1-2
Step 1 starts with compute. The forward pass takes 100 milliseconds and the backward pass 200 more, and the network is silent throughout, because nothing needs to be exchanged yet. The backward pass leaves each GPU with 2 gigabytes of gradients, one 2-byte number per parameter, and no GPU can update its weights until all eight sets have been summed.

@3
At 300 milliseconds all eight start the all-reduce at once. In NCCL's ring algorithm each GPU sends 2 times seven eighths of 2 gigabytes, which is 3.5 gigabytes, and receives as much, so every rail 1 link runs flat out. At 400 gigabits per second, 50 gigabytes per second, that takes 70 milliseconds, and the GPUs have nothing to compute until it ends.

@4
So step 1 takes 370 milliseconds, and 70 of them, 18.9 percent, are GPU idle time spent waiting for the network. Real frameworks start reducing early gradients while the backward pass still runs, but whatever does not overlap adds straight to every step.

@5
Before step 2, srv005's link flaps and comes back at 200 gigabits per second instead of 400, for example because the two ends negotiated a lower speed over a marginal copper cable. The link is up and passing traffic, so a monitor that only checks whether links are up sees nothing wrong. The flap in the log and the port's speed are the only clues.

@6
Step 2's forward and backward passes take the same 300 milliseconds as before, srv005 included. A slow link costs nothing while no one is sending.

@7-8
Then the all-reduce, with the same 3.5 gigabytes each. But a ring advances in lockstep: each of its 14 steps, 2 times 8 minus 1, waits for the slowest transfer, the one through srv005. Every other link drops to half its rate. At the 70 millisecond mark, when step 1's all-reduce had already finished, every GPU has moved only half its data. At 25 gigabytes per second the whole exchange takes 140 milliseconds, and all eight GPUs sit idle for every millisecond of it.

@9
Step 2 takes 440 milliseconds, 70 longer than step 1, and each GPU's idle time doubles to 140 milliseconds, 31.8 percent of the step. One slow link out of eight slowed all eight GPUs, because a synchronous collective finishes only when its slowest transfer does.

@10
Now the scope problem this creates. Ask the GPUs which one is slow and they cannot say: all eight report the same step time and the same idle time. So scope from the network side. The leaf's port counters during that all-reduce show seven ports carrying 200 gigabits per second at 50 percent of their speed, and srv005's carrying the same 200 at 100 percent of its reduced speed. The one saturated port, the only one not running at 400, is the straggler.

@11
In the full 1,024 GPU job, one such link among 1,024 has the same effect. If it added the same 70 milliseconds to a 440 millisecond step, 15.9 percent of every GPU-hour would be wasted, about 3,900 GPU-hours a day. That is why AI fabrics are judged by job completion time rather than average link utilisation, and why you localise from per-port counters: in a lockstep job, every host looks equally slow.
