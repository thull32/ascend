@0
The job on screen: take Meridian's border gateway gw1.ams out of service for a software upgrade. Today it carries all of Meridian's ISP1 traffic in both directions. Users reach Meridian's public servers, 203.0.113.0 slash 26 behind leaf1.ams, through ISP1 and gw1, and outbound traffic leaves the same way. One rule decides how this ends: withdraw BGP first and remove the IGP last, because a router that still announces destinations it can no longer reach becomes a black hole.

@1
Look at gw1's forwarding table. It depends on two protocols. It announces 203.0.113.0 slash 24 to ISP1 from a static discard route, which needs nothing else to stay up. And it reaches the slash 26 through a BGP route whose next hop, leaf1's loopback 10.255.1.11, only IS-IS can resolve. On the right, ISP1 prefers gw1's announcement, with a local preference of 200 for a customer, over the prepended path through its peer ISP2, at 100.

@2
Step one, in the wrong order: gw1's IS-IS adjacencies are shut, and its BGP is left untouched. On a spine this really is the first step, because spines run no BGP, and the engineer was working from the spine runbook. gw1 loses every IS-IS route, so the next hop 10.255.1.11 can no longer be resolved, and the slash 26 leaves its forwarding table. The static slash 24 discard route stays.

@3
Outbound traffic recovers by itself. Every other router sees gw1's loopback, 10.255.1.201, vanish from IS-IS, so routes with that BGP next hop become unusable, and gw2's routes through ISP2 take over. The cost is a few seconds of outbound loss, below alert thresholds.

@4
Inbound does not recover. The eBGP session to ISP1 runs over the direct link, not over IS-IS, so it stays Established. gw1 keeps announcing the slash 24 from its static route, ISP1 keeps preferring it, and every inbound packet for Meridian, here one for 203.0.113.10, still arrives at gw1.

@5
Inside gw1 the longest match for 203.0.113.10 is now the slash 24 discard route, so the packet is dropped, and so is every other packet ISP1 delivers. This is the BGP black hole, live for nearly all of Meridian's inbound internet traffic. In the incident that was about 6 gigabits per second, for 45 minutes.

@6
Now the table that explains the 45 minutes. The session is Established. One prefix advertised, as usual. IS-IS down, as the plan says. The ISP1 link shows normal inbound traffic, because utilisation measures arrivals, not delivery. Only two signals show the outage: the discard counter on gw1, climbing, and a probe from outside to 203.0.113.10, failing. And in the incident, the discard counter sat behind the alert suppression for the open maintenance window.

@7
The correct order is BGP withdrawn first, a measured check that almost nothing still arrives, and only then the IGP. The guard rails worth building: announce the aggregate only while the routes behind it exist, probe your own addresses from outside through each ISP, and have change tooling that knows each device role's steps and checks their order. The general lesson: during a maintenance, monitoring should assert the expected state rather than mute alerts. A drained device that still receives and discards 6 gigabits per second is a critical alert precisely because a maintenance is open.
