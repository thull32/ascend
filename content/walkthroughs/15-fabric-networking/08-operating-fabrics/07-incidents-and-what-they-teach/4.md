@0
On screen, a small piece of Meridian's San Jose back end: two of its eight spines and three of its sixteen leaves, both halves of rail 1 and the first leaf of rail 2. Each server box is that server's NIC on that leaf's rail. Priority 3 is lossless, and every link runs at 400 gigabits per second. The idea to hold: a PAUSE names only a priority on a link, not a flow, so it stops everything queued behind it, and pauses climb towards the senders.

@1
Three RDMA flows start. srv1 at 400 gigabits per second and srv65 at 200 both write to srv3: an incast of 600 into one 400-gigabit port. And srv66 writes 200 to srv5, an unrelated flow whose destination port is idle. rail1-leaf2 hashes both of its flows onto ai-spine1, which fills that uplink exactly but does not overload it.

@2
srv3's port is offered 600 for 400, its queue grows, and rail1-leaf1's priority 3 buffers cross XOFF on both ports that feed it, the one from srv1 and the one from ai-spine1. So it pauses both. The PAUSE to ai-spine1 stops all priority 3 traffic on that link, srv66's packets for the idle srv5 included.

@3
ai-spine1 can no longer send priority 3 down to rail1-leaf1, so packets pile up inside it, charged to the port they came in on, the one from rail1-leaf2. That buffer crosses XOFF, and ai-spine1 pauses rail1-leaf2. The congestion has moved one more hop upstream.

@4
rail1-leaf2's queue towards ai-spine1 stops too, its buffers from srv65 and srv66 fill, and it pauses both NICs. srv66 has stopped, although srv5's port is idle, because its packets sit behind srv3's in queues that PFC has frozen. This is head-of-line blocking, and srv66's flow is the innocent victim.

@5
What srv66's owner sees: throughput at zero, with zero drops, no ECN marks and no errors, while PAUSE counters climb on every link back towards the incast. Only following the pauses hop by hop, from rail1-leaf2 to ai-spine1 to rail1-leaf1, leads to srv3's congested port. In this lesson's incident the root was srv40's NIC, which never stopped pausing, so its tree never cleared.

@6
Here the root is an incast, and it ends, or DCQCN slows the senders. The buffers drain, resume frames travel back the same way, and srv66 is at 200 again. Congestion spreading like this is temporary. The next failure is not.

@7
Later the optic on the link between ai-spine1 and rail1-leaf1 fails. ai-spine1 sees the loss of light at once and recomputes first. Its only ways left to rail1-leaf1 go down through another leaf and back up via ai-spine2: valley paths. rail1-leaf2 has not updated yet, and still spreads that traffic over both spines.

@8
So rail1-leaf2 still hashes srv66's flow to ai-spine1, which sends it straight back down to rail1-leaf2, which sends it up again. The packets ping-pong in a micro-loop, losing one from their TTL at every pass, 63, then 62. That link is now offered far more than 400 gigabits per second.

@9
Queues fill in both directions. rail1-leaf2's buffer for packets that came from ai-spine1 crosses XOFF, so it pauses ai-spine1. And ai-spine1's buffer for packets that came from rail1-leaf2 crosses XOFF, so it pauses rail1-leaf2.

@10
Now each switch waits for the other. rail1-leaf2 cannot send until ai-spine1 frees buffer, and ai-spine1 cannot free buffer until it may send to rail1-leaf2, which it may not. That is a cyclic buffer dependency: a deadlock. The packets never move again, so not even their TTL can expire them, because TTL only falls when a packet is forwarded.

@11
Milliseconds later rail1-leaf2 updates its forwarding table and sends rail1-leaf1's traffic via ai-spine2 only. But the deadlock outlives the loop that caused it: the stuck packets are already queued for ports that stay paused. srv66's old packets still fill its buffer, so it stays paused, and any flow hashed onto ai-spine1, such as srv65's to srv2 on rail 2, is stuck too.

@12
The way out is the PFC watchdog. It notices a priority 3 queue paused, with packets waiting and nothing sent, for longer than its detection time, 200 milliseconds by SONiC's default. It declares a PFC storm, ignores further PAUSE frames for that queue, and, in drop mode, drops what it holds. In the incident the watchdogs had been left in alert mode, which only logs.

@13
Dropping the stuck packets breaks the cycle. srv66's flow resumes on its new path via ai-spine2, and srv65's reaches srv2. Once 200 milliseconds pass without a new storm, the queue is lossless again. The RoCE senders resend from the first missing sequence number, after a negative acknowledgement or an acknowledgement timeout, and the NICs count both.

@14
Prevention beats recovery. The valley path made the loop possible, and an underlay in which all spines share one AS rejects such paths; ConnectX NICs have a similar guard against pause storms of their own. The general lesson: congestion spreading is pauses up a tree, and a deadlock is pauses around a ring. So alert on pause duration, not frame count, page on watchdog storm events rather than logging them, and walk the pauses automatically to their root to name the server.
