@0
On screen is the small form of Meridian's Amsterdam underlay: two spines and four leaves in one IS-IS area, every router reaching the other five loopbacks. Since a recent change, the switches take their time from an NTP server reached through the fabric itself, behind leaf1.ams, not over the out-of-band network. Hold onto one rule, because the whole incident follows from it: any IS-IS router may purge any router's LSP, and every other router believes the purge.

@1
leaf4.ams reboots after a software upgrade, so for now the other five routers reach only four loopbacks each. Its hardware clock has lost its setting and starts at midnight on the first of January 2000. Nothing checks the clock before routing starts, and IS-IS works perfectly well in the year 2000.

@2
41 seconds after boot, its adjacencies to both spines come up, every router reaches all five loopbacks again, and the spines send leaf4 the other five LSPs. This leaf's operating system stores each LSP's expiry as a wall-clock time, receipt time plus remaining lifetime, instead of counting down. spine1's LSP arrived with 1,043 seconds left, so it expires at 18 minutes past midnight, on the first of January 2000.

@3
With the underlay up, leaf4 can finally reach its NTP server, through spine1 and leaf1. Notice the loop that has just formed: time now depends on the network, and, as the next frames show, the network depends on time.

@4
The reply says it is the 31st of October 2026, just after nine in the morning, and leaf4 steps its clock forward by 846,752,400 seconds, about 26 years and 10 months, in one jump, as NTP clients may do at boot. Every expiry it stored is now 26 years in the past. Its own LSP it simply re-originates. The other five are marked expired.

@5
So it purges each of them. A purge is the LSP flooded with its sequence number unchanged, a remaining lifetime of zero, and every topology field removed, as the header on screen shows. Each one also carries a purge-originator TLV, from RFC 6232, holding leaf4's system ID. That one field is the only clue to where the purges came from.

@6
The five purges flood to both spines. A purge beats a stored copy with the same sequence number, so each spine swaps the other routers' LSPs for the purges and floods them on, as if those routers had really vanished. The one purge a spine refuses is the purge of its own LSP.

@7
Within milliseconds every router holds purges for every LSP except its own and leaf4's. SPF uses a link only when the LSPs at both ends describe it, so it finds almost nothing. Each spine still reaches its direct neighbour leaf4, one loopback of five, and the leaves reach nothing at all. The topology has vanished everywhere at once, with every link and adjacency still up.

@8
Everything above the underlay falls with it. BGP marks every EVPN route whose next-hop loopback is no longer reachable as unusable, the VXLAN tunnels between leaves are removed, and tenant traffic stops across the whole site. The iBGP sessions to the route reflectors, which run between loopbacks, start counting down their hold timers.

@9
Meanwhile each router that received a purge of its own LSP, one it knows is still valid, re-issues it at once with a sequence number one higher and a fresh lifetime of 1,200 seconds. The new copies flood everywhere and replace the purges.

@10
leaf4 stores the new copies with expiries computed from its now-correct clock, so it has no reason to purge again, and SPF everywhere rebuilds the full topology: five of five, on every router. Here the gap lasts well under a second. In a real area flooding is paced, commonly one LSP every 33 milliseconds per interface, so Meridian's 70 or so LSPs take over 2 seconds to cross one link.

@11
IS-IS heals first; the overlay takes longer. Meridian's BGP hold timers are 9 seconds, and in a domain of hundreds of routers the gap outlasts them: the sessions drop and re-exchange every EVPN route, MAC and ARP state is relearned, and tenants see minutes. And the blast radius is the flooding domain, not the leaf. Meridian runs one level-2 domain across all three sites, so in the real incident this Amsterdam leaf's purges reached Singapore and San Jose too.

@12
The fixes remove the two latent conditions: take time over the out-of-band network, so the clock is right before routing starts, and age LSPs with a countdown, as the standard intends. The signals that would have named leaf4 within seconds are a clock-step log line from it, a burst of purges carrying its system ID, and the LSP count collapsing on every router at once. The general lesson: when one node can delete shared state for everyone, its mistakes reach the whole domain. So correlate a change on one device with a collapse everywhere, and halt a staged rollout when an outage is bigger than the unit of change.
