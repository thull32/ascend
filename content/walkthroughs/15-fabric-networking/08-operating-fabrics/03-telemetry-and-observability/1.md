@0
On screen is the small Amsterdam example: two spines, leaf2.ams with Globex's servers below it, and leaf4.ams with Meridian's collector, 10.250.1.10. On the left, on dashed lines, a management switch and an out-of-band monitor. Hold onto one idea, because the whole scenario rests on it: a signal that travels the same path as the failure cannot tell you what failed. To tell dead from congested, you need witnesses that do not share that path. Right now all is well. The collector holds a gNMI subscription, dial-in, so the collector opened the session to leaf2's port 9339, and every 10 seconds leaf2 sends its interface counters from its loopback, 10.255.1.12, up through spine1 and down to the collector. Look at the header panel: the telemetry is marked DSCP zero, best effort. The chart shows the link from leaf2 to spine1 at 38 percent, and leaf2 and spine1 agree.

@1
At five seconds past ten, a Globex batch job starts offering about 1.1 terabits a second towards the spines, more than leaf2's 800 gigabits a second of uplink. Both uplinks go to 100 percent. The telemetry waits in the same best-effort queue as the job and is tail-dropped with it: the sample stamped ten seconds past ten is lost on its way to spine1. TCP backs off after each loss, and the stream stalls. On the chart, leaf2's line has a hole where spine1 reports 99.8 percent.

@2
At thirty seconds past ten, the collector has missed three samples in a row and raises an absence of data alert. Its table shows leaf2's last sample still at ten o'clock, no data for 30 seconds, while the other three devices keep streaming. Without this alert nothing would fire. No value crossed a threshold, because no value arrived, and the dashboard would keep drawing 38 percent, green.

@3
Now the real question: is leaf2 dead, or alive but unable to get its data out? A second table lists four signals, with what each would show in either case. The first is already filled in. The gNMI session's own keepalive, an HTTP 2 ping, goes unanswered, but it would go unanswered either way, because it travels the same congested path as the data. It cannot decide.

@4
So the out-of-band monitor pings leaf2's management port, 172.16.1.12, over the separate management network, which the batch job never touches. The reply comes back in 0.6 milliseconds. The device is powered, its operating system is running and its CPU answers. A dead device would not reply.

@5
The second witness is the neighbours, whose own telemetry still flows. spine1 and spine2 report their links to leaf2 up, their IS-IS adjacencies to it Up, and 399 of 400 gigabits a second arriving from it on each port. Why do the IS-IS hellos survive when the telemetry does not? Switches send routing protocol packets in a network control queue, ahead of user traffic. Telemetry at DSCP zero gets no such priority.

@6
Verdict, at thirty-five seconds past ten: alive and congested, not dead. The platform shows leaf2 as alive but telemetry blind, neither down nor green. And it raises the real problem, saturated uplinks, from the spines' counters, because the device's own telemetry stopped at exactly the moment it had the most important thing to say.

@7
At four minutes and five seconds past ten, the job finishes. The old session did not survive four minutes of unanswered keepalives, so the collector closed it and kept re-dialling. Now a new subscription comes up, and the sample at four minutes and ten seconds past ten arrives, showing 40 percent. The collector records the missing samples as a gap from a device known to be alive, never as zeros. Look at the chart: leaf2's line is blank across those four minutes while spine1's sits at 99.8 percent. Zeros would have drawn an idle link in the busiest four minutes of the day.

@8
The follow-up change marks telemetry DSCP 16, CS2, the class set aside for operations and management traffic, and gives that class its own queue with a guaranteed share; the header panel now shows 16. Here is the pattern to take away. The bulk of the telemetry rides the data network, because only the data network has the bandwidth. Heartbeats and the alert path ride out of band, because only they need independence. Alert on absence, record a gap as a gap, and give every silent device two independent answers. The trap is checking liveness over the path that failed: a keepalive or a loopback ping across the fabric shares fate with the telemetry it is meant to verify.
