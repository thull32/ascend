@0
The same eight values, 38, 27, 43, 3, 9, 82, 10 and 5, now drawn as bars in the array itself. Merge sort splits in halves recursively, then merges sorted halves with two pointers, one at the front of each half, always taking the smaller front. That merge is the combine step, and it runs in a single pass.

@1-3
The splits come first: positions 0 to 7 into 0 to 3 and 4 to 7, then 0 to 3 into two pairs, then the first pair into single elements.

@4-5
The first merge takes 27 from the right half into position 0, then 38 from the left into position 1.

@6-8
Positions 2 and 3 split, and their merge takes 3 from the right, then 43 from the left.

@9-12
Now the two sorted pairs merge into positions 0 to 3. 3 comes from the right, then 27 and 38 from the left, then 43 from the right. Every step of a merge is one comparison of two fronts and one write.

@13-19
The right half of the array goes the same way. 9 and 82 merge in order, 9 then 82. 10 and 5 merge as 5, then 10.

@20-23
Those two pairs merge into positions 4 to 7: 5, 9, 10, 82.

@24-31
The final merge fills the whole array: 3 from the left, then 5, 9 and 10 from the right, then 27, 38 and 43 from the left, and 82 from the right.

@32
Sorted. About log n levels, each doing linear merge work, gives n log n on every input, because the split never depends on the values. The price is linear scratch space for the merge, and because ties take from the left half first, the sort is stable. Keep this merge in mind: when it takes an element from the right half, every element still waiting on the left is larger, and counting them is exactly how the lesson counts inversions at no extra cost.
