@0
What runs here is merge sort itself, on eight values: 5, 3, 2, 1, 4, 6, 7 and 0. It is here for its shape, not its values. The fast Fourier transform has exactly this recursion tree: two half-size subproblems, and a linear combine step on the way back up. Merge sort combines with a merge; the transform combines with a butterfly.

@1-3
The splits go down the left edge: eight positions into two halves of four, four into two pairs, and the first pair into single elements. Three levels of splitting, as with any eight inputs. The transform splits differently, into the even-indexed and the odd-indexed coefficients, but it also halves the problem at every level, so the tree is the same.

@4-9
Single elements are the base case and return at once: 5, then 3. Then the first combine at the bottom level: 3 is written first, then 5. Two writes for two elements.

@10-17
The second pair, 2 and 1, splits and combines the same way: 1, then 2.

@18-22
One level up, the two sorted pairs combine into 1, 2, 3, 5. Four writes for four elements. Each combine touches every element of its node exactly once.

@23-44
The right half does the same, level by level. 4 and 6 combine as 4, 6. 7 and 0 combine as 0, 7. Then those pairs combine into 0, 4, 6, 7, another four writes.

@45-53
The root's combine writes all eight: 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7.

@54
15 calls, and each merging level moved all 8 elements: four combines of 2, two combines of 4, one of 8. That is the recurrence: two calls on half the size, plus linear work, which solves to n log n. Replace each merge with the butterfly, which takes the even half's value and the odd half's value times a root of unity, and emits their sum and their difference, and you have the FFT, with the same cost. The roots of unity are what let the halving continue all the way down, because their squares pair up as plus and minus again.
