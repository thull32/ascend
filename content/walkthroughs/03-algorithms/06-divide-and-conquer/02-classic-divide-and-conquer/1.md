@0
Ten sorted values, from 2 up to 91, and the target is 23. Each call looks at the middle of its range and makes exactly one recursive call, on the half that can still contain the target. One call per level means the recursion tree is not a tree at all but a chain.

@1
The first call covers positions 0 to 9. The middle is position 4, which holds 16. 16 is less than 23, so the target can only be on the right. Recurse on positions 5 to 9, and the left half is gone without ever being looked at.

@2
Positions 5 to 9: the middle is position 7, which holds 56. 56 is greater than 23, so recurse on the left, positions 5 to 6.

@3
Positions 5 to 6: the middle is position 5, which holds 23. Found. This call returns 5.

@4-5
Now the answer travels back up the chain. The call on 5 to 9 returns 5 unchanged, and so does the call on 0 to 9. The recursive call was the last thing each call did, so nothing is combined on the way up.

@6
Found at position 5 after 3 calls. That is the recurrence with one subproblem of half the size and constant work, which solves to log n time. Quickselect is the same idea with a linear partition in each call, and it finds a median in expected linear time. So when your divide and conquer lands on n log n and you need linear, ask whether you can tell which half holds the answer without solving both.
