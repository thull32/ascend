@0
Five meetings, sorted by start: 1 to 4, 2 to 5, 3 to 6, 4 to 7 and 5 to 8. How many rooms do they need? Keep a min-heap of the end times of the rooms in use, so the root is always the room that frees up first. For each meeting, if that earliest room is already free by the meeting's start, reuse it: pop it. Then push the new meeting's end. The answer is the largest the heap ever gets.

@1
The meeting from 1 to 4 starts. No rooms are open, so we push its end, 4. One room.

@2-3
The meeting from 2 to 5 starts. The earliest end is 4, and 4 is after 2, so that room is still busy. Push 5. It is appended at the bottom, is not smaller than its parent 4, and stays. Two rooms.

@4-5
The meeting from 3 to 6. The earliest end is still 4, still busy at time 3. Push 6. Three rooms, and that is the peak: at time 3, three meetings are in progress at once.

@6-8
The meeting from 4 to 7 starts. Now the earliest end, 4, is not after the start, so the room that meeting 1 to 4 used is free at exactly 4. Pop it. The last leaf, 6, moves up to the root and sinks below 5, so 5 is the new earliest end.

@9-10
Push this meeting's end, 7. The heap is back to three rooms, but nobody new was given one: a room was reused.

@11-13
The meeting from 5 to 8 starts. The earliest end, 5, is free at 5, so pop it. 7 moves to the root and sinks below 6.

@14-15
Push 8. The heap holds 6, 7 and 8, still three rooms.

@16
Three rooms, the largest the heap ever got. Each push and pop walks one path from root to leaf, so with the sort the whole thing is n log n. Why is the peak the minimum and not only a bound? At time 3 three meetings overlap, so nothing can do with fewer, and the heap opens a new room only when none is free, so it never uses more. Watch the comparison: a room freed at 4 is usable by a meeting starting at 4. Test it with back-to-back meetings; a strict less-than there invents a phantom conflict.
