@0
Coins worth 1, 3 and 4, and we want to make 6 with as few coins as possible. Greedy grabs the largest coin that fits: a 4, then a 1, then another 1. Three coins. Before the table fills in: can you do better?

[pause]

The table answers it. Each cell holds the fewest coins that make one amount, from 0 up to 6. The rule: the best for an amount is one coin plus the best for whatever is left after that coin, trying every coin that fits.

@1
The base case. Amount 0 needs no coins.

@2-3
Amount 1 can only use the 1 coin, on top of amount 0: one coin. Amount 2 is a 1 on top of amount 1: two coins.

@4-5
Amount 3 has a choice. A 1 on top of amount 2 costs three coins, but the 3 coin on top of amount 0 costs one, so the cell is 1. Amount 4 is the same story with the 4 coin: one coin.

@6
Amount 5. A 1 on top of amount 4 gives two coins, as good as anything else, so the cell is 2.

@7
Now amount 6, the one greedy got wrong. Three options: a 1 on top of amount 5, which is three coins; a 3 on top of amount 3, which is two; or a 4 on top of amount 2, which is three. The 3 wins. Greedy's first choice, the 4, leaves 2, and 2 costs two more coins.

@8
Fewest coins for 6 is 2: three plus three, recovered by following the recorded choices back from 6 to 0. The table costs the amount times the number of coins. The lesson here is the counterexample: no optimal way to make 6 contains a 4, so the greedy-choice property fails for this coin system. Largest-first is only safe for canonical systems, like US or Euro coins. In an interview, look for the counterexample before you try the proof.
