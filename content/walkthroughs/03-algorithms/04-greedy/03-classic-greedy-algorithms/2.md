@0
Five nodes, A to E, joined by weighted edges, and we want the shortest distance from A to each. A starts at 0 and everything else at infinity. Dijkstra's greedy rule: always settle the closest unsettled node, and claim that its distance is final. Hold onto why: every other path to it has to leave the settled set through some node that is already at least as far, and with no negative weights a path can only get longer from there.

@1
A is the closest, at 0, so it is settled first.

@2-3
Relax A's edges. The edge to B weighs 4, so B's tentative distance drops from infinity to 4. The edge to C weighs 1, so C drops to 1.

@4
The closest unsettled node is now C, at 1. Settle it. Nothing can ever reach C more cheaply.

@5
Relax C's edges. C to B weighs 2, so B can be reached for 1 plus 2, which is 3, better than the 4 it had. B drops to 3.

@6-7
C to D weighs 8, so D becomes 9. C to E weighs 10, so E becomes 11.

@8
The closest unsettled node is B, at 3. Settle it. Notice that B's first guess, 4, was wrong, and settling in order of distance is what fixed it before it mattered.

@9
B to D weighs 5, and 3 plus 5 is 8, better than 9. D drops to 8.

@10
Settle D, at 8. Its edge to E gives 8 plus 3, which is 11, no better than the 11 E already has, so nothing changes.

@11
Settle E, at 11.

@12
Every distance is final: C at 1, B at 3, D at 8, E at 11, and the highlighted edges form the shortest-path tree. With a binary heap the cost is the number of nodes plus edges, times log of the number of nodes. Once settled, a distance never changes: that is the greedy-choice property, and it is the same cut idea that justifies Prim and Kruskal. Negative edges break exactly the step where a path can only get longer, which is why Dijkstra is wrong on them and Bellman-Ford exists.
