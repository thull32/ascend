@0
Six symbols with their frequencies per 100 characters: a is 45, d is 16, b is 13, c is 12, e is 9 and f is 5. Huffman's algorithm builds the shortest prefix code with one rule, repeated: take the two lightest subtrees, join them under a new parent whose weight is their sum, and put that parent back. A min-heap is exactly the tool, because it always hands you the lightest.

@1-11
First, all six weights go into the heap: 5, 9, 12, 13, 16 and 45. Each is appended at the next free slot and is already no smaller than its parent, so nothing sifts. The lightest, 5, sits at the root.

@12-18
The first merge. Pop 5, which is f. The last leaf, 45, moves to the root and sinks back down. Then pop 9, which is e. f and e are the two rarest symbols, and they become siblings at the bottom of the tree.

@19-20
Push their parent, weight 14.

@21-26
The second merge. The two lightest are now 12 and 13, c and b, so pop them both.

@27-29
Push their parent, 25. It lands at the bottom, under 45, and sifts up one level.

@30-37
The third merge pops 14, the subtree holding f and e, and 16, which is d. Their parent, 30, is pushed.

@38-43
The fourth merge pops 25 and 30 and pushes 55. Only two subtrees remain: a, at 45, and everything else, at 55.

@44-47
The last merge pops 45 and 55, and pushes the root, 100.

@48
The heap holds a single node, the root of the whole tree. Reading the tree with left as 0 and right as 1, a gets a one-bit code, b, c and d get three bits, and e and f get four. Here is the shortcut worth remembering: the pushed weights, 14, 25, 30, 55 and 100, add up to 224, the total encoded length in bits, because every merge adds one bit to the code of every symbol beneath it. That is 2.24 bits per character against 3 for a fixed-width code. Why is grabbing the two lightest safe? An exchange argument: in some optimal tree the two rarest symbols are siblings at the deepest level, and merging them leaves a smaller problem of the same kind. With a heap, the whole build is n log n.
