@0
A 3 by 3 grid of costs, moving only right or down, and you want the cheapest route from the top-left to the bottom-right. The costs are 1, 3, 1 on the top row, then 1, 5, 1, then 4, 2, 1. Each table cell will hold the cheapest path cost to reach it, including both ends. The fill order is exactly the one from unique paths; only the combine changes. Instead of adding the cell above and the cell to the left, take the cheaper of the two and add this cell's own cost.

@1
Base case: the start cell costs 1, so it holds 1.

@2-4
The borders are running sums, because each has only one neighbour. Along the top row: 3 plus 1 is 4, then 1 plus 4 is 5. Down the left column, the first cell of row 1 is its own 1 plus the 1 above, which is 2.

@5
The first real decision, the middle cell, which costs 5. Above it is 4, to its left is 2. Arrive from the cheaper side: 5 plus 2 is 7.

@6
Row 1, column 2 costs 1. Above is 5, left is 7. The cheaper is above, so 1 plus 5 is 6.

@7-8
Row 2 starts with the left column again: 4 plus the 2 above is 6. Then the middle of the bottom row costs 2; above is 7, left is 6, so 2 plus 6 is 8.

@9
The answer cell costs 1. Above it is 6, to its left is 8. The cheaper is above: 1 plus 6 is 7.

@10
The minimum path sum is 7, and the route is highlighted: right, right, down, down, through the cells costing 1, 3, 1, 1 and 1. The table stores costs, not moves, so the route is read back from the corner: at each cell, step to whichever neighbour, above or left, produced its value. From the corner that is the cell above, 6; from there the cell above again, 5; then left along the top row to the start. The cost is rows times columns, and the proof is the cut-and-paste argument: a cheapest path's prefix must itself be a cheapest path to its end, or you could swap in the cheaper one.
