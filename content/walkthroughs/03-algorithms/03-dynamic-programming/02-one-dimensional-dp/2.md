@0
Coins of 1, 3 and 4, and the target is 6. Cell a holds the fewest coins that make amount a exactly. The recurrence asks about the last coin: if it was coin c, the amount before it was a minus c, made as cheaply as possible. So cell a is 1 plus the smallest of the cells a minus c, over every coin that fits. Fill from 0 upwards, because a minus c is always smaller than a.

@1
Base case: cell 0 is 0. Zero coins make zero.

@2-3
For amounts 1 and 2, only the 1 coin fits. Cell 1 is 1 plus cell 0, which is 1. Cell 2 is 1 plus cell 1, which is 2.

@4
Amount 3 is the first real choice. Coin 1 would land on cell 2, which is 2; coin 3 lands on cell 0, which is 0. The smaller is 0, so cell 3 is 1: a single 3 coin.

@5
Amount 4 reads cells 3, 1 and 0, holding 1, 1 and 0. The 4 coin on top of nothing wins: cell 4 is 1.

@6
Amount 5 reads cells 4, 2 and 1, holding 1, 2 and 1. The best is 1, so cell 5 is 2, a 1 coin on top of the 4.

@7
Now the answer cell, amount 6. Coin 1 lands on cell 5, which is 2. Coin 3 lands on cell 3, which is 1. Coin 4 lands on cell 2, which is 2. The minimum goes through cell 3: cell 6 is 1 plus 1, which is 2. The greedy route, a 4 and then two 1s, is the coin 4 candidate here, worth 3 coins, and it loses.

@8
Fewest coins for 6 is 2: 3 plus 3. Following the recorded choice back from 6 to 0, coin 3 then coin 3, reconstructs it. The cost is the amount times the number of coins, which is pseudo-polynomial: it grows with the numeric value of the amount, not with the number of digits needed to write it. That is fine for the amounts of ten thousand or so that an interviewer gives you, but an amount of a billion needs a billion cells.
