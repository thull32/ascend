@0
Coin change is the unbounded knapsack in disguise. The coins, 1, 3 and 4, are items whose weight is their face value; the amount, 6, is the capacity; and instead of maximising value you minimise the number of coins. Each coin may be used any number of times. Cell a holds the fewest coins that make amount a: 1 plus the smallest cell a minus c, over every coin c that fits. The sweep goes upward, from 0.

@1
Base case: cell 0 is 0. No coins make zero.

@2-3
Cell 1 is 1, one coin of 1. Cell 2 is 1 plus cell 1, which is 2. Look at what that read means: cell 1 already contains a 1 coin, and cell 2 adds another. Because the sweep runs upward, a coin can be reused, and that is exactly what unbounded means.

@4
Cell 3: coin 3 on top of cell 0 gives 1, beating coin 1 on top of cell 2. A single 3 coin.

@5
Cell 4 reads cells 3, 1 and 0. The 4 coin on top of nothing wins: 1.

@6
Cell 5 reads cells 4, 2 and 1, holding 1, 2 and 1. The best is 1, so cell 5 is 2.

@7
The answer cell, 6. Coin 1 reads cell 5, which is 2. Coin 3 reads cell 3, which is 1. Coin 4 reads cell 2, which is 2. The minimum is through cell 3, so cell 6 is 2. Cell 3 itself is a 3 coin, so the same coin is used twice.

@8
Two coins: 3 plus 3, not the greedy 4 plus 1 plus 1. This is the one rule worth remembering from the knapsack family. Every read here lands on a smaller amount that is already final and may already contain the same coin, so coins repeat: unlimited copies. In the one-row 0/1 knapsack the sweep runs downward, so each read sees the row from before the current item, and each item is used at most once. Same three lines of code, opposite direction, and the wrong direction gives silently wrong answers that small tests often miss.
