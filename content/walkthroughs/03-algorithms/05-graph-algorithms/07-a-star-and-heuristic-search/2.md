@0
A five by five grid with seven wall cells. The start is the top-left corner, and the goal is the bottom-right corner. Every move costs 1, so breadth-first search finds the shortest path. Keep a number in mind for comparison: A star, guided by the Manhattan distance to the goal, expanded 9 cells on this grid.

@1-3
Breadth-first search uses only the distance from the start. It expands the start at distance 0, then both of its neighbours at distance 1: one step down the left column, and one step along the top row.

@4-7
At distances 2 and 3, the search grows in two directions at once. One front goes down the left column and turns right along row 2. The other runs along the top row.

@8-11
At distances 4 and 5, the first front turns down through the only gap in row 3, at column 2. The second reaches the top-right corner and starts down the right-hand column.

@12-16
At distances 6 and 7, the first front reaches the bottom row and spreads both ways along it: right towards the goal, and left towards the bottom-left corner, which leads nowhere. The second keeps descending the right column. Every cell at distance 7 is expanded before anything at distance 8.

@17
The goal is reached at distance 8, and the path is highlighted: down the left column, along row 2, down through the gap and right along the bottom row. Count the work: breadth-first search settled all 16 open cells closer than the goal before reaching it, while A star expanded 9. Along A star's route, every cell had the same f of 8, distance so far plus estimate remaining, so it walked one corridor and left the rest in the queue. Breadth-first search, like Dijkstra, has no estimate of the distance to the goal, so it expands everything nearer than the goal, in every direction.
