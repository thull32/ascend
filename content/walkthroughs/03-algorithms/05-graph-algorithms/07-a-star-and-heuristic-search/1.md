@0
Six nodes, and we want the cheapest route from A to F, the goal marked on the right. Each label shows h, an estimate of the remaining distance to F, here a scaled straight-line distance: 9 from A, 7 from B and C, 4 from D and E, and 0 at F itself. A star is Dijkstra with one change. The heap is ordered by f, which is g, the distance so far, plus h, the estimate of what remains.

@1-3
A is expanded first, with g of 0 and h of 9, so f is 9. Its edges to B and C both weigh 4, so each gets g of 4, and with h of 7 each has f of 11.

@4-6
B and C tie at 11, and B is expanded first. B to D gives D a g of 8, and with h of 4 its f is 12. B to E gives E a g of 9, and f of 13.

@7
C is expanded next, at 11. Nothing changes: going through C, D would have a g of 8 again, no better than before. The open list holds D at 12 and E at 13.

@8-9
D is the smallest, at 12. Its edge to F, weight 5, gives F a g of 13, and since h at the goal is 0, F's f is 13. F has been reached, but it is only pushed, not popped, and a pushed entry carries only a tentative distance.

@10
E and F now tie at 13. The tie is broken towards the larger g, as the lesson recommends: F's g is 13 and E's is 9, so F is popped first, with cost 13, by the path A, B, D, F, after expanding four nodes. E was never expanded. Popped, not pushed, is what makes the answer final. Why is it optimal? The heuristic is admissible: it never overestimates the true remaining cost. So any cheaper path would have an unexpanded node on it with a smaller f than the goal's, and that node would have been popped first. E's f of 13 already says that no path through E can cost less than 13, so it cannot beat the goal. Dijkstra, by contrast, would have settled every node closer than 13, E included. On a graph this small that is one node saved; on bigger graphs the search expands a narrow corridor towards the goal instead of a circle around the start.
