@0
Six nodes, A to F, joined by undirected weighted edges, and we want the cheapest set of edges that connects them all. Prim grows one tree, starting here from A. The rule comes from the cut property: the lightest edge crossing between the tree and everything else always belongs to some minimum spanning tree. So, every step, add the cheapest edge leaving the tree.

@1-2
The tree is just A. Two edges leave it: A to B at 4, and A to C at 2. The cheapest is A to C, so C joins, and the tree weighs 2.

@3-4
Now the tree is A and C, and four edges cross the cut: A to B at 4, B to C at 5, C to D at 8, and C to E at 3. The cheapest is C to E, so E joins, and the tree weighs 5.

@5-6
With A, C and E inside, the crossing edges are A to B at 4, B to C at 5, C to D at 8, E to D at 6, and E to F at 9. The lightest is A to B. B joins, and the tree weighs 9. Notice that B was reachable two ways, at 4 from A and at 5 from C, and the cheaper one won.

@7-8
B to C no longer crosses the cut, because both ends are in the tree. The candidates for D are C to D at 8, E to D at 6 and B to D at 10, plus E to F at 9. E to D at 6 is cheapest, and the tree weighs 15.

@9-10
Only F is left, reachable by E to F at 9 or D to F at 7. D to F wins, and the tree weighs 22.

@11
Five edges for six nodes: A to C, C to E, A to B, E to D and D to F, total weight 22. With a heap, Prim runs in order of E log V, and the code is Dijkstra with one change to the key: the heap holds the edge weight, the distance from the tree, instead of the distance from the source. One trap to remember: this is not a shortest-path tree. The tree's route from A to D costs 11, through C and E, while the shortest path, A to C to D, costs 10.
