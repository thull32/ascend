@0
Four nodes, A, B, C and D, and the matrix holds the distance from every node to every other: row is the source, column is the target. At the start it holds only the edges. A reaches B at 3 and C at 7, B reaches C at 1, C reaches D at 2, D reaches A at 6, and everything else is infinity. Floyd-Warshall runs in stages. At stage k, every pair asks one question: is going through k cheaper than what I have?

@1
Stage one allows A as an intermediate. Only D benefits, because D to A is the only edge into A. D reaches B at 6 plus 3, which is 9, and C at 6 plus 7, which is 13. Two pairs improved.

@2
Stage two allows B. A to C drops from the direct 7 to 3 plus 1, which is 4. And D to C improves from 13 to 10, going through B, using the D to B distance of 9 that the previous stage just computed.

@3
Stage three allows C. A and B finally reach D: A at 4 plus 2, which is 6, using the improved A to C, and B at 1 plus 2, which is 3. That reuse is legal because C's row and column were finished with respect to A and B before this stage began.

@4
Stage four allows D, and the cycle closes. B reaches A at 3 plus 6, which is 9. C reaches A at 2 plus 6, which is 8, and C reaches B at 11, through D and A. Three pairs improved.

@5
Every entry is now finite: nine were at the start, sixteen at the end. That is all-pairs shortest paths in order of V cubed time and V squared memory, with no data structure at all. The trap is the loop order. The intermediate node k must be the outer loop, because it is the stage of the dynamic program, and every stage must finish before the next begins. Put it inside and you compute shortest paths with at most one intermediate. And a negative value on the diagonal after the loops means that node lies on a negative cycle.
