@0
Five nodes in an undirected graph: a triangle of A, B and C, and a tail running from B to D to E. A bridge is an edge whose removal splits the graph. The test comes from a depth-first search with low-link values: the edge from a parent to its child is a bridge when the child's low-link is greater than the parent's discovery time, meaning the child's subtree cannot climb back to the parent, or above it, without that edge.

@1-3
The search starts at A, discovery time 0, then goes to B, time 1, and on to C, time 2. Each label shows discovery time, then low-link, and each node's low-link starts equal to its discovery time.

@4
As D is discovered at time 3, look at the triangle. C's edge back to A climbed to discovery time 0, so C's low-link is 0, and when the search returned to B, B inherited that 0. Because C's low of 0 is not greater than B's discovery time of 1, the edge from B to C is not a bridge: C can reach above B without it.

@5
D leads to E, discovered at time 4. E's only edge is the one it arrived by, which the search skips, so E can climb nowhere at all.

@6
Back in D: E's low-link of 4 is greater than D's discovery time of 3. Nothing below E reaches D or above except through that edge, so D to E is a bridge.

@7
Back in B: D's low-link is still 3, greater than B's discovery time of 1. So B to D is a bridge too.

@8
Back at A, B's low-link of 0 is not greater than A's discovery time of 0, so A to B survives. Two bridges, found for every edge at once in a single pass, order of V plus E, instead of removing each edge and re-running a search. The same pass finds articulation points by changing greater-than to greater-than-or-equal: a back edge that reaches the parent itself saves the edge, but not the node, because removing the node removes that back edge's endpoint too. Here D and B are both articulation points. One implementation trap: skip the edge you arrived by using its index, not its endpoint, or parallel edges will be reported as bridges.
