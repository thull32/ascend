@0
Six numbers, unsorted: 5, 2, 4, 6, 1 and 3. Insertion sort grows a sorted prefix on the left, one element at a time. Hold onto the invariant: after we handle position i, everything from position 0 to i is sorted. Each new element walks left past every larger neighbour until it sits behind something smaller or equal.

@1-3
We take the element at position 1, which is 2. The 5 to its left is larger, so 5 shifts one slot right. There is nothing further left, so 2 drops into position 0. The first two positions now read 2, 5, and they are sorted.

@4-6
Next is 4, at position 2. The 5 is larger and shifts right; the 2 is smaller, so 4 stops and lands at position 1. The prefix reads 2, 4, 5.

@7-8
Now 6. Its left neighbour, 5, is already smaller, so nothing moves at all: one comparison and 6 stays where it is. This is the case that makes insertion sort fast on nearly sorted data.

@9
Then comes the expensive one. Position 4 holds 1, which is smaller than everything in the prefix.

@10-13
So every element of the prefix has to shift. 6 moves right, then 5, then 4, then 2: four shifts, one for each larger element sitting in front of 1.

@14
1 lands at position 0. The first five positions read 1, 2, 4, 5, 6.

@15-19
Last, 3. The 6, the 5 and the 4 each shift right, the 2 is smaller, and 3 settles at position 2. The whole array reads 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6.

@20
Sorted, with nine shifts in all. That count is not a coincidence: each shift removes exactly one inversion, a pair of elements in the wrong order. A random array has about n squared over four inversions, so the worst and average cases are n squared. But on nearly sorted input there are few inversions and the sort is close to linear, which is why production sorts, Timsort among them, hand small runs to insertion sort.
