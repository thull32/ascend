@0
Seven numbers: 38, 27, 43, 3, 9, 82 and 10. Merge sort never looks at the values while it splits. It cuts the range in half, sorts each half the same way, and then merges two sorted halves by repeatedly taking the smaller of their two front elements.

@1-3
The first cut splits positions 0 to 6 into 0 to 3 and 4 to 6. The left part is cut again, into 0 to 1 and 2 to 3, and then 0 to 1 into two single elements. A single element is already sorted, so the recursion stops there.

@4-5
Now the first merge: 38 against 27. 27 is smaller, so it goes to position 0, then 38 follows into position 1.

@6-8
The same for positions 2 and 3: split into 43 and 3, then merge. 3 comes first, then 43.

@9-12
Now two sorted pairs are merged: 27, 38 on the left and 3, 43 on the right. The fronts are 27 and 3, so 3 goes first. Then 27, then 38, and once the left half is used up, 43 is copied without a comparison. Positions 0 to 3 read 3, 27, 38, 43.

@13-19
The right side gets the same treatment. Positions 4 to 6 split into 4 to 5 and a lone 10. 9 and 82 merge as 9, 82, and then that pair merges with 10: 9, then 10, then 82.

@20-26
The final merge brings together 3, 27, 38, 43 and 9, 10, 82. 3 is the smaller front, then 9 and 10 from the right half, then 27, 38 and 43 from the left, and finally 82 is copied in. Six comparisons to merge seven elements; a merge never needs more than one fewer comparison than the number of elements it outputs.

@27
Sorted. There are about log n levels of splitting, and each level does linear merge work, so merge sort is n log n, and it is n log n on every input, sorted, reversed or random, because the split never depends on the values. The price is a scratch buffer of linear size for the merge. And because a tie takes from the left half first, merge sort is stable.
