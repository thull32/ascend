@0
A stream of eight numbers: 3, 2, 1, 5, 6, 4, 9, 7. We want the three largest, and we will see each number once. The tool is a min-heap that holds at most three. It sounds backwards, but hold onto this: the root is the smallest of the best three seen so far, so it is the only one a newcomer has to beat.

@1-3
The first three numbers just go in. 3 goes in as the root. 2 is appended next, and because it is smaller than its parent 3, it sifts up to the root.

@4-5
1 is appended and sifts up past 2. The heap is full, holding 1, 3 and 2, with 1 at the root: the bar to get in is now 1.

@6-8
5 arrives. 5 beats the root 1, so 1 leaves the top three. 5 overwrites the root and sifts down, swapping with its smaller child 2. The root is now 2.

@9-11
6 beats the root 2. 2 leaves, 6 takes the root and sinks below its smaller child, 3. The bar is now 3.

@12-13
4 beats 3. 4 takes the root and is already smaller than both its children, 6 and 5, so it stays.

@14-16
9 beats 4. 9 sinks below 5, which becomes the root.

@17-19
Last, 7 beats 5. 7 sinks below 6, and 6 is the root.

@20
The heap holds the three largest: 9, 7 and 6, and the root, 6, is the third largest. Each element costs at most log k, so the pass is n log k, with memory for just k numbers. That is why this is the answer for streams, where you cannot hold the whole input. Any newcomer at or below the root is rejected with a single comparison and no heap work at all. When k is a large fraction of n, quickselect's linear expected time wins instead.
