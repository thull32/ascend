@0
Seven sorted numbers: 1, 2, 2, 2, 3, 5 and 8. We want the first 2, not just any 2. Ask one yes-no question of every position: is the value at least 2? Because the array is sorted, the answers run false, then true, and never flip back. The answer is the first true. The range runs from low at position 0 up to high at 7, and high is exclusive: it points just past the end. The invariant is that everything below low is false, and everything at or above high is true.

@1
The middle is position 3, which holds 2. That is true, but it might not be the first true, so we do not stop. High moves to position 3, keeping 3 reachable as the eventual answer.

@2
Now low is 0 and high is 3, so the middle is position 1, which holds 2. True again, so high moves to 1.

@3
Low is 0 and high is 1, so the middle is position 0, which holds 1. One is less than 2, so the predicate is false, and the answer must be after it: low moves to 1.

@4
Low and high both sit at position 1, so the range is empty and low is the answer: the first 2 is at position 1. Notice that the search found a 2 at its very first probe and kept going; that is the point. With this one template, called first true, the variants are one-liners: the first occurrence, the insertion point, the last occurrence by asking for the first value greater than the target and stepping back one, and the first bad version. It is exactly what Python's bisect left does.
