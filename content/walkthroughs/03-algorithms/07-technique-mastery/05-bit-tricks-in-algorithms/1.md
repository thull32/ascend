@0
Four items: 2, 3, 5 and 7. A four-bit integer names a subset of them: bit i set means item i is in. Bit 0 is the rightmost column, so it belongs to 2, and bit 3, on the left, belongs to 7. Counting from 0 to 15 visits every subset exactly once.

@1-4
Mask 0 has no bits set: the empty subset. Mask 1 sets bit 0, so the subset is just 2. Mask 2 sets bit 1, just 3. Mask 3 sets both, giving 2 and 3. The lowest bit flips on every step, like the units digit of an ordinary counter.

@5-8
Mask 4 sets bit 2, and the subset is just 5. Then 5 pairs with 2, with 3, and finally with both: mask 7, bits 0, 1 and 2, is 2, 3 and 5.

@9-12
Mask 8 sets only the top bit, so the subset is just 7. The next three masks repeat the pattern of the first four, with 7 added to each: 2 and 7, 3 and 7, then 2, 3 and 7 for mask 11.

@13-16
Mask 12 is 5 and 7. Then 2, 5 and 7, then 3, 5 and 7, and finally mask 15, every bit set, the full set.

@17
All 16 subsets in order of 2 to the n. Every set operation is now one or two machine instructions: membership is a shift and an AND, union is OR, intersection is AND. And numeric order is more than convenient. Adding an element to a mask always makes a larger integer, so every subset comes before all of its supersets. That makes plain counting a valid order for dynamic programming over subsets, such as Held-Karp's shortest tour, with no other ordering logic.
