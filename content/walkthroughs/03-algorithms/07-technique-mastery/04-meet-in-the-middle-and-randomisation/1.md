@0
Meet in the middle splits the input into two halves and enumerates every subset of each half. Here is one half: three items, 5, minus 7 and 3. A three-bit mask names one subset. Bit i set means item i is in, and bit 0 is the rightmost column, which is why the items read 3, minus 7, 5 from left to right above the bits. Counting the masks from 0 to 7 visits all eight subsets.

@1
Mask 0, all bits clear: the empty subset.

@2-3
Mask 1 sets bit 0, so the subset is just 5. Mask 2 sets bit 1, so the subset is just minus 7.

@4
Mask 3 sets bits 0 and 1: the subset holds 5 and minus 7.

@5-7
Mask 4 sets only bit 2, so the subset is just 3. Mask 5 adds bit 0, giving 5 and 3. Mask 6 is bits 1 and 2, giving minus 7 and 3.

@8
Mask 7 sets every bit: the full set, 5, minus 7 and 3.

@9
All eight subsets, in order of 2 to the n. Masks in increasing order also have a useful property: every proper subset of a mask is numerically smaller, so this order is valid for subset dynamic programming. For meet in the middle, record each mask's sum, and that is this half's list. The payoff is in the sizes. With 40 items, each half of 20 has about a million masks, instead of the roughly one trillion subsets of the whole. Sort one half's sums, sweep the other half against it with two pointers, and every subset of the full input is covered as one row from each half.
