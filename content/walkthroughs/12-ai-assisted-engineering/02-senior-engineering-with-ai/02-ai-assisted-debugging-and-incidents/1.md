@0
Mid-incident, an AI assistant proposes a fix. Treat it the way the lesson treats every model output: as a hypothesis, this time in code form. On screen are users, a load balancer, nine stable instances, one canary slot, and a metrics service that compares the two versions on the same traffic. The rule: a remediation takes the normal path, through the same canary as any release.

@1
The proposed fix, version 2, is deployed to one instance. No traffic yet; health checks pass.

@2-3
A tenth of the traffic goes to it, 2 requests in 20. No errors, and a 99th percentile of 120 milliseconds against stable's 118. The gate passes, and the share widens. Notice that passing at a tenth proves little; it is a prediction that has not yet been refuted.

@4-5
At half the traffic, the canary meets a path the small slice never exercised. 4 of its 10 requests fail and its 99th percentile jumps to 410 milliseconds, while stable, on the same window, stays clean. The hypothesis has been refuted, by data rather than by anyone's confidence.

@6
The canary rolls back automatically: version 2's weight goes to zero. The wrong fix touched only the canary's share of traffic for one analysis window, instead of becoming a second incident on top of the first. Nobody had to notice it first.

@7-8
A corrected version, 2.1, starts the whole gate again from a tenth, and stays clean at half: 121 milliseconds against 118.

@9
Only now is it promoted to every instance.

@10
The canary's own limits still apply under pressure: too few requests per window to see a small error rate, a slice that only gets the easy traffic, and sessions that need sticky routing.

@11
During an incident it is tempting to push the fix straight to every instance. This is the answer to that temptation. The fix is a hypothesis; the canary is the experiment that can refute it; and automatic rollback means a wrong answer costs one window on a slice of traffic, not every user.
