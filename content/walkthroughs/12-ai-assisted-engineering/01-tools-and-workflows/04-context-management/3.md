@0
A retrieval pipeline, drawn over policy documents rather than code: a query, an embedding step, a vector index, the top two chunks, prompt assembly, the model, and an answer with citations. The query is: what is the refund window? Instead of hoping the model memorised your documents, you fetch the relevant passages at query time and put them in the prompt. Replace the documents with chunks of a codebase, and the question with where do we compute refunds, and this is an embedding index.

@1
The query is embedded with the same model that embedded the documents, giving a vector of numbers, about 1,500 of them in practice. It has to be the same model on both sides, or the two spaces do not line up.

@2
The index is searched for the nearest chunks, out of 12,400, each about 300 tokens. By cosine similarity: chunk 3 of the policy document scores 0.91, the first returns chunk 0.63, a warranty chunk 0.55, a shipping chunk 0.42.

@3
Keep the top two. The policy chunk says refunds are accepted within 30 days of delivery. The returns chunk is about the return address and process. Everything below the cut is gone: if the right chunk had scored third, the model would never see it.

@4
The prompt is assembled: instructions, the two chunks with their ids, and the question, about 227 tokens in all.

@5-6
The model answers from what it was given: refunds are accepted within 30 days of delivery, citing the policy chunk. That is the strength and the trap in one. Most failures of this kind are retrieval failures, so measure whether the right chunk reaches the top, before you tune the prompt. And an index built before today's edits returns yesterday's code. That is why terminal agents lean on searching the current files with grep instead.
