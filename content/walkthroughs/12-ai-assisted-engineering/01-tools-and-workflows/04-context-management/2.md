@0
Two requests, the second one extending the first, which is how every agent call after the first looks. Request 1 is three tokens, The cat sat. The expensive part of any model call is prefill: one pass over the prompt that computes a key and a value for every token, in every layer, and stores them. Three tokens, three pairs. The last position predicts the first output token, on.

@1
Decode, step 1. Only on runs through the layers; its pair is appended, four cached, and it attends over all of them to emit the. Inside one request, this is the cache that saves recomputing earlier tokens.

@2
Step 2 does the same for the, five cached, and emits mat.

@3
Request 1 ends with the reply, on the mat. Its working cache would normally be freed now. With prompt caching, the provider keeps the prompt's three pairs as a cache entry for that exact prefix, for about five minutes.

@4
Request 2 resends everything: the three-token prompt, the reply and the new message, nine tokens. The first three are byte-identical to the cached prefix, so the lookup hits and those three pairs are read from the cache instead of computed.

@5
Prefill runs only over the six new tokens: six pairs computed instead of nine. Less prefill means a shorter wait for the first token, as well as a smaller bill.

@6
Here the saving is 3 of 9 tokens. In a coding session the prefix is the system prompt, the tools, the memory file and every earlier turn, tens of thousands of tokens, so most of each call is a cache read, billed at about a tenth of the input rate on most models, with only the new tail written at 1.25 times. That is why the stable part of a prompt must come first, and why changing one byte early in the prefix makes everything after it miss.
