@0
Users, a load balancer, nine instances of the stable version, one slot for a canary, and a metrics service that compares them. Some bugs only appear under real traffic: a data shape nobody had in a fixture, a load pattern, a dependency's odd behaviour. Every earlier layer of verification has passed. This last layer cannot prevent those bugs; it limits how many users meet them.

@1
Version 2 is deployed to one instance beside the nine stable ones. It gets no traffic yet, and its health checks pass.

@2-3
Stage 1: a tenth of the traffic goes to the canary, 2 of the next 20 requests, and the rest stays on stable. The metrics service compares the two versions on the same window. Stable: no errors, a 99th percentile of 118 milliseconds. Canary: no errors, 120 milliseconds. Within tolerance, so the share widens.

@4-5
Stage 2: half the traffic. Now the canary sees enough requests to hit a code path that a tenth of the traffic never exercised. 4 of its 10 requests fail, and its 99th percentile jumps to 410 milliseconds, while stable, serving the other half of the same traffic, stays clean at 118. The gate fails. Because both versions are measured on the same window, the difference can only be the release.

@6
Automatic rollback. The load balancer's weight for version 2 goes to zero, and stable takes everything again. The bad change reached only the canary's share of traffic, half of it, for one analysis window, instead of everyone for as long as it took a human to notice.

@7-8
The fixed version, 2.1, starts again from a tenth. Nothing is trusted because it passed last time; every promotion repeats the same gate. At a tenth it is clean, and at half it is clean too: 121 milliseconds against 118. Comparing like with like means a traffic spike that slows both versions does not fail the canary.

@9
Promotion. The remaining instances roll to 2.1, and it becomes the new stable version.

@10
Three ways a canary lies. Two canary requests per window, at the first stage, are far too few to see a 1 percent error rate, so the gate needs a minimum sample, not just a rate. A canary that only gets the easy traffic, cache hits or one region, passes and then fails at full load. And stateful sessions need sticky routing, or one user bounces between versions.

@11
So the canary bounds the blast radius of a bad release to its share of traffic, and catches what only real traffic reveals, at the cost of slower rollouts and two versions live at once. For AI-written code that passed every test, it is the layer that turns an unknown bug into a short, partial, automatically reversed one.
