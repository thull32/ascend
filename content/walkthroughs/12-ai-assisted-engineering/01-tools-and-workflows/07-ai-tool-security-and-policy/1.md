@0
A retrieval pipeline: a query, an embedding step, a vector index, the top two chunks, prompt assembly, the model, and an answer. The query is harmless: what is the refund window? Nothing malicious happens on this screen. Watch instead for the moment text from a document enters the prompt, because that moment is the whole attack surface.

@1-2
The query is embedded and the index is searched. Out of 12,400 chunks, the closest are a policy chunk at 0.91 and a returns chunk at 0.63, ahead of warranty and shipping.

@3
The top two are kept. Note who chose them: a similarity score, not a person. Whatever those chunks contain is about to be read by the model.

@4
Here is the moment. The prompt is assembled, the system instruction, then the two retrieved chunks, then the question, all in one stream of tokens. The model sees no wall between your instructions and the documents. If a chunk said ignore the above and do something else, it would sit right there beside the real instructions. A system prompt saying the chunks are data, not instructions, reduces the risk. It does not remove it.

@5-6
This time the documents were honest, and the answer is correct: refunds are accepted within 30 days of delivery, with a citation. Now swap the documents for what a coding agent reads: a README, a code comment, a dependency's docs, an issue, a fetched web page. Any of them can carry instructions, and the model cannot reliably keep them as data. So the defence that holds is outside the model: permissions and sandboxing that bound what the agent can do when the instruction gets through.
