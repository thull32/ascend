@0
Seven sorted numbers: 1, 3, 4, 6, 8, 11 and 14, and the lesson's closest pair sum, with target 15. Low starts at the left end and high at the right, six positions apart. Each iteration adds the two, remembers the sum if it is the closest yet, and moves exactly one pointer inward: low up if the sum is too small, otherwise high down.

@1
Iteration 1: 1 plus 14 is 15, a perfect hit, so best becomes 15. The sum is not below the target, so high moves down. The loop does not stop on a hit; it only stops when the pointers meet.

@2-3
Iteration 2: 1 plus 11 is 12, too small, so low moves up. Iteration 3: 3 plus 11 is 14, still too small, so low moves up again. Neither beats the best.

@4
Iteration 4: 4 plus 11 is 15 again, no closer than the best, and since it is not below the target, high moves down.

@5-6
Iterations 5 and 6: 4 plus 8 is 12, then 6 plus 8 is 14. Both are too small, so low moves up twice, and now low and high point at the same position, 4.

@7
Low is no longer below high, so the loop stops: six iterations for seven elements, exactly the six rows of the lesson's table, and the closest sum is 15. That is the bound in action. The pointers start n minus 1 apart, and every iteration closes the gap by exactly one, so the loop runs at most n minus 1 times, whatever the data. That argument, that every iteration makes progress on a bounded quantity, is the standard way to bound a loop whose counter is not obvious. Put it after a sort, and the whole function is n log n.
