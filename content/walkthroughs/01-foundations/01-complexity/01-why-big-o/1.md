@0
Six numbers: 5, 3, 8, 1, 9 and 2. Bubble sort walks along them comparing neighbours and swapping any pair that is out of order, so the largest value still unsorted bubbles to the end. It is the same nested loop shape as the pairs version of the duplicate check, and the thing to watch is not the swaps but how many comparisons it takes.

@1-5
The first pass makes five comparisons. 5 and 3 swap, 5 and 8 are fine, 8 and 1 swap, 8 and 9 are fine, and 9 and 2 swap. Nine has bubbled all the way to the last position, where it stays.

@6-9
The second pass only has to cover the first five positions, so it makes four comparisons. 5 and 1 swap, then 8 and 2 swap, and 8 settles next to 9.

@10-12
The third pass makes three comparisons. 3 and 1 swap, 3 and 5 are fine, 5 and 2 swap, and 5 takes its place.

@13-15
The fourth pass makes two comparisons, swapping 3 and 2, and the fifth makes just one: 1 and 2, already in order.

@16
Sorted. Count the comparisons: five, then four, three, two, one. That is 15, which is six times five over two, exactly the triangular sum the duplicate check produced. Double the input and that count roughly quadruples, because it grows with n squared, not n. No constant in front of a linear algorithm can hide that gap once the list gets long.
