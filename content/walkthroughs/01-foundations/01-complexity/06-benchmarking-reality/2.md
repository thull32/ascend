@0
The same six values, 9, 4, 7, 1, 8 and 2, now with merge sort. It splits the range in halves recursively, then merges sorted halves back together with two pointers. Asymptotically it does less work. The question here is what each unit of that work costs.

@1-3
First the splits. Positions 0 to 5 split into 0 to 2 and 3 to 5. Then 0 to 2 splits into the lone 9 and positions 1 to 2, and 1 to 2 splits into two single elements. Every split is a recursive call.

@4-5
The first merge writes 4, then 7, into positions 1 and 2.

@6-8
Then the 9 merges with 4, 7, writing 4, 7, 9 into positions 0 to 2. Notice that 4 and 7 were just written, and now they are written again: each merge goes through a temporary copy.

@9-15
The right half goes the same way: split down, merge 8 and 2 into 2, 8, then merge the lone 1 with 2, 8 to get 1, 2, 8.

@16-21
The final merge takes the smaller front each time: 1 and 2 from the right half, 4 and 7 from the left, 8 from the right, and 9 last.

@22
Sorted, the same 1, 2, 4, 7, 8, 9. Merge sort is order n log n always, it is stable, and it needs order n scratch space. But count what it did here: 16 writes, about as many as insertion sort's ten shifts and five inserts, plus a recursive call for every split and a temporary array for every merge. At this size that overhead costs more than the comparisons it saves. In CPython the crossover sits between 64 and 128 elements, and that is why production sorts switch to insertion sort for small ranges: at 16 elements in libstdc++, and at 12 or fewer in Go. Which algorithm wins depends on what one unit of work costs, and only a measurement tells you that.
