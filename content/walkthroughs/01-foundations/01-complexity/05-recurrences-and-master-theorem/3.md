@0
Four discs on peg A, to be moved to peg C, never putting a larger disc on a smaller one. The recursive rule has three steps. To move n discs, first move the n minus 1 smaller ones out of the way onto the spare peg, then move the biggest disc, then move the n minus 1 back on top of it.

@1-3
So moving 4 discs to C starts by moving 3 discs to B, which starts by moving 2 discs to C, which starts by moving 1 disc to B. Each call hands a smaller problem down, one disc smaller each time.

@4-7
A single disc just moves. Disc 1 goes from A to B, which is move 1. Disc 2 is now free and goes from A to C, move 2. Disc 1 follows it from B to C, move 3. Two discs took 3 moves.

@8-13
Now disc 3 is free, and it moves from A to B, move 4. Then the same two-disc trick brings discs 1 and 2 from C onto it, in moves 5, 6 and 7.

@14-15
Three discs are on B, after 7 moves. Disc 4, the biggest, is finally free, and it goes from A to C. That is move 8, the only time it moves.

@16-28
What is left is the whole three-disc job again, this time from B to C, using A as the spare. It takes exactly 7 more moves, 9 through 15, and it looks just like the first half with the pegs relabelled.

@29-30
All four discs are on C after 15 moves, which is 2 to the 4, minus 1. Look at the shape of the tree. Each call makes two calls, but the problem only shrinks by one disc, so the tree doubles in width at every level and is n levels deep. That is the recurrence T of n equals 2 T of n minus 1, plus 1, which is exponential: order 2 to the n. The stack, though, only ever holds one path, so the space is order n.
