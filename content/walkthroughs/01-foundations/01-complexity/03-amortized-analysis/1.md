@0
A new dynamic array. On the stack sits a small header: the length, the capacity, and a pointer. The elements themselves live in a block on the heap, which has room for 2. Watch the copy counter, because the whole argument is about how big it gets.

@1-2
Appending 1 and then 2 is cheap. The length is below the capacity, so each value is written straight into the next free slot, in constant time. After the second append the block is full.

@3
Now we take a pointer, p, to the first element. It is an alias into the current block, and it stays valid only until the next growth moves the elements somewhere else.

@4
Appending 3 finds no room: length and capacity are both 2. So the array allocates a new block on the heap, twice the size, with room for 4. The old block is untouched for now.

@5-6
Every existing element is copied across, first 1 and then 2. This is the expensive append, the one that costs order n.

@7
The header now points at the new block, and the old block is freed. But p still holds the old address. It is dangling, and reading through it is undefined behaviour. That is the practical shadow of growth: the copy happens at a moment you did not choose, and anything pointing into the old block is left behind.

@8-9
With spare room again, 3 and 4 are written in place, one constant-time step each. The block of 4 is full.

@10-14
Appending 5 triggers the next growth. A block of 8 is allocated, and all four elements are copied across, one by one.

@15
The header switches to the block of 8 and the block of 4 is freed.

@16-18
Then 5, 6 and 7 each drop into a spare slot in constant time, leaving one slot free.

@19
Seven appends, and the total copying was 6 elements: 2 at the first growth and 4 at the second. Because capacity doubles, the copies always add up to less than n, so append is constant time amortised, even though a single append can cost order n. Amortised is a promise about the whole sequence, not about latency: the append that triggers the copy really is slow, and on a big hash table that pause is tens of milliseconds.
