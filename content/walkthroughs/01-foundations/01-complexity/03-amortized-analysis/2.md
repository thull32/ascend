@0
Two stacks that together behave like a queue. Enqueue pushes onto the in stack. Dequeue pops from the out stack, and only when out is empty does it refill it by draining in. Draining reverses the order, which turns last in, first out back into first in, first out.

@1-3
Enqueue 1, 2 and 3. Each is a single push onto in, and out is not touched.

@4-6
Now the first dequeue, and out is empty, so the transfer runs. Pop 3 from in and push it onto out, then 2, then 1. Three moves, and when they finish the oldest item, 1, is on top of out.

@7
Dequeue returns 1, straight off the top of out. That dequeue was the expensive one, costing order n moves.

@8
Enqueue 4 goes onto in as usual. Out still holds 2 and 3, and it is left alone.

@9-10
The next two dequeues return 2 and then 3. Both are free: out was not empty, so no transfer was needed.

@11
The last dequeue finds out empty again, so the one item on in, 4, moves across.

@12
And dequeue returns 4. The order out was 1, 2, 3, 4, exactly the order they went in.

@13
Count the moves: 4 across 8 operations. Each element crosses from in to out at most once in its lifetime, so the moves can never add up to more than a constant per operation. That is amortised constant time, even though a single dequeue can cost order n. It is the same argument as the doubling array: the expensive step pays for itself because it can only happen after enough cheap ones.
