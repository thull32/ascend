@0
Fast exponentiation computes x to the 13th not with 13 multiplications but by reading the exponent's bits. The loop needs one operation to walk through those bits: the shift, which moves every bit sideways. Shifting left fills with zeros on the right, and anything pushed off the left end of the 8-bit word is lost.

@1
The exponent is 13, which in binary is 1 1 0 1, since 13 is 8 plus 4 plus 1. Those three set bits are why x to the 13th is x to the 8th, times x to the 4th, times x.

@2-3
Shifting left is the direction the loop does not use, but it shows what a shift means. One place left gives 1 1 0 1 0, which is 26, twice 13. Two places gives 52, four times 13. Each left shift doubles.

@4
The loop shifts the other way. Shift 13 right by one and the lowest bit, a 1, falls off the end, leaving 1 1 0, which is 6. That is floor division by 2. In the loop, that dropped bit is the one just consumed: it was 1, so the result was multiplied by x before the shift.

@5
Shifts cost constant time. Each right shift removes one bit of the exponent, so a four-bit exponent is used up after four shifts, and the loop runs about log n times. For 3 to the 13th that is four squarings and three multiplications, and the same skeleton with a remainder after each multiplication is modular exponentiation, the workhorse of RSA and Miller-Rabin.
