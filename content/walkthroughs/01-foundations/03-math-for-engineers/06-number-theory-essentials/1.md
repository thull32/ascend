@0
Fast exponentiation computes x to the 13th not with 13 multiplications but by reading the exponent's bits from the low end. The loop needs one operation to walk through those bits: the right shift, which moves every bit one place toward the low end. The lowest bit falls off, which is floor division by 2.

@1
The exponent is 13, which in four bits is 1 1 0 1, since 13 is 8 plus 4 plus 1. Those three set bits are why x to the 13th is x to the 8th, times x to the 4th, times x.

@2
First shift. The lowest bit, a 1, falls off the end, leaving 0 1 1 0, which is 6. In the loop, that dropped bit is the one just consumed: it was 1, so the result was multiplied by x before the shift.

@3
Second shift. 6 loses its low bit, a 0, and becomes 3. A 0 means no multiplication this time, only the squaring.

@4
Third shift. 3 loses a 1 and becomes 1.

@5
Fourth shift. The last 1 falls off, and the exponent is 0, so the loop stops. Four shifts emptied a four-bit number, one per bit, and the bits came out in the order 1, 0, 1, 1: the binary of 13 read from the low end.

@6
Shifts cost constant time. Each right shift removes one bit of the exponent, so the loop runs about log n times. For 3 to the 13th that is four squarings and three multiplications, and the same skeleton with a remainder after each multiplication is modular exponentiation, the workhorse of RSA and Miller-Rabin.
