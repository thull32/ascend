@0
Now choose exactly 2 of the four numbers 1, 2, 3 and 4. Combinations are the subsets with exactly k items, and there are 6 of them here. Two rules shape this tree. Pick items in increasing index order, so each combination appears once and never in a second order. And prune any branch that can no longer reach 2 items.

@1-5
At the root we need 2 more, and any of the four numbers can come first. Choose 1. Now we need 1 more, and the candidates are only the numbers after it: 2, 3 and 4. Each one completes a pair, so we record 1 and 2, 1 and 3, and 1 and 4. Combinations 1 to 3.

@6-10
Every pair starting with 1 is done. Back at the root, choose 2. The candidates for the second item are only 3 and 4, because 2 with 1 has already been counted as 1 with 2. That gives 2 and 3, and 2 and 4. Combinations 4 and 5.

@11-13
Choose 3. Only 4 comes after it, so we record 3 and 4, combination 6.

@14
Now the root would try 4 as the first item. But nothing comes after 4, and we still need one more. This branch can never fill up, so it is pruned: the call is never made, and nothing below it is explored.

@15-16
Done: 6 combinations, from 10 calls; the pruned branch was never one of them. That matches the formula for pairs: 4 times 3 ordered choices, divided by 2 because each pair was counted once in each order. Enumerating all 16 subsets of four items and keeping the ones of size 2 would have done more work for the same answer. Pruning the branches that cannot reach k is what makes combination enumeration cheaper than subset enumeration.
