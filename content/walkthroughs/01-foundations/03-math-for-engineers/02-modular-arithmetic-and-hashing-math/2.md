@0
We are searching the text abracadabra for the pattern abra. First we hash the pattern once, with base 31, and to make collisions easy to see this visualisation uses a tiny modulus, 101. The pattern's hash is 57. Every window of four characters in the text gets the same hash, but thanks to rolling, each one costs constant time instead of four steps.

@1-6
Window 0, the first four characters, hashes to 57. That equals the pattern's hash, so it is a candidate. A matching hash is not a matching string, so we verify character by character: a, b, r, a, all four agree. A genuine match at position 0.

@7
Now the roll. To get the next window's hash we do three things: subtract the outgoing a times 31 cubed, multiply by 31 to shift everything up one place, and add the incoming c. No character of the window is re-read.

@8-9
Window 1 is b, r, a, c, and its rolled hash is 57 again, so it is a candidate. But the very first character, b, differs from the pattern's a. This is a spurious hit, a hash collision, and verification throws it out. With a modulus of 101, two different strings collide about one time in a hundred. That is why the lesson wants a large prime modulus, and why a hash hit must always be checked.

@10-19
The rest of the middle is the cheap part. Roll in a, and the window hashes to 78: not 57, so skip it without looking at a single character. Roll again: 90, skip. Then 68, 39 and 19, each one skipped after one comparison of two numbers.

@20-26
The roll that drops d and adds a gives 57 again. Window 7 is a candidate. Verify: a, b, r, a. All four agree, a genuine match at position 7.

@27
Done: two matches, at positions 0 and 7, and one collision caught along the way. The expected cost is linear in the text plus the pattern, because every window costs constant time unless its hash collides. The two traps from the lesson: the subtraction can go negative in languages whose remainder keeps the sign, and skipping verification turns the algorithm into one that is occasionally wrong.
