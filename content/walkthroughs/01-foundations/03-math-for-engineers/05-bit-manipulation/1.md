@0
Two numbers written in binary at four bits: 12 is 1 1 0 0, and 10 is 1 0 1 0. Here is the one idea behind every bitwise operator: it combines the two bits in each column on its own, with no carries and no regard for any other column.

@1
First, AND. The result bit is 1 only when both bits are 1. We work from the rightmost column, the least significant, to the leftmost.

@2-5
Bit 0: zero and zero, so 0. Bit 1: zero and one, still 0. Bit 2: one and zero, 0 again. Bit 3: one and one, the only column where both are set, so 1.

@6
12 AND 10 is 1 0 0 0, which is 8. AND is a mask: it keeps only the bits set in both, which is how you pull a field out of a number.

@7-11
Next, OR. The result bit is 1 when either bit is 1. Bit 0: zero and zero, 0. Bit 1: zero and one, 1. Bit 2: one and zero, 1. Bit 3: one and one, 1.

@12
12 OR 10 is 1 1 1 0, which is 14. OR sets bits: it turns bits on without disturbing the others, which is how you combine flags.

@13-17
Last, XOR, exclusive or. The result bit is 1 when the two bits differ. Bit 0: both zero, so 0. Bit 1: they differ, 1. Bit 2: they differ, 1. Bit 3: both one, so 0.

@18
12 XOR 10 is 0 1 1 0, which is 6. XOR toggles: applying the same mask twice gives back the original, and XOR of any number with itself is 0. That cancelling property is the basis of a whole family of tricks later in the lesson.

@19
To sum up: 12 AND 10 is 8, OR is 14, XOR is 6. Each one is constant time, because the hardware evaluates every column at once.
