@0
A logarithm base 2 counts one thing: how many times you can halve n before you get down to 1. Here is that count at work. Eight sorted numbers: 2, 5, 8, 12, 16, 23, 38 and 56, and we are looking for 23. Low sits at position 0 and high at position 7, and the rule that drives everything is this: if 23 is present, it lies between low and high.

@1
Binary search looks at the middle. Between positions 0 and 7 the middle is position 3, which holds 12. Twelve is less than 23, and the array is sorted, so the target can only be to the right. Low moves to position 4. One comparison, and eight candidates have become four.

@2
The new middle is position 5, which holds 23. Found, after 2 comparisons. That was a lucky early hit. In the worst case, eight candidates become four, then two, then one: three halvings, because the log base 2 of 8 is 3, plus one last comparison to check the single candidate that remains. So at most 4 comparisons for 8 elements.

The step count is the log of the array length, not the length. Growing the input from a thousand items to a trillion, a billion-fold increase, moves the log from 10 to only 40. That is why an operation that halves its problem each step costs a few dozen steps whatever the input size.
