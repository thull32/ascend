@0
The interviewer's hint was a question: which earlier days can still be waiting when a new day arrives? The candidate's answer was a structural fact. If an older day and a newer day are both unanswered, the newer one cannot be warmer, or it would have answered the older one. So the waiting days, oldest to newest, never rise. This is that waiting set, kept on a stack of day numbers, for eight temperatures: 73, 74, 75, 71, 69, 72, 76 and 73.

@1-5
Day 0, 73, waits. Day 1, 74, is warmer, so it answers day 0 and takes its place on the stack. Day 2, 75, answers day 1 the same way and waits in turn. So far each new day answers exactly the one before it.

@6-7
Day 3 is 71 and day 4 is 69. Neither is warmer than anything waiting, so both join the stack. The waiting temperatures read 75, 71, 69: falling, exactly as the candidate derived.

@8-10
Day 5 is 72. Because the waiting set falls from oldest to newest, the days it answers are all at the newest end. It pops 69, then 71, both answered by 72. Then it meets 75, which is warmer, and stops, because everything older than 75 is at least as warm. Day 5 joins the stack. That is the candidate's check, step for step: 72 pops 69, pops 71, and 75 stays.

@11-13
Day 6 is 76, warmer than everything waiting. It pops 72, then 75. Day 2 waited four days for its answer. The stack empties, and day 6 waits.

@14-15
Day 7, 73, is colder, so it waits too. The input ends with days 6 and 7 still on the stack: no warmer day ever came. Each day was pushed once and popped at most once, linear in total. The candidate named the technique only after deriving it, and that is what the write-up remembers: given a nudge about the waiting set, they derived the monotone property and the stack unaided.
