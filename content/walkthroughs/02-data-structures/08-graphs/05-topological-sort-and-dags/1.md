@0
Six vertices, 0 to 5, and six directed edges, each pointing from something that must come first to something that depends on it: 5 to 2, 5 to 0, 4 to 0, 4 to 1, 2 to 3, and 3 to 1. We want an order where every edge points forward. Kahn's algorithm has one rule: a vertex with in-degree zero, no remaining prerequisites, is ready. Take it, and remove its outgoing edges. Vertices 4 and 5 have nothing pointing at them, so they start in the queue.

@1
Take 4 as position 1. Its edges lead to 0 and 1, which each still show an in-degree of 2.

@2-4
Take 5 as position 2. With 4's edges removed, 0 and 1 each drop to an in-degree of 1: 0 still waits on 5, and 1 on 3. Removing 5 then drops 2's in-degree to zero, and 0's as well: 0 was waiting on 4 and 5, and both are now gone. Both are enqueued.

@5-6
Take 2 as position 3. Its only successor, 3, now has no prerequisites left, so 3 is enqueued.

@7
Take 0 as position 4. It has no outgoing edges, so it unlocks nothing.

@8-9
Take 3 as position 5. That removes the last edge into 1, and 1 is enqueued.

@10
Take 1 as position 6. Every vertex has been emitted.

@11
The order is 4, 5, 2, 0, 3, 1, and every edge points forward in it. The cost is V plus E: each vertex is taken once, and each edge decrements one in-degree once. The queue is more than bookkeeping: it is the set of jobs ready to run, which is exactly what a parallel build scheduler hands to its workers. And the cycle check comes free: if the queue empties before every vertex is emitted, the leftover vertices are on a cycle or downstream of one.
