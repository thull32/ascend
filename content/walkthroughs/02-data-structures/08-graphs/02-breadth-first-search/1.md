@0
Eight vertices, numbered 0 to 7, and nine undirected edges. Breadth-first search from 0 runs on one invariant worth being able to prove: vertices leave the queue in non-decreasing order of distance from the start. Watch the queue as we go. It only ever holds a run of one distance followed by a run of the next.

@1-3
0 comes off the queue at distance 0. Its neighbours 1 and 2 are discovered at distance 1, marked visited the moment they are enqueued, and each records 0 as its parent.

@4-6
1 comes off. Its neighbour 0 is already seen. 3 and 4 are new, so both are discovered via 1 at distance 2.

@7
Now 2 comes off. Its neighbours are 0 and 4, and both are already discovered. Vertex 4 was claimed by 1 a moment ago, so it keeps distance 2 and parent 1. The first discovery wins, and that is exactly why it is the shortest: anything that reaches 4 later is at least as far from the start.

@8-9
3 comes off at distance 2, and finds 5, at distance 3.

@10-11
4 comes off. Of its neighbours 1, 2, 5 and 6, only 6 is new: distance 3, parent 4.

@12-13
5 comes off and discovers 7, the last vertex, at distance 4.

@14-15
6 and 7 come off with nothing new to find, and the queue is empty.

@16
Every vertex was dequeued once and every adjacency list scanned once, so the cost is V plus E. The labels are the shortest path lengths from 0. To recover the path to 7, follow parents backwards: 7, 5, 3, 1, 0, then reverse it. That is four edges, matching its distance of 4. The path through 2 and 4 is also four edges, but BFS returns the one through whichever neighbour was listed first.
