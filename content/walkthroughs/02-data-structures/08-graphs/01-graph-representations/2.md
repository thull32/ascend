@0
A grid of 4 rows and 5 columns, where 1 is land and 0 is water. Here is the idea: this is already a graph. Each cell is a vertex, and its up, down, left and right neighbours are its edges. Nobody builds an adjacency list for it; a neighbour function with a bounds check is the whole representation, and it costs zero bytes. To count islands, scan the cells in order, and every unvisited land cell starts a flood fill that marks its whole island.

@1-4
The scan meets land at row 0, column 0, not yet visited, so it starts island 1. The flood fill spreads to the cell on its right, row 0 column 1, and the cell below, row 1 column 0. Those three cells are the whole island.

@5-8
The scan moves on, skipping cells already marked, and finds unvisited land at row 1, column 3: island 2. It floods right to column 4, and down to row 2, column 3.

@9-10
At row 3, column 0, another unvisited land cell: island 3. Its neighbours are all water, so the flood fill marks just this one cell.

@11-12
And at row 3, column 2, island 4, also a single cell. It touches the island 2 cell at row 2 column 3 only diagonally, and diagonals are not edges here.

@13
Four islands. Each cell was visited once, so the cost is rows times columns. The same pattern, an implicit graph with a neighbour function, is behind rotting oranges, Pacific Atlantic, every maze problem, and state spaces where the vertices are configurations and the edges are legal moves.
