@0
Five vertices, A to E, joined by five undirected edges: A to B, B to C, C back to A, C to D, and D to E. Stored as an adjacency list, each vertex keeps a list of its neighbours, and an undirected edge appears in both endpoints' lists. Watch what a traversal actually touches: at each step it reads exactly one vertex's list. A breadth-first search from A uses a first-in, first-out queue, so it explores vertices in order of distance.

@1-3
A comes off the queue at distance 0. Its list holds B and C, so both are discovered at distance 1 and enqueued.

@4
B comes off next. Its neighbours are A and C, both already discovered, so reading B's list adds nothing.

@5-6
C comes off. Its list holds A, B and D, and only D is new: discovered via C, at distance 2.

@7-8
D comes off, and its list gives E, at distance 3.

@9
E comes off. Its only neighbour, D, is already done, so the queue is empty.

@10
Every vertex was dequeued once, and every list was read once, so the total work is the number of vertices plus the sum of all the list lengths: V plus E. That is why the adjacency list is the default layout for traversals. A matrix would make each of those five reads cost a full row of V entries, however few neighbours the vertex has. The labels left on the vertices are the shortest path lengths from A.
