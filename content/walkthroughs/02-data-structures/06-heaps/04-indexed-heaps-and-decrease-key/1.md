@0
An item already inside a heap needs a smaller key: Dijkstra found a shorter path, or an operator bumped a job's priority. A plain heap cannot find the old entry without scanning, because siblings are unordered. Lazy deletion sidesteps the problem. Leave the old entry where it is, push a fresh one with the new key, and keep a record of each item's current key on the side. When an entry surfaces at the top, check it against the record and throw it away if it is stale. Every entry here is labelled with its item, A, B, C or D.

@1-5
Three items arrive: A with key 7, B with key 3, C with key 9. B's 3 climbs to the root; C's 9 stays put. The record lists A as 7, B as 3 and C as 9.

@6
Now item A's key drops to 3. We do not touch A's old entry. We push a second entry for A, with key 3, and update the record: A is 3. The heap now holds two entries for A.

@7-8
The new 3 for A climbs past A's old 7 and stops under B's 3, because it is not strictly smaller. Two 3s sit at the top, one for B and one for A, and the stale 7 for A has sunk to the bottom.

@9-11
Pop. B's 3 comes off the top. It matches the record, so it is returned, and B is marked as served. The last leaf, A's old 7, moves to the root and sifts down below A's 3.

@12-14
Pop again: A's 3. The record says A is 3, so this entry is current; it is returned and A is served. C's 9 moves up and sifts down below A's 7.

@15-16
A new item, D, arrives with key 1 and climbs straight to the root.

@17-18
Pop: D's 1 comes out, and A's old 7 is back on top.

@19-20
Pop, and the heap hands back A's 7. But the record says A was already served, at key 3. This entry is stale, so it is discarded, not returned: it lands in the discarded list, and C's 9 moves up. The heap itself did nothing special here. The check against the record is the whole mechanism, and in Dijkstra it is simply: has this vertex already been finalised?

@21
The last pop returns C's 9, and the heap is empty.

@22
Returned, in order: B's 3, A's 3, D's 1 and C's 9. Discarded: A's stale 7. Every push and pop was the ordinary logarithmic sift. The cost of laziness is garbage: the heap reached four entries for three items, because it holds one entry per update, not one per item, so for Dijkstra it grows with the number of edges rather than vertices. That is fine in most cases, and the lesson's measurements found far fewer than the worst case. When cancellations vastly outnumber pops, you need a bound, as asyncio has: rebuild the heap once more than half its entries are cancelled. Otherwise, move to an indexed heap with a position map.
