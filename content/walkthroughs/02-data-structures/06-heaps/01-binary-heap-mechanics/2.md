@0
We have nine numbers in no order: 9, 4, 7, 1, 8, 3, 6, 2, 5. Pushing them one at a time would cost n log n. Heapify does better by working bottom-up. Every leaf is already a valid one-element heap, and the leaves are the whole second half of the array. So start at the last internal node, position 3, and walk backwards to the root, sifting each node down.

@1-2
Position 3 holds 1. Its children, 2 and 5, are both leaves and therefore already heaps. One is smaller than both, so nothing moves.

@3-5
Position 2 holds 7, with children 3 and 6. The smaller is 3, and it beats 7, so they swap. Seven lands at position 5, a leaf, and stops.

@6-9
Position 1 holds 4, with children 1 and 8. Swap with 1. Now 4 sits at position 3, with children 2 and 5; swap with 2. It reaches a leaf. Notice that by the time we reach a node, both of its subtrees are already heaps, because we fixed them earlier. That is why one sift-down is enough.

@10-14
Last, the root, 9. It swaps with 1, then with 2, then with 4, falling three levels to a leaf. This is the only expensive sift, and there is only one root.

@15
The array is now 1, 2, 3, 4, 8, 7, 6, 9, 5, a valid heap, with six swaps for nine elements. Here is why heapify is linear. A sift costs at most the node's own height, not the tree's. Half the nodes are leaves and cost nothing, a quarter cost at most one step, an eighth at most two, and the sum stays under about n swaps and 2n comparisons. The expensive nodes near the root are few, and the many nodes near the leaves are cheap. When you get a batch of items before you start extracting, heapify it; don't push them one by one.
