@0
Heap sort, ascending, on 5, 2, 9, 1, 5 and 6, entirely inside the array. It uses a max-heap, so the largest value sits at the root. First heapify; then, again and again, swap the root to the end of the heap and shrink the heap by one. The array splits into a shrinking heap at the front and a growing sorted section at the back.

@1-3
Heapify starts at position 2, which holds 9 with child 6: already in order. Position 1 holds 2, with children 1 and 5. This is a max-heap, so it swaps with the larger child, 5.

@4-6
At the root, 5 has children 5 and 9. It swaps with 9, then meets 6 below it, which is larger, and swaps again.

@7
The array is a max-heap: 9, 5, 6, 1, 2, 5. The maximum is at position 0. Now extract.

@8
Swap the root, 9, with the last heap element, 5, and shrink the heap to five elements. Nine is now in its final sorted place at the end, and it will never move again.

@9-10
The new root, 5, sifts down: its children are 5 and 6, so it swaps with 6 and stops at a leaf.

@11-13
Swap 6 to the boundary. The heap shrinks to four. The new root, 2, has two children that are both 5; it swaps with the left one, then stops above 1.

@14-16
Swap 5 into place. The heap is three elements; the root, 1, swaps with the larger child, 5.

@17-19
Swap the other 5 into place. Two left in the heap; 1 swaps with 2.

@20-21
Swap 2 into place. One element remains, and it is already where it belongs.

@22
Sorted: 1, 2, 5, 5, 6, 9. A linear heapify plus n extractions of logarithmic cost gives n log n in the worst case, with constant extra space and no recursion. Two catches. It is not stable: the two 5s finished in swapped order, because sifts jump. And on real hardware it loses to quicksort and merge sort, because each sift jumps between far-apart positions and misses the cache. It earns its place as the fallback inside introsort, which guarantees n log n when quicksort recurses too deep.
