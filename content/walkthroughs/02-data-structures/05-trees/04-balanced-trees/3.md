@0
This is a B-tree index, drawn small. Every node is a fixed-size page. The root holds two separator keys, 20 and 40, which route a lookup to one of three leaves: 5, 10 and 15 in the first, 20, 25 and 30 in the second, 40, 45 and 50 in the third. Each leaf here holds at most four keys. Every leaf sits at the same depth, and that is the B-tree's balance rule. Reading a page costs the same whether it holds one key or a hundred, so a B-tree packs many keys into each node.

@1
Look up 45. Read the root and search its separators: 45 is at least 20 and at least 40, so follow the pointer to the third leaf. That is one page read.

@2
Read the third leaf, search it, and 45 is found. Two page reads in total, and the root is always cached, so only one of them goes to disk. In a real database a page holds hundreds of keys, so with a fan-out of around 300, four levels index billions of rows.

@3
Now a range scan, 25 to 45. Descend once to the second leaf, then follow its sibling pointer across to the third. The leaves are linked in key order, so a range query reads sequentially and never goes back up to the root.

@4
Insert 42. It routes to the third leaf, which had room, and 42 slides into place between 40 and 45. The leaf now holds four keys, its capacity. Nothing else in the tree changes.

@5
Insert 47. The third leaf would need five keys, one more than it can hold. The page must split.

@6
Half the keys stay, 40, 42 and 45, and the rest move to a new fourth leaf, 47 and 50. The new leaf's smallest key, 47, goes up into the root as a third separator. If the root had overflowed, it would split too, and the tree would grow a new level from the top. That is why all leaves always stay at the same depth: no rotations, only splits.

@7
Insert 12. It routes to the first leaf, which has room, and the leaf becomes 5, 10, 12, 15. Again, nothing else changes.

@8
Three things go wrong in production. A split writes several pages at once, so a crash halfway through needs the write-ahead log to keep the tree consistent. Random inserts leave pages only about 70 percent full. And auto-incrementing keys all land in the rightmost leaf, so every insert queues on that one hot page.

@9
The trade: lookups touch very few pages, scans read in order, and updates happen in place. The price is writing a whole page for every change, and random writes when keys arrive in random order. Log-structured merge trees make the opposite bet: sequential writes, with more work on every read.
