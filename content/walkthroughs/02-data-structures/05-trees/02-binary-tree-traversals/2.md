@0
The same nine-key tree, now visited level by level, top to bottom and left to right. Depth-first walks use a stack; breadth-first needs a queue. The queue holds the frontier: nodes we have seen but not yet visited. It starts with just the root, 8.

@1
Here is the one extra idea that grouping by level needs. Before draining, read the queue's length. Level 0 has one node in the queue, so we pop exactly one.

@2
Dequeue 8 and visit it. Its children, 3 and 10, go on at the back. The queue is 3, 10.

@3
Level 1 begins. The queue's length is 2, so this level is exactly two pops.

@4-5
Dequeue 3 and append its children 1 and 6. The queue now holds 10, 1, 6, which mixes two levels. Only the length we took a moment ago tells us that 10 is the last node of this level. Dequeue 10, append its one child, 14.

@6
Level 2: three nodes in the queue, 1, 6 and 14, so three pops.

@7-9
Node 1 is a leaf and adds nothing. Node 6 adds 4 and 7. Node 14 adds 13. The level so far reads 1, 6, 14, and the queue holds the whole next level: 4, 7, 13.

@10
Level 3 has three nodes in the queue.

@11-13
All three are leaves. Each one is dequeued and visited, nothing is added, and the queue empties.

@14
The output is 8, 3, 10, 1, 6, 14, 4, 7, 13, in four levels. Time is linear, but space is the widest level, not the height: in a complete tree that is half the nodes, far more than a depth-first walk keeps. That is the trade. Breadth-first finds the shallowest thing first and gives you levels for free; depth-first uses less memory and gives you paths. Every level-order variant is this loop with a different line inside: right side view keeps the last node of each level, zigzag reverses every other level.
