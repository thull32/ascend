@0
The nine-key tree again, and we will delete 3. Deletion is where search tree code gets hard, because removing an internal node leaves a hole, and the hole must be filled without breaking the ordering rule. What we do depends on how many children the node has. First, find it.

@1
Three is less than 8, so go left.

@2
Found 3, and it has two children, 1 on the left and 6 on the right. We cannot simply remove it without orphaning a subtree. The trick: replace its value with its in-order successor, the smallest key in its right subtree, and then delete that successor instead.

@3
To find the successor, step once into the right subtree, to 6, then go left as far as possible.

@4
Six's left child is 4, and 4 has no left child of its own. So 4 is the successor: the next key after 3 in sorted order.

@5
Copy 4 over the 3. Everything on the left, just 1, is still smaller than 4, and everything on the right, 6, 7 and the rest, is still larger. The ordering holds because 4 was the very next key.

@6
Now remove the old node that held 4. A successor never has a left child, by definition, so this second delete is always the easy case: a leaf to unlink, or a single child to splice up. Here it was a leaf, and it simply disappears.

@7
The in-order sequence is 1, 4, 6, 7, 8, 10, 13, 14: still sorted, which is the check to run in your head after any mutation. The cost is one walk to find the node plus at most one walk down to the successor, so it is proportional to the height. One caution from the lesson: always taking the successor pulls nodes only from right subtrees, and over many random deletes that skews the tree. That is one more reason nobody ships an unbalanced search tree.
