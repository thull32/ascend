@0
Here is a five-node tree: 10 at the root, 5 on the left with a right child 6, and 15 on the right with a right child 20. We want to know whether it is a valid search tree. The obvious check, comparing each node with its children, is wrong. The correct check is that every node must lie inside a window set by all of its ancestors, and we pass that window down as we go.

@1
The root has no ancestors, so its window is open on both sides, and 10 passes. Going left, the upper bound tightens to 10. Going right, the lower bound rises to 10.

@2
Node 5 must be below 10, and it is. Its own right child will inherit the window from 5 to 10.

@3
Node 6 must be greater than 5 and less than 10. It is, so it passes. Look at that window: it remembers the root's 10, which a parent-only check would have forgotten. In the lesson's broken tree, 6 hung under 15, inside the root's right subtree. There it would have inherited a lower bound of 10 and failed, even though 6 is smaller than its parent 15, exactly as it should be. The visualiser builds its tree by insertion, so 6 lands correctly here.

@4
Node 15 must be greater than 10, with no upper bound. It passes, and passes the window from 15 to infinity on to its right child.

@5
Node 20 sits inside that window, greater than 15. It passes.

@6
Every node respects its inherited bounds, so this tree is valid. The equivalent test is that an in-order walk is strictly increasing: 5, 6, 10, 15, 20. Both methods are linear time, use stack space proportional to the height, and stop at the first violation. One detail for interviews: represent no bound as no bound, not as a sentinel like the smallest integer, and ask which side duplicates go before you write the comparison.
