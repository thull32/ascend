@0
Here is the broken tree from the lesson: 10 at the root, 5 on its left, and 15 on its right with two children, 6 and 20. Every parent and child pair looks fine, which is exactly why the obvious check, comparing each node with its children, passes it. The correct check is that every node must lie inside a window set by all of its ancestors, and we pass that window down as we go.

@1
The root has no ancestors, so its window is open on both sides, and 10 passes. Going left, the upper bound tightens to 10. Going right, the lower bound rises to 10.

@2
Node 5 must be below 10, and it is. It has no children, so this side is finished.

@3
Node 15 must be greater than 10, with no upper bound. It passes. Its left child inherits the window from 10 to 15: greater than the root, because it is in the root's right subtree, and smaller than 15, because it is 15's left child.

@4
Node 6 is smaller than its parent 15, exactly as a left child should be, so a parent-only check would accept it. But it must also be greater than 10, and it is not. The window remembered the root's 10, which the parent check forgot. The tree is invalid, and the walk stops at this first violation; 20 is never checked.

@5
One bad node is enough. This is the same failure that breaks search in the lesson: looking for 6 goes left at 10 and never reaches it. The equivalent test is that an in-order walk must be strictly increasing, and here it reads 5, 10, 6, which fails at the third key. Both methods are linear time, use stack space proportional to the height, and stop at the first violation. One detail for interviews: represent no bound as no bound, not as a sentinel like the smallest integer, and ask which side duplicates go before you write the comparison.
