@0
Eight slots, and every entry lives in the slot array itself: no chains. A key's home slot is its FNV-1a hash modulo 8. If the home slot is taken, linear probing tries the next slot, then the next, wrapping around at the end. These are the five inserts from the lesson's table, and the thing to watch is a run of occupied slots growing.

@1-3
Melon's home is slot 4. It is empty, so melon goes straight in.

@4-7
Lime's home is also slot 4. Melon is there, a collision, so probe slot 5: empty. Lime lands one past its home. The occupied run is now slots 4 and 5.

@8-11
Fig's home is slot 5, which lime now holds. Probe 6: empty, so fig goes there. Fig never collided with melon, yet it was pushed along by the run that melon and lime started.

@12-16
Pear's home is also 5. Lime, then fig, then slot 7 is free. Two slots past home.

@17-22
Mango's home is 5 as well. Lime, fig, pear, then the probe wraps around to slot 0, which is empty. Three slots past home. Only two home slots were ever involved, 4 and 5, yet the run now covers slots 4, 5, 6, 7 and 0: a primary cluster of five. Every key whose home falls anywhere inside it pays to walk to its end.

@23-28
Look up peach, which was never inserted. Its home is slot 6. Fig, no. Pear, no. Mango, no. Slot 1 is empty, and an empty slot ends every probe sequence, so peach is absent. Four probes and three full key comparisons, for a key that collides with nothing, only because its home landed inside the cluster.

@29-32
Delete lime. Its home is slot 4: melon, no. Slot 5: lime, found after 2 probes. But slot 5 is not emptied. It gets a tombstone, a marker that says something used to be here, keep probing. Watch why.

@33-37
Look up pear. Its home is slot 5, which now holds the tombstone. If slot 5 had simply been emptied, the lookup would stop right here and report pear missing, although it sits in slot 7. Instead it keeps going: fig in slot 6, then pear in slot 7. Found after 3 probes.

@38
Four keys and one tombstone in eight slots, and the tombstone still counts toward the load factor, which is 5 out of 8. Linear probing is cache friendly, because the probes walk adjacent memory, but misses get expensive fast: at a load factor of two thirds the expected miss already costs 5 probes, and at 0.9 about 50. That is why open-addressing tables resize early, and why tombstones are only cleared when the table is rebuilt.
