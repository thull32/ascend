@0
A ring, with four servers hashed onto it: N1 at 344 degrees, N2 at 161, N3 at 342 and N4 at 159. Keys are hashed onto the same ring, and the rule is a single sentence: a key belongs to the first server you reach walking clockwise from it, wrapping around past the top.

@1-2
User 1 hashes to 187 degrees. Walking clockwise, the first server is N3 at 342, so N3 owns it. User 7, at 181, also walks to N3.

@3
Video 9 lands at 345, just past N1 and N3. Walking clockwise it wraps around the top and the first server it meets is N4 at 159.

@4-6
Session 3 at 164, cart 5 at 294 and feed 2 at 165 all walk to N3 as well. Look at how the servers sit: two of them near 160 degrees and two near 342. N3 owns the whole arc from 161 to 342, about half the ring, so it holds five of the six keys. Four random points on a ring routinely give one server a huge share, and that is exactly the imbalance virtual nodes exist to fix.

@7
Now add a fifth server, N5, at 340 degrees. It claims one arc: the stretch between the server before it, N2 at 161, and itself. Only keys in that arc change owner, and they all move to N5. Here that arc held five of the six keys, because it was the oversized arc N3 used to own. Video 9 stays on N4, and no key moves between the old servers. With hash modulo N, by contrast, adding a server reshuffles keys across every server.

@8
With one point per server, how much moves depends on where the new point happens to land. That is why real rings place each server at many points, its virtual nodes: about 100 points per server brings each one within roughly 10 percent of its fair share, so a new server takes close to its fair share of keys from all the others, and a failed server's load spreads across everyone instead of one neighbour. Memcached's ketama ring uses 160 points per server, and Cassandra used 256 tokens per node until version 4.
