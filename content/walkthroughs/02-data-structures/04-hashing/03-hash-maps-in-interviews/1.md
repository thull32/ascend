@0
Six empty buckets, with a key's bucket chosen by its hash modulo 6. This is the lesson's group anagrams trace: the words eat, tea, tan, ate, nat and bat, in that order. Each word is reduced to its sorted letters, its canonical key, and the value stored under that key is the list of words in the group. The point to watch is that a repeated key never creates a second entry.

@1-2
Eat becomes A-E-T. That key hashes to 96,464, which is bucket 2. The bucket is empty, so a new entry goes in, with a list holding just eat.

@3-5
Tea also becomes A-E-T. Same key, so same hash, so same bucket, 2. The first entry in the chain is compared and it is equal, so the existing entry is found and tea is appended to its list: eat, tea. Nothing new was added to the table. A map never holds two entries for one key, and that is exactly what makes grouping work.

@6-7
Tan becomes A-N-T, which hashes to bucket 5, empty so far. A new entry, with the list tan.

@8-10
Ate is A-E-T again: bucket 2, one comparison, found, and the list grows to eat, tea, ate.

@11-13
Nat is A-N-T: bucket 5, found on the first comparison, and its list becomes tan, nat.

@14-16
Bat becomes A-B-T, which also hashes to bucket 5. It is compared with A-N-T, a different key, so the walk reaches the end of the chain and appends a new entry with the list bat. That is a collision: two different groups sharing one bucket, kept apart by comparing the full keys.

@17-19
Finally, look up A-E-T. Bucket 2, one comparison, and the answer is the group eat, tea, ate, in input order, as the exercise requires.

@20
Six words produced only three entries in six buckets, a load factor of one half. The hashing was never the hard part. The hard part was choosing a key under which equal things become identical, here the sorted letters, and building that key costs time proportional to the word's length for every word. In the interview, say what the key is before you say hash map.
