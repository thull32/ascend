@0
An empty binary search tree, and eight keys to insert in this order: 50, 30, 70, 20, 40, 60, 80 and 35. One rule holds throughout: every key in a node's left subtree is smaller than it, and every key in its right subtree is larger. Each insert walks down from the root, turning left or right by that rule, until it finds an empty spot.

@1
The tree is empty, so 50 becomes the root.

@2-3
Thirty is smaller than 50, and the left child is empty, so 30 attaches there. Seventy is larger, so it attaches on the right. Both sit at depth 1.

@4-5
Twenty: smaller than 50, go left; smaller than 30, and the left child is empty, so it becomes a leaf at depth 2.

@6-7
Forty: smaller than 50, go left; but larger than 30, so it attaches to the right of 30, also at depth 2. Notice that 40 lands between 30 and 50, which is exactly where it belongs in sorted order.

@8-11
The right side fills the same way. Sixty is larger than 50 but smaller than 70, so it goes to the left of 70. Eighty is larger than both and goes to the right of 70. The tree is now perfectly balanced: seven keys in three levels.

@12-14
The last key, 35. Smaller than 50, go left. Larger than 30, go right. Smaller than 40, and that left child is empty, so 35 becomes a leaf at depth 3, tucked between 30 and 40.

@15
Eight nodes, height 3, the shortest possible for eight keys. Every insert cost one comparison per level, so its cost is the height: logarithmic when the tree is balanced, linear if the keys arrive already sorted and the tree degenerates into a chain, which is why real ordered maps rebalance.

@16
Now the payoff. Walk the tree in order: for every node, first its whole left subtree, then the node, then its right subtree. Because every left subtree holds smaller keys and every right subtree larger ones, that visits the keys from smallest to largest.

@17-20
The walk starts at the leftmost node, 20, which has no left subtree, so it comes out first. Then 30, whose left side is done. Then 30's right subtree: 35, and then 40. The output reads 20, 30, 35, 40.

@21-24
With the whole left side of the root finished, the root, 50, comes next, and then its right subtree in the same order: 60, 70, 80.

@25
The output is 20, 30, 35, 40, 50, 60, 70, 80: sorted, and no sorting step ever ran. That in-order walk is the sorted iteration an ordered map gives you for free, linear in the number of keys, and a range scan is the same walk started at the first key in the range. A hash map cannot do either without sorting everything first.
