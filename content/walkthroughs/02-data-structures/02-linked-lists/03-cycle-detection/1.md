@0
Six nodes, 1 through 6, but the last one, 6, points back to node 3 instead of to null. A plain traversal would loop forever. Floyd's tortoise and hare detects the loop with no visited set: two pointers start at the head, slow moves one node per step, and fast moves two. If fast reaches null, there is no cycle. If there is one, they must meet.

@1-2
Step 1: slow moves to 2, fast to 3. Step 2: slow moves to 3, fast to 5. Slow has just entered the loop, and fast is already inside it.

@3
Step 3: slow moves to 4. Fast goes from 5 to 6, follows the back pointer, and lands on 3. Fast is now behind slow on the loop, and this is the key fact: once both are inside, fast gains exactly one node per step. A gap that shrinks by exactly one cannot skip over zero.

@4
Step 4: both land on 5. They can only meet if fast lapped slow inside a loop, so a cycle exists. The comparison is by node identity, never by value.

@5
Phase 2 finds where the cycle begins. Reset slow to the head and leave fast on the meeting point, and from now on both move one node per step. The distance from the head to the cycle's entry equals the distance from the meeting point to the entry, modulo the cycle length. Here both are 2.

@6
One step: slow is on 2, fast is on 6.

@7
One more, and they meet on 3, the node where the cycle begins. The whole algorithm is linear time and constant space. A hash set of visited nodes is the honest first answer, but on a million nodes it costs tens of megabytes; this costs two pointers. And the speed of 2 is not arbitrary: it guarantees the meeting time is a multiple of the cycle length, which is what makes phase 2 land exactly on the entry.
