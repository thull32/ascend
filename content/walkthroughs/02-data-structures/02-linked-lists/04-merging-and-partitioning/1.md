@0
Two sorted lists. List A is 1, 2, 4, 7, and list B is 1, 3, 4, 8, 9. Because both are sorted, the smallest value left anywhere is always one of the two heads. So the whole algorithm is one rule: compare the heads, move the smaller one onto the end of the merged list, and advance that pointer. This is the merge step of merge sort.

@1
The heads are 1 and 1, a tie. Take the 1 from list A. Ties always go to A, and that rule is what makes the merge stable: equal values keep the order of the lists they came from.

@2
Now the heads are 2 and 1. B's 1 is smaller, so take it and advance B.

@3-4
2 against 3: take A's 2. Then 4 against 3: take B's 3.

@5-6
4 against 4, another tie, so A's 4 goes first. Then 7 against 4, and B's 4 follows it.

@7
7 against 8: take A's 7. List A is now empty.

@8
With A exhausted, the rest of B, 8 and 9, is already sorted and already linked. One pointer assignment attaches it. No more comparisons are needed.

@9
Nine nodes merged with 7 comparisons, so the cost is linear in the two lengths together. Done this way it allocates nothing: the existing nodes are re-threaded by rewriting their next pointers, where an array merge would need a whole output array. And keep the tie rule exactly as it is. Compare with strictly less than, and ties come from the second list; in a database compaction, that is how the older version of a record ends up winning.
