@0
This is the call stack, and the cost of any recursion lives here. The example on screen is factorial of 4, but the mechanics are exactly those of recursive list reversal. main calls factorial of 4. Every call needs its own frame, holding its argument, its return address and its locals.

@1-4
Factorial of 4 cannot finish yet: it needs factorial of 3 first, so it pushes another frame and waits, with its own n of 4 parked in its frame. Factorial of 3 does the same, then 2, then 1. The stack grows one frame per call, and nothing is computed on the way down.

@5
Factorial of 0 is the base case: no further call, it returns 1. This is the deepest point, 6 frames counting main. Recursion depth is live memory. Recursive reversal behaves the same way: one waiting frame per node, so the stack is as deep as the list is long.

@6-10
Now the frames unwind in last in, first out order. Each return pops a frame and hands its value to the caller, which resumes right after the call and multiplies by its own n: 1, then 1, then 2, then 6, then 24.

@11
Factorial of 4 is 24, and the stack is back to main alone. Five frames were pushed and popped. Here is why it matters for lists: CPython's default recursion limit is 1,000, so a recursive reversal of 50,000 nodes fails at once, and Node runs out of stack at about 9,600 frames. Recursive reversal also does work after the call returns, so even tail-call elimination would not save it. Write the iterative version, and never recurse over a list whose length you do not control.
