@0
Two stacks side by side: the main stack, and an auxiliary min stack that is always exactly as tall. Each entry in the min stack is the minimum of the main stack from the bottom up to that height. So the top of the min stack is the minimum of everything currently stored, and asking for the minimum is a single read, with no scan.

@1
Push 5. Both stacks are empty, so 5 is the minimum so far, and 5 goes onto each.

@2
Push 3. Three goes onto the main stack, and onto the min stack goes the smaller of 3 and the min stack's top, 5. That is 3, a new minimum.

@3
Push 7. Seven goes onto the main stack, and the min stack gets the smaller of 7 and 3, which is 3 again. The minimum has not changed, but it is recorded once more at this height, so the two stacks stay the same height.

@4
Push another 3. The smaller of 3 and 3 is 3. A tie is just another push here, with no special rule. The min stack now reads 5, 3, 3, 3.

@5
Get min reads the top of the min stack: 3.

@6
Pop. The second 3 comes off the main stack, and the min stack pops at the same time, with no comparison at all. Its new top is 3, the minimum of 5, 3 and 7, which is exactly what remains.

@7
Get min: 3.

@8
Pop 7, and pop the min stack with it. Its top is still 3.

@9-10
Pop the first 3, and the min stack pops with it. Its top is now 5, the only value left. Get min confirms it: 5.

@11
Push, pop, top and get min were all constant time. The price is a second stack exactly as tall as the first, so linear extra space every time. In exchange, pops never compare anything, and duplicates cannot go wrong, because the two stacks always move together. The same trick works for any associative summary, such as a max, a sum or a gcd: keep a parallel stack of prefix summaries. The space-saving version, which pushes only on a new minimum or a tie, is worth mentioning, but this is the one to write on a whiteboard.
