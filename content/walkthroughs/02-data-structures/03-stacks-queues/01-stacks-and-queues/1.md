@0
An empty stack. A stack has exactly one working end, the top. Push writes there, pop reads there, and that single rule is the whole data structure: the last item in is the first item out.

@1-3
Push 3, then 7, then 1. Each one lands on top, and the items below it are never touched. After three pushes the stack holds 3 at the bottom, 7 in the middle and 1 on top, and the size is 3.

@4
Now pop. Out comes 1, the item pushed most recently, even though it arrived last. Seven is the new top and the size drops to 2.

@5
Push 9. It goes on top of 7, so the stack is 3, 7, 9.

@6-8
Three pops in a row. Nine leaves first, then 7, then 3, and the stack is empty. Look at the popped list: 1, 9, 7, 3. Each pop took whichever item had arrived most recently among those still on the stack.

@9
Every operation touched only the top, so push, pop and peek each cost constant time. That is why a plain dynamic array is already a stack: append and pop at its end do exactly this. The one decision left to you is what pop does on an empty stack, and that boundary is the case that breaks bracket matching and expression problems, so check for empty before you pop.
