@0
Two empty stacks: one called in, where every enqueue pushes, and one called out, where every dequeue pops. The rule that makes this a queue: refill out only when it is empty, by draining in completely. Watch the move counter in the corner.

@1-3
Enqueue 1, 2 and 3. Each is a single push onto in, and out is not touched. In now holds 1 at the bottom and 3 on top. Zero moves so far.

@4-6
Now a dequeue, and out is empty, so first comes the transfer. Pop 3 from in and push it onto out. Then 2. Then 1. Three moves, and the order has flipped: 1, the oldest item, which was buried at the bottom of in, is now on top of out.

@7
Pop the top of out: 1, the first item enqueued. Two items remain in out, so the next two dequeues need no moves at all.

@8
Enqueue 4. It goes onto in, not out. Out still holds 3 and 2, which are older than 4, so 4 has to wait behind them.

@9-10
Dequeue twice: 2, then 3, each a single pop from out. Four is still sitting in in. This is the point of the rule: transfer only when out runs dry. Moving 4 across while 2 and 3 were still there would have put it on top of them and broken the order.

@11
Out is now empty and we need another dequeue, so transfer again. One move: 4 crosses from in to out.

@12
Pop it. Four comes out, and the dequeued list reads 1, 2, 3, 4: first in, first out.

@13
Count the work. The first dequeue cost three moves; most of the others cost none. Each element is pushed onto in once, moved to out once and popped from out once, so 8 operations caused only 4 moves. A single dequeue can be linear, but the amortised cost per operation is constant, and that is the answer an interviewer is listening for.
