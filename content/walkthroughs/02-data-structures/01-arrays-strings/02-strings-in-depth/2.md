@0
The sentence is "the quick brown fox", and we want the words in reverse order, "fox brown quick the", without a second buffer. The trick is two reversals. First reverse the whole string: the words land in the right order, but each one is spelled backwards. Then reverse each word on its own to fix the spelling.

@1-3
Phase one uses two pointers, one at each end. Swap position 0 with position 18, the t with the x, and move both pointers inward. Then the h with the o, then the e with the f. The first word on screen already reads x, o, f: fox, backwards.

@4-9
The pointers keep closing in. At positions 3 and 15 two spaces swap with each other, which changes nothing. Then the letters of quick and brown trade places across the middle, one pair per step, until only the space at position 9 is left between them.

@10
The whole string is reversed: x o f, n w o r b, k c i u q, e h t. Every word is in its final position, just spelled backwards. Phase two walks the string, finds each word between the spaces, and reverses it with the same two-pointer swap.

@11-12
The first word is positions 0 to 2. One swap, the x with the f, and it reads fox.

@13-15
The next word is positions 4 to 8. Two swaps: the outer pair, then the inner pair. The middle letter stays put. It reads brown.

@16-18
Positions 10 to 14, two more swaps, and it reads quick.

@19-20
The last word, positions 16 to 18. One swap, and it reads the.

@21
The result is "fox brown quick the". Every character is swapped at most twice, once in each phase, so the whole thing is linear time with constant extra space. In Python, strings are immutable, so in-place work like this means converting to a list first and joining at the end.
