@0
A five by five grid, where a 1 is a wall. We want the shortest path from the top-left corner to the bottom-right. Look at it as a graph: every open cell is a node, and its up, down, left and right neighbours are its edges. Nobody builds an adjacency list; the neighbour function is the adjacency list. Every move costs 1, so breadth-first search finds the shortest path. The start cell sits alone in the queue.

@1-4
Expand the start at distance 0. Its only open neighbour is to the right, so that goes into the queue. From there the walls leave a single corridor: right again to row 0, column 2 at distance 2, then down to row 1, column 2 at distance 3. The queue never holds more than one cell, because there is only one way forward.

@5
Row 2, column 2, at distance 4, is the first fork. Row 2 is completely open, so two neighbours join the queue, one to the right and one to the left.

@6-7
Both cells at distance 5 are expanded in turn, the right one, then the left one. Each adds its own next neighbour. The search spreads in both directions at once, like a ripple.

@8-9
Distance 6. The cell at the right edge of row 2 opens two new cells, one above and one below it. The cell at the left edge opens the cell below it.

@10-12
Distance 7. Three cells come out of the queue, and the frontier now touches the bottom-right corner. Breadth-first search finishes every cell at one distance before it touches any cell at the next, and that ordering is why the first time it reaches a cell, it has found the shortest route there.

@13
The goal is reached at distance 8, and the path is highlighted: along the top, down the corridor, across row 2, and down the right edge. The detour to the left was explored and wasted, which is the price of a blind search. The cost is linear in the number of cells, rows times columns. And on a big open grid, prefer this queue, or an explicit stack, to a recursive depth-first search: a thousand by thousand grid can recurse a million deep, far past Python's default limit of 1,000.
