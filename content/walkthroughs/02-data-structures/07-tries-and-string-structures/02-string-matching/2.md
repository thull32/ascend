@0
Rabin-Karp compares hashes instead of characters. The text is the digits 3 1 4 1 5 9 2 6 5 3, and the pattern is 5 9. Here the window is treated as a number in base 31, modulo 101, and the pattern hashes to 84. The trick that makes this linear is the roll: each window's hash comes from the previous one in constant time.

@1
The first window, 3 1, hashes to 14. That is not 84, so it is skipped without looking at a single character.

@2-3
To roll, drop the leading 3 by subtracting its contribution, shift by multiplying by the base, and add the incoming 4. The new hash is 56, computed in constant time. Not 84: skip.

@4-7
The window keeps sliding. 4 1 hashes to 45, then 1 5 to 57. Neither equals 84, and neither costs a character comparison.

@8-9
Roll again, dropping 1 and adding 9: the window 5 9 hashes to 84, equal to the pattern's hash. That makes it a candidate, not yet a match, because two different windows can share a hash.

@10-12
So verify, character by character. 5 equals 5, and 9 equals 9: a genuine match at position 4.

@13-20
The scan rolls on through the rest of the text: 9 2 hashes to 100, 2 6 to 89, 6 5 to 10, and 5 3 to 78. None equals 84, so all are skipped.

@21
One match, at position 4. Expected cost is the text length plus the pattern length, because each window costs constant time unless its hash collides. That last clause is the trap. With a modulus as small as 13, the windows 1 4, 9 2 and 5 3 all share one hash, and without verification a search would report false matches. In production, use a large prime modulus, a random base chosen at start-up, and always verify a hash match.
