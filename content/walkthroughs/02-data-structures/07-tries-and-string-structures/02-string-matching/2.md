@0
Rabin-Karp compares hashes instead of characters. The text is the digits 3 1 4 1 5 9 2 6 5 3, and the pattern is 5 9. As in the lesson's table, each window is read as a base 10 number and reduced modulo a deliberately tiny 13, so the pattern hashes to 59 mod 13, which is 7. The trick that makes this linear is the roll: each window's hash comes from the previous one in constant time.

@1
The first window, 31, hashes to 5. That is not 7, so it is skipped without looking at a single character.

@2-3
To roll, drop the leading 3 by subtracting 3 times 10, multiply by the base, 10, and add the incoming 4. Modulo 13 that gives 1, and indeed 14 mod 13 is 1. Not 7: skip.

@4-7
The window keeps sliding. 41 hashes to 2, and so does 15: two different windows, one hash. Neither equals 7, so neither costs a character comparison.

@8-9
Roll again, dropping 1 and adding 9: the window 59 hashes to 7, equal to the pattern's hash. That makes it a candidate, not yet a match, because two different windows can share a hash, as you just saw.

@10-12
So verify, character by character. 5 equals 5, and 9 equals 9: a genuine match at position 4.

@13-20
The scan rolls on through the rest of the text: 92 hashes to 1, 26 and 65 both to 0, and 53 to 1. None equals 7, so all are skipped.

@21
One match, at position 4. Expected cost is the text length plus the pattern length, because each window costs constant time unless its hash collides. That last clause is the trap. With a modulus as small as 13, the windows 14, 92 and 53 all hash to 1, so a search for 92 would find three candidates, and without verification it would report false matches at positions 1 and 8. In production, use a large prime modulus, a random base chosen at start-up, and always verify a hash match.
