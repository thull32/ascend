@0
Inside one point of presence there are many cache servers. If requests were spread randomly, every popular object would end up on every server, and the PoP would hold one server's worth of distinct content. Instead, the front layer hashes the cache key to the one server responsible for it. Servers and keys share a circle, and a key belongs to the first server clockwise from it. Four servers sit on this ring: N4 at 159 degrees, N2 at 161, N3 at 342 and N1 at 344.

@1
The trailer page hashes to 169 degrees. Walking clockwise, the first server is N3, at 342.

@2-3
The poster image lands at 22 degrees, and the JavaScript bundle at 15. For both, the next server clockwise is N4 at 159.

@4
The API page hashes to 358 degrees, past every server, so the walk wraps around the top of the circle to the first server, N4 again.

@5-6
The video segment lands at 24 degrees and goes to N4. The stylesheet lands at 286 and goes to N3. Notice the imbalance: N4 owns four of the six keys, and N1 and N2 own none.

@7
Now add a fifth server, N5, at 340 degrees. Only the keys between its predecessor, N2 at 161, and 340 change owner: the trailer page and the stylesheet move from N3 to N5. That is 2 of 6 keys. Every other server keeps its cache warm. With hash mod N, nearly every key would have moved, and the whole PoP would go cold at once.

@8
That imbalance you saw is why real systems give each physical server around 100 to 200 virtual positions on the ring, so load evens out and a failed server's keys spread across many neighbours. The price of one owner per key is a hot key: one viral object lands on one server, which is why CDNs also keep extra copies and coalesce requests for it.
