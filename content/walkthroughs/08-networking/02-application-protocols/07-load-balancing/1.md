@0
Routers spread flows across a pool of L4 balancer machines, and a change in that pool can send the next packet of an established connection to a machine that has never seen it. That machine must still pick the same backend, without asking anyone. The answer is consistent hashing: every balancer computes the backend from the same hash, so they agree without coordination. Here, four backends sit on a ring: N4 at 159 degrees, N2 at 161, N3 at 342 and N1 at 344. A flow, identified by its client address and port, belongs to the next backend clockwise.

@1
The first flow, from 10.1.4.7 port 51220, hashes to 320 degrees. The next backend clockwise is N3.

@2-4
The next three flows land at 138 degrees, 10 degrees, and 139 degrees, and all three belong to N4 at 159. Two of them come from the same client, on ports 51220 and 51221, and still land in different places: it is the whole flow that is hashed, not just the client.

@5-6
The last two flows land at 258 and 309 degrees, and both go to N3. N1 and N2 own nothing on this small ring, which is the imbalance real systems fix with many virtual positions per backend.

@7
Now a fifth backend, N5, joins at 340 degrees. Only the flows between its predecessor, N2 at 161, and 340 change owner: the three at 258, 309 and 320 move from N3 to N5. The other three keep their backend. With hash mod N, nearly every flow would move, and nearly every established connection would break.

@8
Google's Maglev uses the same idea with a lookup table instead of a ring, sized to a prime well over 100 times the number of backends so shares differ by at most 1 percent. Each machine also keeps a local connection table, so flows that were already established keep their backend even while the table changes.
