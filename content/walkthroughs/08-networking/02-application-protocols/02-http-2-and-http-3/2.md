@0
This is the problem HTTP/2 inherits from TCP. Under all the streams there is one ordered byte stream, and TCP knows nothing about HTTP streams. The segments on screen could carry frames for several unrelated requests, and TCP will still deliver them strictly in order.

@1
Four segments go out. The one at sequence 1001 is lost.

@2
Sequence 1 arrives and is delivered. The receiver acknowledges 1001.

@3-4
Sequence 2001 and then 3001 arrive. They are complete, and they may hold frames for streams that have nothing to do with the lost one. But they sit after the gap, so the receiver buffers them, hands none of them to the application, and keeps acknowledging 1001. Those are duplicate acknowledgements one and two.

@5
The third duplicate tells the sender that a single segment is missing.

@6
It retransmits sequence 1001 at once, without waiting for the timer. Every stream that had bytes after the gap has now waited at least a round trip for a segment it did not need.

@7
The retransmission fills the hole, the acknowledgement jumps to 4001, and the buffered bytes are finally released to HTTP/2 all at once.

@8
Put numbers on it. With 100 packets per round trip across 10 active streams and 1 percent independent loss, about 63 percent of round trips contain a loss, and over HTTP/2 each one stalls all 10 streams. If each stream recovered on its own, a given stream would stall in only about a tenth of round trips. On lossy cellular links, plain HTTP/1.1 over several connections can even beat HTTP/2. TCP cannot realistically be changed, so the fix is a transport that knows about streams: QUIC, and HTTP/3 on top of it.
