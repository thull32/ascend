@0
One client, 999 other clients behind it, and a service that is overloaded. The client will retry, and the rule is exponential backoff: after each failure, wait longer, doubling from a 100 millisecond base, capped at 2 seconds, with at most 4 attempts. And each wait is randomised, full jitter, so clients that failed together do not retry together.

@1
Attempt 1 fails with a 503. That is worth retrying: a 503 or a timeout may be transient. A 400 or a 404 is not, and retrying it only adds load.

@2
The schedule says wait 100 milliseconds. Full jitter picks a random value between zero and 100, here 24. So the next attempt goes at 24 milliseconds.

@3-4
Attempt 2 fails too. The schedule doubles to 200 milliseconds, and jitter picks 183, so attempt 3 is due at 207 milliseconds.

@5-6
Attempt 3 fails. The schedule doubles again to 400, jitter picks 245, and attempt 4 is due at 452 milliseconds.

@7
Attempt 4 succeeds: 200 OK, after 452 milliseconds of total waiting, while the service recovered. Had all four attempts fired immediately, every one would have landed inside the same overload and failed.

@8
Now the failure mode jitter prevents. A thousand clients fail at the same instant, all sleep exactly 100 milliseconds, and all retry in lockstep. The service, just beginning to recover, is hit by the same wave again, now perfectly synchronised. Retries have turned an outage into a longer one.

@9
With full jitter, the same thousand retries are spread across the whole 100 millisecond interval, and the service sees a gentle ramp it can actually serve. Jitter is not optional; it is the part that makes backoff work at scale.

@10
Two more rules. Cap retries with a budget, for example retries at most 10 percent of requests, so a total outage cannot multiply load by the number of attempts. And retry only idempotent operations, or a charge that timed out but actually succeeded gets charged twice.

@11
gRPC builds all of this into its service config. Its backoff jitter is narrower, plus or minus 20 percent rather than full, and its retry throttling is the budget: tokens that failures spend and successes slowly earn back, so retrying stops during an outage. Retries turn transient failures into successes, at the cost of latency and extra load exactly when the system can least afford it. Backoff bounds the load, jitter spreads it, and a budget with a circuit breaker stops it when the failure is not transient.
