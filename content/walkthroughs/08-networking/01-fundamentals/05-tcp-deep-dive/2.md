@0
TCP numbers bytes, not packets. A segment's sequence number is the number of its first payload byte, and an acknowledgement number means: I have every byte before this one, send me this one next. In this picture each segment holds 1,000 bytes and the receiver's window is 4,000 bytes.

@1
The sender does not wait for an acknowledgement after each segment. It sends four back to back, at sequence numbers 1, 1001, 2001 and 3001, which puts 4,000 bytes in flight. How much it may have unacknowledged is the smaller of two windows: the congestion window and the receiver's window.

@2
The receiver sends a single acknowledgement, 4001, which covers all four segments: every byte below 4001 arrived, in order. This is the delayed acknowledgement rule at work. The receiver acknowledges at least every second full segment and otherwise waits on a timer, 40 milliseconds minimum on Linux, hoping to piggyback the ACK on response data.

@3
That rule halves acknowledgement traffic on bulk transfers. It is also half of the 41 millisecond stall from the lesson's opening: a small request split over two writes, Nagle's algorithm holding the second write until an ACK arrives, and the receiver deliberately delaying that ACK. Keep the bound in mind too: throughput is at most the window divided by the round trip time, so a 64 kilobyte window over 100 milliseconds caps you at 640 kilobytes a second, which is why window scaling exists.
