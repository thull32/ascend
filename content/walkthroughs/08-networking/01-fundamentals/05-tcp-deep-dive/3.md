@0
Waiting 200 milliseconds or more for a retransmission timer is a disaster on a 20 millisecond path. So most losses are detected by watching the receiver instead. The receiver's acknowledgements are cumulative: it keeps naming the last byte it has in order, and that repetition is the signal.

@1
The sender transmits four segments of 1,000 bytes. The one at sequence 1001 is lost on the way.

@2
Sequence 1 arrives, and the receiver acknowledges 1001: send me byte 1001 next.

@3
Sequence 2001 arrives out of order. The receiver buffers it, but it cannot acknowledge past the hole, so it says 1001 again. That is duplicate ACK number 1.

@4
Sequence 3001 arrives and is buffered too: duplicate ACK number 2. With selective acknowledgement, this ACK would also carry a block saying, I hold 2001 to 4000.

@5
Duplicate ACK number 3. Three duplicates tell the sender something specific: later segments are getting through, so the path is working, and exactly one segment is missing. This is an isolated loss, not a collapse.

@6
So the sender retransmits sequence 1001 immediately, without waiting for the timer. It halves its congestion window rather than resetting it to one segment; that harsher reset is reserved for a real timeout.

@7
The retransmission fills the hole, and the cumulative acknowledgement jumps straight to 4001, covering the two segments the receiver had buffered.

@8
The cost difference is the whole point. A timeout means waiting at least 200 milliseconds and restarting the congestion window from one segment. Fast retransmit, with selective acknowledgements telling the sender exactly which ranges arrived, repairs an isolated loss in about one round trip and keeps the pipe full.
