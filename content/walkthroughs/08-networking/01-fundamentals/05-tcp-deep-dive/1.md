@0
A TCP connection is two byte counters, one per direction, and each starts at a random initial sequence number. The handshake exists so each side learns the other's starting number. It is also the only moment the two sides can negotiate options, so everything this connection will be able to do is decided in its first two packets. On screen, a closed client and a listening server.

@1
The client sends a SYN with its initial sequence number, 100, and enters SYN sent. In a real capture this packet also carries the client's options: its maximum segment size, permission for selective acknowledgements, timestamps and a window scale.

@2
The server replies with a SYN-ACK. It acknowledges 101, not 100, because the SYN itself consumes one sequence number, so 101 is the next byte it expects. It picks its own initial number, 300, and moves to SYN received. Until the handshake completes, this half-open connection waits in the server's SYN queue.

@3
The client acknowledges 301, the server's number plus one, and is established. This third packet may already carry data.

@4
When the ACK arrives, the server is established too, and the completed connection moves to the accept queue until your process calls accept. The random starting numbers are a defence: an attacker off the path cannot guess them to inject data, and a delayed segment from an old connection on the same addresses and ports is unlikely to land in the new one's window. And if that accept queue overflows because your process is too slow, the kernel drops the handshake, the client retries its SYN after one second, and your connect latency clusters at whole seconds.
