@0
The perimeter model trusts any packet from inside the network, and it fails the day one inside host is compromised. Zero trust drops network location as a credential. On screen is one way to do that for services: three services, Orders, Payments and Inventory, each with a sidecar proxy beneath it that handles all of its traffic, and a control plane at the top. The table shows the policy every sidecar will receive: mutual TLS required with certificates rotated hourly; Orders may call Payments with a 500 millisecond timeout and two retries; and an endpoint is ejected after five consecutive server errors.

@1
The control plane pushes routes, policy and short-lived certificates to every sidecar. The application containers never see any of it.

@2
Orders makes what it thinks is a plain HTTP call to Payments on port 8080. Packet redirection rules in the pod quietly send that connection into Orders' own sidecar. The application has no idea a mesh exists.

@3
Orders' sidecar picks a healthy Payments endpoint and opens a mutually authenticated TLS connection to the Payments sidecar. Here is the point of the picture: both sides verify who the other is from its certificate, its workload identity, not from its IP address.

@4
The Payments sidecar checks the authorisation policy, which names identities: Orders may call Payments. It terminates TLS and hands plain HTTP to the Payments application over localhost.

@5
The response, 200 OK in 32 milliseconds, flows back through both sidecars, each recording latency, status and a trace span. The two extra hops cost roughly 1 to 2 milliseconds.

@6
Now Payments starts failing. Orders' sidecar applies the retry policy it was given, a second attempt within its budget of two retries, without any change to Orders' code.

@7
After five consecutive server errors, the sidecar ejects that Payments endpoint from its pool and answers Orders with a fast 503. That is outlier detection, a circuit breaker per endpoint. When Payments recovers, it is let back in after a cool-down.

@8
Every sidecar reports metrics, traces and access logs in the same shape, whatever language the service is written in, so you get a service graph and per-edge signals for free.

@9
Traffic shaping becomes configuration: the control plane moves to config version 2 and tells Orders' sidecar to send 10 percent of Payments calls to a new version 2. Canary releases are now a policy change, not a code change.

@10
The price. The control plane is a new critical dependency. When it goes down, sidecars keep running on their last config, but new pods cannot join and certificates stop rotating. Every pod pays a proxy's memory and CPU, and debugging now spans the app, the sidecar and the mesh configuration.

@11
So the trade: uniform mutual TLS, retries, routing and observability with zero application changes, against an extra hop and a control plane to operate. Worth it at dozens of services in several languages; overkill for a handful. And for zero trust specifically, the win is the one you saw in the middle: a compromised host gains nothing from its IP address, because every call is authorised by identity. Security groups stay, as defence in depth.
