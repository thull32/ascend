@0
Same sender, same chart of the congestion window per round trip, starting at one segment with a slow start threshold of 16. This time the network will drop a packet, and you will see the rule every classic algorithm is built on: additive increase, multiplicative decrease.

@1-8
The opening is the same exponential ramp. One segment, then 2, then 4, then 8, each window fully acknowledged and each doubling the next. After round trip 4 the window reaches 16.

@9
Round trip 5 sends 16 segments, of which the picture shows 8.

@10
This time a loss is detected by three duplicate acknowledgements. That is the multiplicative decrease: the threshold becomes half the window, 8, and the window drops to 8 too. This is fast recovery. Had the retransmission timer fired instead, the sender would assume something worse had happened and drop the window all the way to one segment, starting slow start again.

@11-16
Now the window is already at the new threshold, so growth is additive: 8 segments in round trip 6, then 9, then 10, one more each round trip, with every window fully acknowledged.

@17
The chart reads 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, then down to 8, then 9, 10, 11. Extend it and you get the sawtooth: a linear ramp up to a loss, a halving, another ramp. Its period tells you something: climbing back from half a window to a full one takes half a window's worth of round trips, so a flow with a 100 segment window and a 50 millisecond round trip loses a packet about every 2.5 seconds. Loss-based algorithms fill buffers until they overflow, which is the bufferbloat story. Cubic grows faster on long, fat paths, and BBR models bandwidth and round trip time instead of reacting to loss.
