@0
IP addresses are logical. To put a frame on an Ethernet link you need the next device's MAC address, and ARP is how you get it. On screen, host A at 10.0.0.5 wants to send to host B at 10.0.0.9, on the same link, through a switch, with host C at 10.0.0.7 also attached. Until A learns B's MAC, its first IP packet cannot leave.

@1
A broadcasts to the all-ones MAC address: who has 10.0.0.9? Tell 10.0.0.5.

@2
The switch floods a broadcast to every host on the segment. Host C receives the question, sees it is not about its address, and ignores it.

@3
Host B receives the same question and recognises its own IP.

@4
B answers A directly, not by broadcast, with its MAC address. A caches the mapping, keeping it for tens of seconds and then revalidating it, and sends the IP packet it was holding. The cache is what makes this cheap: only the first packet to a neighbour waits, and on a LAN that wait is typically well under a millisecond.

@5
Three things to take away. Gratuitous ARP lets a host announce an address change without being asked, which is how a standby router takes over a shared address. ARP has no authentication, so anyone on the segment can answer for someone else's address, which is ARP spoofing and why cloud networks answer ARP from a controller-managed table instead of trusting broadcasts. And IPv6 does not use ARP at all: neighbour discovery replaces it.
