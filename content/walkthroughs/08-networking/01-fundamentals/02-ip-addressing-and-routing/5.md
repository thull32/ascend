@0
Between autonomous systems, routers run BGP, a path-vector protocol. An announcement says: I can reach this prefix, and here is the list of autonomous systems the route passes through. On screen, a content network at AS 64500 on the left, a transit provider, an intermediate ISP, and your ISP on the right. Remember one thing about BGP: policy, not distance, picks the best route.

@1
The origin, AS 64500, announces the prefix 198.51.100.0 slash 24, with an AS path containing only itself.

@2
The transit provider, AS 3356, prepends its own number, so the path is now 3356, 64500, and re-announces it to the neighbours its policy allows. Those export rules keep traffic flowing only where someone is paying for it.

@3
AS 7018 prepends itself in turn, and your ISP receives the route with a three-AS path: 7018, 3356, 64500. If it also heard the prefix over a direct peering with 64500, it would compare the two, and the order matters: local preference, set by business relationship, comes first, and AS path length only breaks the tie after that.

@4
The path does two jobs. It prevents loops, because a router rejects any announcement that already contains its own AS number. And it feeds the decision order: local preference, then path length, then origin, then the multi-exit discriminator, and so on. That is why big content networks peer directly with ISPs: the path becomes one hop, cheaper and lower latency. The risk is that BGP believes what it hears, so route leaks and hijacks spread the same way, which is what RPKI exists to mitigate.
