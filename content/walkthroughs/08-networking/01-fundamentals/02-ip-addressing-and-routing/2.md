@0
A packet has to travel from host A at 10.0.0.5, through three routers, to host B at 203.0.113.9. Here is the idea to hold: no device on this path knows the whole route. Each one looks up the destination in its own table, picks the row with the longest matching prefix, forwards one hop, and forgets.

@1
A's own table says 203.0.113.0 slash 24 is not on its local network, so the packet goes to the default gateway, R1. The Ethernet frame is addressed to R1's MAC, but the IP header still says destination 203.0.113.9, and the time to live starts at 64.

@2
R1 has two rows that contain the destination: 203.0.0.0 slash 16 and 203.0.113.0 slash 24. The slash 24 is longer, more specific, so it wins, and it points to R2. R1 decrements the time to live to 63 and builds a fresh link-layer frame for the next hop.

@3
R2 makes its own decision, independently, and forwards to R3. The time to live is now 62. Because every hop decides on its own, a routing change in the middle of a flow does not break anything: later packets simply take the new path.

@4
R3 has a route for B's network that is directly connected, so it resolves B's MAC with ARP and delivers the packet. It arrives with a time to live of 61: three routers, three decrements.

@5
That counter is the safety net. If a misconfiguration creates a routing loop, the packet circulates only until its time to live reaches zero, and then a router drops it instead of letting it spin forever. Traceroute turns the same counter into a tool for discovering the path, which is the next picture.
