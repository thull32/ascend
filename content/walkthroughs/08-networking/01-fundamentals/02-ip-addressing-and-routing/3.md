@0
Traceroute discovers a path that no single router knows. The trick is to send probes with a deliberately small time to live. Every router decrements it, and the router that takes it to zero drops the probe and sends back an ICMP time exceeded message, which reveals the router's address.

@1
The first probe goes out with a time to live of 1.

@2
Hop 1 decrements it to zero, drops the probe, and reports back. The round trip is about 1 millisecond, and the first hop is now known.

@3
The next probe has a time to live of 2, so it gets past hop 1.

@4
Hop 2 takes it to zero and reveals itself, at about 8 milliseconds.

@5
The third probe goes out with a time to live of 3.

@6
And nothing comes back before the timeout. Hop 3 either rate-limits ICMP or filters it, so traceroute prints three stars for that line. This does not mean the path is broken: the router still forwards traffic, it just declines to complain.

@7
A probe with a time to live of 4 reaches the target itself.

@8
The destination answers differently. For UDP probes it says port unreachable, and for ICMP probes it sends an echo reply. That different answer is how traceroute knows it has arrived.

@9
Read the output with care. Latency jumps can mean a long link or a congested hop, but a router generates these replies on a slow path, so a spike at one hop that does not persist at the hops after it is just ICMP being deprioritised, not a problem. The return path may differ from the forward path, and some networks hide hops entirely. For continuous per-hop loss and latency, use mtr.
