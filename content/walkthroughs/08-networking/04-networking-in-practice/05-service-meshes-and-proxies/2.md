@0
Three services, Orders, Payments and Inventory, each with a sidecar proxy beneath it that intercepts all of its traffic, and a control plane above them all. That split is the mesh: the sidecars are the data plane, carrying every request, and the control plane only configures them. The table shows the policy every sidecar receives: mutual TLS required with hourly certificate rotation, Orders may call Payments with a 500 millisecond timeout and two retries, and an endpoint is ejected after five consecutive server errors.

@1
The control plane pushes routes, policy and certificates to every sidecar over long-lived streams. The application containers never see any of it.

@2
Orders makes what it thinks is an ordinary HTTP call to Payments. Redirect rules in the pod send it into Orders' own sidecar.

@3
The sidecar does not need DNS here: it already holds the list of healthy Payments endpoints from the control plane, picks one, and opens a mutually authenticated TLS connection to the Payments sidecar. Each side checks the other's certificate identity, not its IP address.

@4
The Payments sidecar checks the authorisation policy, Orders may call Payments, terminates TLS, and hands plain HTTP to the application over localhost.

@5
The response, 200 OK in 32 milliseconds, flows back through both sidecars, each recording latency, status and a trace span. The two extra hops cost roughly 1 to 2 milliseconds.

@6
Payments starts failing, and Orders' sidecar retries within its budget, with no change to Orders' code. Be careful with this in real configuration: a proxy cannot know whether a charge is safe to repeat. Retrying on connect failures is always safe, because the request provably never arrived; retrying on server errors belongs only on idempotent routes or ones carrying an idempotency key.

@7
After five consecutive server errors, the sidecar ejects that Payments endpoint and fails fast with a 503. That is outlier detection, a breaker per endpoint.

@8
Every sidecar reports the same metrics, traces and access logs, whatever language the service is written in.

@9
Traffic shaping is configuration: the control plane moves to version 2 of its config and tells Orders' sidecar to send 10 percent of Payments calls to a new version.

@10
Then the control plane goes down. The sidecars keep forwarding on their last configuration, but new pods cannot join, endpoint changes stop propagating, and certificates stop rotating. With day-long certificates, a day-long control plane outage becomes a mutual TLS outage: a new tier-zero dependency with a delayed blast radius.

@11
The trade: uniform mutual TLS, retries, routing and telemetry in every language with zero application changes, against an extra hop, a proxy's memory in every pod, and a control plane to run. Worth it at dozens of services in several languages; overkill for a handful, where a shared client library would do.
