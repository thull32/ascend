@0
Backoff slows retries; a circuit breaker stops them. On screen, a checkout service, a breaker in front of its dependency, and the payments API. The breaker has three states. Closed passes calls through and counts failures. After 3 consecutive failures it opens and fails every call instantly. After 5 seconds it goes half-open, and lets one trial call decide what happens next.

@1
Normal operation: the breaker is closed, call 1 returns 200 OK in 40 milliseconds, and a success resets the failure count.

@2
Then payments starts timing out. Call 2 holds a checkout thread for the full 2 second timeout before failing. Failure 1 of 3.

@3
Failure 2 of 3, another 2 seconds and another thread. Under load, this is how one slow dependency exhausts a caller's whole thread pool.

@4
Call 4 times out too, failure 3 of 3, and the breaker trips open.

@5
Call 5 is rejected by the breaker in under a millisecond, without touching the network. A slow failure has become a fast one, and the checkout thread is free again.

@6
Over the next 5 seconds the breaker sheds 15 more calls the same way, saving 15 threads from 2 seconds each. Payments gets quiet time to recover instead of a queue of retries, and checkout can serve a fallback: a cached answer, or payment pending.

@7
The 5 second timer elapses, and the breaker goes half-open, ready to let exactly one trial call through.

@8
The trial, call 6, times out: payments is still sick. The breaker opens again for another 5 seconds. Many implementations back this timer off exponentially.

@9
Meanwhile payments recovers. Five seconds later, the breaker goes half-open again.

@10
This trial, call 7, returns 200 OK in 45 milliseconds. The breaker closes, resets its counter, and traffic flows normally.

@11
The pitfalls. A raw count of three trips on a single flaky burst; use a failure rate over a sliding window, with a minimum volume. Use one breaker per dependency, or a broken refund endpoint takes down charges too. And a breaker without a fallback still fails the user, just faster.

@12
So the breaker gives up the few calls that might have succeeded while it was open, in exchange for freeing the caller's resources and giving the dependency room to recover. It makes failure explicit and fast, and that is what lets the rest of the system stay up.
