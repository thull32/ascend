@0
One client, 999 others that failed at the same moment, and an overloaded service. Retries are cheap when the failure rate is low. They hurt when a dependency is down, because then every attempt fails, and with three tiers each retrying three times, the bottom tier receives 27 times its normal load. So retries must be spaced out. The schedule here is exponential backoff, doubling from 100 milliseconds, capped at 2 seconds, at most 4 attempts, with full jitter: each wait is a random value between zero and the schedule's delay.

@1-2
Attempt 1 fails with a 503, which is worth retrying. The schedule says 100 milliseconds, and jitter picks 24, so attempt 2 goes at 24 milliseconds.

@3-4
Attempt 2 fails. The schedule doubles to 200, jitter picks 183, and attempt 3 is due at 207 milliseconds.

@5-6
Attempt 3 fails. The schedule doubles to 400, jitter picks 245, and attempt 4 is due at 452. Notice the draws are not monotonic; that randomness is jitter doing its job.

@7
Attempt 4 succeeds, after 452 milliseconds of waiting, while the service recovered. Four immediate attempts would all have landed in the same overload.

@8
Now the herd. Without jitter, all 1,000 clients sleep exactly 100 milliseconds and come back in lockstep, hitting the recovering service with the same wave, now synchronised. The lesson's simulation of exactly this took 32.8 seconds to serve everyone, arriving in waves of 900, 800 and 700 with idle gaps between them.

@9
With full jitter, the same retries spread across the whole interval, a ramp the service can actually serve. In the simulation, every jittered variant finished in 2 to 4 seconds.

@10
Two more rules. Cap retries with a budget, a percentage of traffic, so a full outage cannot multiply the load. And retry only what is idempotent, or a payment that timed out but succeeded is charged twice.

@11
Backoff bounds the extra load, jitter spreads it out, and a budget plus a circuit breaker stop it altogether when the failure is not transient. Without them, the retry load alone can keep a server saturated after the original trigger is gone, which is how a metastable failure starts.
