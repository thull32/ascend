@0
Once data flows, the second limit appears. A sender may have only one window of unacknowledged data in flight. Here the window is 3 frames, and the protocol is Go-Back-N, so this picture also shows what a loss does to a window.

@1
The sender sends frames 0, 1 and 2, and its window is full. Frame 1 is lost. Whatever the link speed, the sender may send nothing more until an acknowledgement comes back.

@2
Frame 0 arrives, and the receiver acknowledges it. Frame 2 arrives out of order, and Go-Back-N's receiver, which keeps no buffer, throws it away.

@3
The acknowledgement for frame 0 frees one slot, so the window slides by one, and the sender may now send frame 3. The ACK clock is the only thing that lets new data out.

@4
The receiver still wants frame 1, so it discards frame 3 too and repeats its acknowledgement of 0.

@5
The timer for frame 1 expires, and the sender goes back to it.

@6
It resends frames 1, 2 and 3.

@7
All three arrive in order, and a single acknowledgement, 3, covers them.

@8
The rule to carry away: throughput is at most the window divided by the round trip time. The pipe holds bandwidth times round trip, the bandwidth-delay product, and the window must be at least that big or the link sits idle while the sender waits. A 1 gigabit per second link with a 70 millisecond round trip holds 8.75 megabytes. TCP's original 16-bit window field tops out at 65,535 bytes, which on that path caps one connection at about 7.5 megabits per second, under 1 percent of the link. Window scaling exists for exactly this, and the ceiling returns whenever something caps the window, such as a small receive buffer or a slow reader.
