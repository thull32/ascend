@0
Once data flows, the second limit appears. A sender may have only one window of unacknowledged data in flight. Here the window is 3 frames, and nothing is lost. The link itself could carry 8 frames in one round trip: that is its bandwidth-delay product, the amount of data the pipe holds.

@1
The sender sends frames 0, 1 and 2 back to back, and its window is full: 3 frames in flight, out of the 8 the link could have carried this round trip.

@2
Now nothing more may leave until an acknowledgement comes back. The sender sits idle for the rest of the round trip, and five eighths of the link go unused. A faster link would not help: it would only make the idle gap longer.

@3
One round trip after frame 0 left, the cumulative acknowledgement for frames 0 to 2 arrives, and the window slides forward by 3. The ACK clock is the only thing that lets new data out.

@4
The sender sends the next window, frames 3, 4 and 5, and idles again.

@5
Their acknowledgement arrives a round trip later. Three frames per round trip, every round trip.

@6
That is the rule to carry away: throughput is at most the window divided by the round trip time. Here it is 3 frames per round trip, 37.5 percent of what the link could carry, whatever its speed. To fill the pipe, the window must reach the bandwidth-delay product, 8 frames here. A 1 gigabit per second link with a 70 millisecond round trip holds 8.75 megabytes. TCP's original 16-bit window field tops out at 65,535 bytes, which on that path caps one connection at about 7.5 megabits per second, under 1 percent of the link. Window scaling exists for exactly this, and the ceiling returns whenever something caps the window, such as a small receive buffer or a slow reader.
