@0
A new TCP connection does not start at the bandwidth-delay product. It starts with a small congestion window and doubles it every round trip. For readability this picture starts at one segment, with a slow start threshold of 16. Real Linux stacks start at 10 segments, about 14.5 kilobytes. The chart plots the window once per round trip.

@1-2
Round trip 1: one segment out, one acknowledgement back, and the window doubles to 2.

@3-6
Round trip 2 sends 2 segments, and the window becomes 4. Round trip 3 sends 4, and it becomes 8.

@7-8
Round trip 4 sends 8, and the window doubles to 16, reaching the threshold.

@9-16
From here, growth is one segment per round trip: 16 segments, then 17, 18, 19. The steep doubling curve flattens into a line.

@17
Here is why this is a latency cost, not just a throughput detail. The lesson traces a 1 megabyte response on a fresh connection starting from 10 segments: 14.5 kilobytes in the first flight, then 29, 58, 116, 232, 463, and the megabyte completes only in the seventh flight. That is 7 round trips, 350 milliseconds at 50 milliseconds each, for an object a 1 gigabit link could serialise in 8 milliseconds. Small objects finish while the window is still tiny, so their cost is round trips. And a reused connection skips the ramp, but only if the kernel does not shrink the window again after an idle second, which is why servers with long-lived connections turn off slow start after idle.
