@0
At the transport layer, corruption looks exactly like loss. A receiving TCP that finds a bad checksum drops the segment silently, telling no one. So watch this as a story about a corrupted segment, even though the picture draws it as lost: from the sender's side, the two are the same event.

@1
Four segments go out. The one at sequence 1001 never makes it into the receiver's buffer: lost on the wire, or delivered with a flipped bit and discarded when its checksum failed.

@2
Sequence 1 arrives intact, and the receiver acknowledges 1001.

@3-4
Sequences 2001 and 3001 arrive and are buffered, but the acknowledgement cannot move past the hole, so the receiver repeats 1001 twice. Duplicate acknowledgements one and two.

@5
The third duplicate tells the sender that one segment is missing while later ones get through.

@6
It retransmits sequence 1001 immediately.

@7
The fresh copy arrives with a good checksum, and the acknowledgement jumps to 4001.

@8
Nothing anywhere logged an error. Corruption shows up as retransmissions, not as failures, and the drop is visible only in a counter of checksum errors on the receiving host. That is the deeper point. The Ethernet CRC is strong but recomputed at every hop, so corruption inside a router gets a fresh, valid CRC. Between two hosts, the only end-to-end check below TLS is TCP's weak 16-bit checksum. The end-to-end argument follows: only a check performed by the endpoints can guarantee correctness, so checksum data where it is created and verify it where it is consumed.
