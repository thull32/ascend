@0
TCP is neither pure Go-Back-N nor pure Selective Repeat. It took the cheap part of each. Its acknowledgements are cumulative, like Go-Back-N: the ACK field always says the next byte I expect. But its receiver buffers segments that arrive after a hole, like Selective Repeat. Watch both behaviours in one loss.

@1
The sender transmits four segments of 1,000 bytes. The one at sequence 1001 is lost.

@2
Sequence 1 arrives, and the receiver acknowledges 1001.

@3
Sequence 2001 arrives after the hole. The receiver keeps it, which is the Selective Repeat half, but its acknowledgement cannot move past the gap, so it repeats 1001, which is the Go-Back-N half. Duplicate number 1.

@4
Sequence 3001 is buffered too: duplicate number 2. The SACK option would make this acknowledgement selective, listing the range held beyond the hole, 2001 to 4000.

@5
Duplicate number 3. Three duplicates mean later data is arriving and exactly one segment is missing.

@6
So the sender fast-retransmits only sequence 1001, without waiting for its timer, and halves its congestion window instead of resetting it.

@7
The hole is filled, and the cumulative acknowledgement jumps to 4001, past everything the receiver had buffered. No good segment was sent twice.

@8
A timeout is the expensive path: at least 200 milliseconds of waiting, and the congestion window back to one segment. Fast retransmit and SACK keep the pipe full after an isolated loss. And this one sliding window does three jobs at once: reliability, flow control through the receive window, and congestion control through the congestion window.
