@0
A sender and a receiver, and a window of 3: the sender may have up to three unacknowledged frames in flight. Go-Back-N keeps the receiver as simple as possible. It tracks one number, the frame it expects next, delivers only that frame, throws everything else away, and acknowledges cumulatively. The sender runs a single timer for its oldest unacknowledged frame, and when it fires, the sender goes back to that frame and resends everything from there.

@1
The sender fills its window: frames 0, 1 and 2. Frame 1 is lost.

@2
Frame 0 arrives in order, and the receiver acknowledges 0. Frame 2 arrives out of order, and because Go-Back-N keeps no buffer, the receiver discards it, even though nothing was wrong with it.

@3
The acknowledgement for 0 slides the window forward to start at frame 1, so the sender sends frame 3. It is still waiting for an acknowledgement of frame 1.

@4
The receiver still expects frame 1, so it discards frame 3 too and repeats its acknowledgement of 0, a duplicate.

@5
Then the timer for frame 1 expires. The sender goes back to frame 1.

@6
It resends frames 1, 2 and 3. Frames 2 and 3 cross the link for a second time, though they arrived perfectly well the first time. Selective Repeat would resend only frame 1, because its receiver would have buffered 2 and 3.

@7
All three arrive in order, and one cumulative acknowledgement, 3, covers them.

@8
So Go-Back-N trades wasted retransmissions for a receiver with no buffer and a sender with one timer. In the lesson's trace, ten frames with one loss cost fourteen transmissions. And whichever variant you use, the window bounds throughput: the bandwidth times the round trip must fit inside the window, or the link sits idle while the sender waits for acknowledgements.
