@0
Map the hash space onto a circle, here drawn as positions from 0 to 359 degrees. Servers sit on it, keys hash onto it, and a key belongs to the first server at or after its position, walking clockwise and wrapping past the top. Four servers sit evenly spaced: N1 at 55 degrees, N2 at 145, N3 at 235 and N4 at 325.

@1
User 1 hashes to 159 degrees. Walking clockwise, the first server is N3, at 235.

@2
User 7 lands at 124, before N2, so N2 owns it.

@3
Video 9 lands at 35 degrees and goes to N1, at 55.

@4
Session 3 lands at 73, past N1, so it walks on to N2.

@5-6
Cart 5 at 322 goes to N4, at 325, and feed 2 at 175 goes to N3.

@7
All six keys are placed: one on N1, two on N2, two on N3 and one on N4. Each server owns the arc that ends at its own position.

@8
Now add N5, which joins at 98 degrees, between N1 at 55 and N2 at 145. It takes over only that arc, and every key on it moves to N5: here just session 3, which leaves N2. That is 1 of 6 keys, and no key moves between the old servers. With hash mod N, going from four servers to five would have moved 5 of these 6 keys, between all the servers.

@9
The ideal is that the newcomer takes its fair share, about a fifth of the keys, and nothing else moves. With one point per server, how much it takes depends on where it lands, and real points land at random: with ten servers placed at random, the unluckiest expects about 29 percent of the keys instead of 10, and a dead server dumps its whole arc on one successor. So real systems give each server around 100 to 200 virtual nodes, scattered around the ring, which evens out the shares and spreads a failed server's keys across many neighbours. Dynamo, Cassandra, Memcached clients and CDNs all do this.
