@0
Map the hash space onto a circle. Each server hashes to a point on it, and so does each key, and a key belongs to the first server clockwise from it, wrapping past the top. Here are four servers: N4 at 159 degrees, N2 at 161, N3 at 342 and N1 at 344.

@1-2
User 1 hashes to 187 degrees, and user 7 to 181. Walking clockwise from either, the first server is N3, at 342.

@3
Video 9 lands at 345 degrees, past every server, so the walk wraps around the top to the first server on the circle, N4 at 159.

@4-6
Session 3 at 164 degrees, cart 5 at 294, and feed 2 at 165 all walk clockwise to N3. Look at the ring now. N3 owns five of the six keys, because its arc, from N2 at 161 all the way to 342, covers half the circle. N1 and N2 own almost nothing.

@7
Now add N5 at 340 degrees. It takes over the arc from its predecessor, N2 at 161, up to 340, and every key on that arc moves to N5, all five that N3 held. No other key moves: video 9 stays on N4. Here that is 5 of 6 keys, because this one arc was enormous, but the rule is what matters: only keys on one arc move, and they all move to the new server. With hash mod N, nearly every key would move, between all the servers.

@8
That lopsided ring is exactly the problem a single point per server causes. With ten servers placed at random, the unluckiest expects about 29 percent of the keys instead of 10, and a dead server dumps its whole arc on one successor. So real systems give each server around 100 to 200 virtual nodes, scattered around the ring, which evens out the shares and spreads a failed server's keys across many neighbours. Dynamo, Cassandra, Memcached clients and CDNs all do this.
