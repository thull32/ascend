@0
Link-state routing in two moves. First, every router floods a description of its own links and their costs to every other router. Second, each router runs Dijkstra on the resulting map, which is identical everywhere. On screen, four routers: A, B, C and D.

@1
A floods its link-state advertisement: B at cost 1, C at cost 4.

@2
B passes A's advertisement on, and adds its own: A at 1, C at 2, D at 5. Each advertisement carries a sequence number. A router installs and re-floods only a newer copy, and drops a duplicate, which is what stops the flood from looping and stale data from winning.

@3
C passes on both, and adds its own: B at 2, D at 1. Flooding continues until every router holds every advertisement, an identical link-state database.

@4
Now each router computes its routes on its own. A to D, for example, is A, B, C, D, costing 1 plus 2 plus 1, which is 4, and every router reaches the same answer independently. The lesson's failure trace shows the payoff: when a link dies, the news reaches every router in a couple of milliseconds, bad news as fast as good. A flood crosses each link at most once in each direction. The price is more CPU and memory than distance-vector, though the Dijkstra run itself is the cheap part of convergence: detection, deliberate delays and programming the forwarding hardware take far longer.
