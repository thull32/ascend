@0
The same five routers and link costs, but now the distances are towards E, and the algorithm is Bellman-Ford: relax every edge, round after round, at most V minus 1 times, here 4. This is what distance-vector routing does, except that real routers run the rounds asynchronously and none of them ever sees the whole graph. E starts at 0, everything else at infinity.

@1
Round 1: only D is next to E. D becomes 3.

@2-3
Round 2: D's news spreads one hop. B, through D, becomes 3 plus 5, which is 8. C, through D, becomes 3 plus 1, which is 4.

@4
Round 3. A first hears from B: 8 plus 1, so A becomes 9.

@5
Then from C: 4 plus 4 is 8, better, so A becomes 8, via C.

@6
And C improves B too: 4 plus 2 is 6, better than 8. B now goes via C.

@7
Round 4: B's improvement reaches A. 6 plus 1 is 7, better than 8, so A now goes via B. Its round 3 answer was wrong, because B's better distance had not arrived yet.

@8
Nothing changes after that, so the distances are final: A 7, B 6, C 4, D 3, the same answers Dijkstra produced from A. Two lessons from the trace. Good news travels one hop per round, and the V minus 1 round bound is the worst case for that spreading. The cost is order of V times E. And Bellman-Ford tolerates negative weights and can detect negative cycles, which Dijkstra cannot.
