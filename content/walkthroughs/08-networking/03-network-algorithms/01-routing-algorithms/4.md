@0
Three routers, A, B and C, each knowing only the cost of its own links and the vectors its neighbours send. A starts with B at 1 and C at 4. B has A at 1 and C at 2. C has A at 4 and B at 2. Distance-vector routing is Bellman-Ford spread across routers: each one periodically tells its neighbours its best distance to every destination, and they relax.

@1
B advertises its vector to A: A at 1, itself at 0, C at 2.

@2
A relaxes: C through B costs 1 plus 2, which is 3, better than its direct link at 4. A's table now says C at 3, via B.

@3
B advertises the same vector to C.

@4
C relaxes the same way: A through B costs 2 plus 1, which is 3, better than its direct 4. C's table now says A at 3, via B.

@5
That is the whole protocol: simple and cheap on CPU. Its weakness is what happens when a link dies. The vectors carry no path, so a router cannot tell that a neighbour's advertised route runs back through itself, and routers can count up towards infinity, rerouting through each other in a loop. Split horizon and poison reverse stop a router from echoing a route to the neighbour it learned it from, which kills two-router loops, but the lesson's trace shows a three-router loop surviving them. The real fixes add information: hold-down timers, or EIGRP's feasibility condition. That is why link-state won inside big networks.
