@0
The name leaky bucket means two different algorithms. This is the queue version, a shaper: requests pour into a bucket that holds 4, and it leaks to the server at exactly 1 request per second. Arrivals that find it full spill over and are dropped. Whatever the input looks like, the output is perfectly steady.

@1-4
Six requests arrive together at time zero. The first four fit, at positions 1 to 4, and their arrival times at the server are already fixed: 0, 1, 2 and 3 seconds. The bucket is now full.

@5-6
Requests 5 and 6 find it full and are dropped. Queueing them would only add delay the client would not tolerate.

@7
At 1 second, one request has leaked out, leaving room. Request 7 takes position 4 and will reach the server at 4 seconds.

@8
Request 8, in the same second, finds the bucket full again and is dropped.

@9-10
At 3 seconds, two more have leaked. Requests 9 and 10 join, due at the server at 5 and 6 seconds.

@11-12
At 6 seconds, three more have leaked, and requests 11 and 12 join, due at 7 and 8.

@13
Now look at the times the requests reached the server: one per second, evenly spaced, even though six arrived at once at the start. The burst became queueing delay, not load.

@14
That is also the failure mode: the bucket hides overload as latency. Here a queued request can wait up to 3 seconds before it even starts. Size the bucket by the delay you can accept, not by the memory you have.

@15
So the trade: a leaky queue gives a perfectly smooth output, ideal in front of a fragile downstream, while a token bucket lets short bursts through at full speed. Used as a meter with no queue, a counter that fills per request and leaks at the rate, the leaky bucket is the token bucket upside down, with identical decisions. When someone says leaky bucket, ask whether requests wait or are rejected.
