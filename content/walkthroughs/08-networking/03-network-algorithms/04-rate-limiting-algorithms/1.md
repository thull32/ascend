@0
A client, a rate limiter and an API, with a limit of 3 requests per 10 seconds. The sliding window log keeps the timestamp of every accepted request. When a request arrives at time t, it drops every timestamp at or before t minus 10, and accepts only if fewer than 3 remain. The log starts empty.

@1-3
Requests arrive at 0, 2 and 4 seconds. Each finds fewer than 3 timestamps in its last 10 seconds, so each is accepted and recorded. The log now holds 0, 2 and 4: full.

@4
Request 4 arrives at 7 seconds. All three timestamps are within the last 10 seconds, so it is rejected. The limiter can even say when to come back: the oldest entry, at 0, expires at 10 seconds, and that is the Retry-After.

@5
Request 5, at 9 seconds, is rejected for the same reason.

@6
Request 6 arrives at 11 seconds. The timestamp at 0 is now more than 10 seconds old and is dropped, leaving 2 and 4. Two is fewer than 3, so request 6 is accepted, and the log holds 2, 4 and 11.

@7-10
The same pattern continues. At 12 seconds the entry at 2 expires and 12 is accepted. At 15 the entry at 4 expires and 15 is accepted. At 21 and 24 seconds, the oldest entries expire in turn and each request is accepted. Notice that rejected requests were never logged: log only accepted requests, or a client that keeps hammering never recovers.

@11
Why bother with a log? A fixed window counter that resets every 10 seconds would allow 3 requests at 9 seconds and 3 more at 10, six in one second, double the intended rate. Any 10-second interval you pick here contains at most 3 accepted requests. There is no boundary to game.

@12
The price is memory: one timestamp per accepted request, per client. Fine for 3 per 10 seconds, ruinous for 10,000 an hour across a million clients. The sliding window counter approximates the log with two counts, weighting the previous window by its overlap, and Redis-backed limiters use that or the token bucket.
