@0
What is the fewest coins that make exactly 11, with coins of 1, 2 and 5? The table holds one cell per amount, from 0 to 11. The recurrence splits every answer by its last coin: the best for amount a is 1 plus the best of the cells you reach by taking away one coin, for each coin that fits. The table is filled from 0 upwards, so every cell it reads is already final.

@1
The base case: amount 0 needs no coins.

@2-3
Amount 1 can only end with a 1 coin, on top of amount 0: one coin. Amount 2 can end with a 1 on top of amount 1, or a 2 on top of amount 0. The 2 wins: one coin.

@4-5
Amount 3 looks back at amounts 2 and 1, both worth one coin, so it takes two. Amount 4 does best with a 2 on top of amount 2: two coins.

@6
Amount 5 is the first cell where the 5 coin fits, and it reaches back to amount 0. One coin, beating the two-coin options from amounts 4 and 3.

@7-8
Amounts 6 and 7 build on that single 5: a 1 on top of 5 gives two coins for 6, and a 2 on top of 5 gives two coins for 7.

@9-10
Amounts 8 and 9 each need three coins: the best cells they can reach back to are worth two.

@11
Amount 10 reaches back to amount 5, which costs one coin, so 10 costs two: 5 plus 5.

@12
Amount 11 looks back at 10, 9 and 6, worth two, three and two. The first of the best is a 1 on top of amount 10, so the answer is three coins.

@13
Three coins: 1, 5 and 5, reconstructed by following the recorded last coin from 11 back to 0. There are amount plus one states, each scanning every coin, so the cost is the amount times the number of coins, with one array of space. That is the sentence to say in an interview: states times transition cost, both factors named.
