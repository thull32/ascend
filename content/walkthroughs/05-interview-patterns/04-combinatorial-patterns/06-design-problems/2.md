@0
The same cache of 3 keys, now least frequently used. Each key carries a count of its accesses, and when the cache is full, the key with the smallest count is evicted. When counts tie, the least recently used among them goes. Popularity beats recency.

@1-2
A misses and enters with frequency 1. A is read again, hits, and its count rises to 2. The more it is read, the harder it is to evict.

@3-5
B and C miss and enter at frequency 1. Then A hits again and climbs to 3.

@6
D misses, and the cache is full. B and C tie at frequency 1, so recency breaks the tie: B, last used at step 3, is evicted. A, with its count of 3, is never in danger.

@7
B comes back, misses, and enters at 1. Now C and D tie at 1, and C, from step 4, is older, so C goes.

@8
E misses. D and B tie at 1, and D, from step 6, is evicted.

@9
A hits and rises to 4.

@10
D comes back and misses. B and E tie at 1, and B, from step 7, is evicted. Notice the pattern: every new key enters at frequency 1, which makes it the first candidate for eviction, while A sits safely at the top.

@11
That is also the failure mode: no decay. A key that was hot last week keeps its high count and can never be evicted, while new keys churn at the bottom and never displace the stale top. Real LFU caches age their counters, halving them periodically, or use a logarithmic counter with decay, as Redis does.

@12
The trade-off: LFU protects genuinely popular keys from one-off scans that would flush an LRU, but it costs a counter per key, adapts slowly when popularity shifts, and needs decay. The constant-time version keeps keys in frequency buckets, each ordered by recency, and tracks the minimum frequency, which only changes when a touch empties the lowest bucket or a new key arrives at 1.
