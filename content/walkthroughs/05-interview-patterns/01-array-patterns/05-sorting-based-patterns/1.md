@0
Seven values: 7, 2, 9, 4, 1, 8 and 3. This is the step inside a sort call, and the same step quickselect is built on. Quicksort picks a pivot, here always the last element of the range, and partitions: everything smaller goes to the left, the pivot lands in its final place, and each side is sorted the same way.

@1-7
The first pivot is 3. A scan walks the range, keeping a region of values smaller than 3 at the front. 7 is not smaller, so it stays. 2 is smaller and is swapped to the front. 9 and 4 are left alone. 1 is smaller and joins the front region. 8 stays.

@8
Now the pivot is swapped in right after the small region, at index 2. That position is final: everything to its left is smaller, and everything to its right is at least 3. Quickselect would stop here if index 2 were the one it wanted, and otherwise recurse into one side only.

@9-11
Quicksort recurses into both sides. The left side, 2 and 1, partitions around 1: 2 is not smaller, so 1 moves to index 0, and 2 is left alone in place.

@12-16
The right side, positions 3 to 6, partitions around 9. Every value is smaller, so each one is swapped with itself, and 9 stays at the end, at index 6. This is the bad case in miniature: the pivot is the maximum, and the range shrinks by only one.

@17-23
It happens again. The range 3 to 5 partitions around 8, the largest value left, and then 3 to 4 around 7. Each pass places one pivot and peels one element off the range.

@24
Sorted: 1, 2, 3, 4, 7, 8, 9. On average quicksort is n log n and sorts in place, but the last part showed the trap: when the pivot is always the largest or smallest value, as with already-sorted input and a last-element pivot, it degrades to quadratic. And for one order statistic, such as the k-th largest, quickselect does this partition but recurses into only the side that holds the index it wants, which is linear on average.
