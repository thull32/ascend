@0
Ten values: 3, 1, 4, 1, 5, 9, 2, 6, 5 and 3. Every key is a small whole number between 0 and 9, and that is the signal for counting sort: a small key range needs no comparisons at all. Count each key, turn the counts into starting positions, then place each element.

@1-10
The first pass reads every value once and bumps the count for its key. When it finishes, the counts read: two 1s, one 2, two 3s, one 4, two 5s, one 6 and one 9, with nothing for 0, 7 or 8.

@11-20
The second pass runs a total over the keys in order. Each key's start is the number of values with a smaller key. Key 1 starts at 0. Key 2 starts at 2, after the two 1s. Key 3 at 3, key 4 at 5, key 5 at 6, key 6 at 8. Keys 7 and 8 do not occur and both start at 9, which is also where the 9 goes. The total ends at 10, the length of the input.

@21-25
The third pass walks the input in its original order and drops each value at its key's start, then moves that start on by one. The first 3 goes to position 3. The first 1 goes to position 0. The 4 goes to position 5. The second 1 lands at position 1, right after the first. The first 5 goes to position 6.

@26-30
The 9 goes to the last position, 9. The 2 to position 2. The 6 to position 8. The second 5 to position 7, and the second 3 to position 4, each right behind its twin.

@31
Sorted: 1, 1, 2, 3, 3, 4, 5, 5, 6, 9, in time proportional to the ten values plus the ten keys, without a single comparison. Because the placement pass runs in input order, equal keys keep their original order, which is what makes the sort stable, and stability is what matters when the keys are attached to records. The condition to check before using it: the key range must be small compared with the input, or the count array costs more than the sort.
