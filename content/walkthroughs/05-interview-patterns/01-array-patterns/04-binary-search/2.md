@0
Eight sorted values: 1, 3, 3, 5, 8, 8, 8 and 10, with three copies of 8, and we want the first one. The boundary form does not look for 8 at all. It asks a yes-no question of each position, is the value at least 8, which reads false four times and then true to the end, and it finds the first true. The range is half-open: low is 0, high is 8, one past the end. Everything below low is known false, everything at or above high is known true.

@1
The middle of 0 to 8 is position 4, which holds 8: true. It might be the first true, so it stays in range, and high moves down to 4. Notice the difference from the exact form: hitting an 8 does not end the search.

@2
The middle of 0 to 4 is position 2, which holds 3: false. Everything up to position 2 is false too, so low moves to 3.

@3
The middle of 3 to 4 is position 3, which holds 5: false. Low moves to 4.

@4
Low equals high at position 4: the first index where the value is at least 8, which is the leftmost 8. The exact form could have stopped on any of the three copies; the boundary form always lands on the first, and when 8 is absent it returns the position where 8 would be inserted. Change the question to greater than 8 and the same loop finds the slot just after the last 8, so the count of 8s is one subtraction.
