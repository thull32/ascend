@0
Six meetings, sorted by start: 1 to 5, 2 to 6, 4 to 8, 6 to 9, 8 to 10 and 9 to 12. How many rooms do they need? The heap holds the end times of the meetings in progress, one per occupied room, and because it is a min-heap its root is always the meeting that ends first. Before each meeting starts, every end time at or before its start is popped: those rooms are free. Then the new meeting's end is pushed. The number of rooms in use is the heap's size, and the answer is its peak.

@1
The meeting from 1 to 5 starts in an empty heap. Push its end, 5. One room.

@2-3
The meeting from 2 to 6: the earliest end, 5, is after 2, so nothing is free. Push 6; it is not smaller than its parent 5, so it stays. Two rooms.

@4-5
The meeting from 4 to 8: 5 is still after 4. Push 8. Three rooms, and that is the peak: at any time between 4 and 5, all three meetings are running.

@6-8
The meeting from 6 to 9 starts, and now two rooms are free. First pop 5, which ended at 5. The last leaf, 8, moves to the root and sinks below 6.

@9-10
The new root, 6, is also at or before the start time of 6, so pop it too: a meeting that ends at 6 frees its room for one that starts at 6. Eight is left alone.

@11-12
Push this meeting's end, 9. Two rooms in use.

@13-16
The meeting from 8 to 10: the root, 8, has ended, so pop it, and push 10. Still two rooms.

@17-20
The meeting from 9 to 12: pop 9, push 12. The heap holds 10 and 12.

@21
The size never went above 3, so three rooms suffice, and three are needed, because three meetings overlap. Every push and every pop walks one root-to-leaf path, so with the sort the whole thing is n log n. Two details carry the solution. The eviction is a loop, not a single check, so that the heap's size is always the number of rooms in use right now, which a follow-up question about any given time depends on. And the test is end at or before start, so back-to-back meetings share a room.
