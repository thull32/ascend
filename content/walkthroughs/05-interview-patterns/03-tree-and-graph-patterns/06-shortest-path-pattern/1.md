@0
Five nodes, A to E, joined by one-way edges with non-negative weights, and we want the cheapest route from A to every node, E included. Each node is labelled with its best distance so far, and all of them start at infinity except A, at 0. Dijkstra's rule: always settle the closest unsettled node. With non-negative weights, its distance can never improve afterwards.

@1-3
Pop A at distance 0 and settle it. Relax its edges: B drops from infinity to 4, and C from infinity to 1. Both go into the min-heap with those distances.

@4
The heap's smallest entry is C, at 1, so C is settled. Nothing can reach C more cheaply, because any other route would have to leave A by the edge to B, which already costs 4.

@5-6
Relax C's edges. Going to B through C costs 1 plus 2, which is 3, better than 4, so B improves to 3 and is pushed again. D goes from infinity to 1 plus 5, which is 6. Notice the old entry for B at 4 is still in the heap. Neither Python's heapq nor JavaScript has a decrease-key, so the old entry simply stays, and is skipped as stale if it ever surfaces.

@7-8
The smallest real entry is B at 3. Settle it. Its edge to D costs 1, so D improves from 6 to 4.

@9-10
The stale entry for B, at 4, surfaces first; B is already settled, so it is popped and discarded. Then pop D at 4 and settle it. D's edge to E makes E 4 plus 3, which is 7.

@11
The stale D at 6 is discarded the same way, then pop E at 7, and every node is settled.

@12
The shortest-path tree is highlighted: A to C, C to B, B to D, D to E. The direct edges from A to B and from C to D were never part of a shortest path. With a binary heap the cost is vertices plus edges, times log of the vertices. The whole proof rests on non-negative weights: one negative edge, and a settled node can turn out to be wrong, which is when you switch to Bellman-Ford.
