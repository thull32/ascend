@0
Six nodes, A to F, and five edges that arrive one at a time. Union-find keeps one parent pointer per node, shown as its label, and every node starts as its own root. Two nodes are connected exactly when following parent pointers up from each reaches the same root. Each edge is a union: find both roots, and if they differ, hang one under the other.

@1-2
The first edge joins A and B. Their roots are A and B themselves. Union by rank attaches the shorter tree under the taller one; here both are single nodes, a tie, so B goes under A. B's parent is now A.

@3-4
The edge from C to D does the same: two separate roots, a tie, and D goes under C. There are now two small trees, rooted at A and C.

@5-6
The edge from B to C is the interesting one. Union never links the nodes it was given; it links their roots. Find walks up from B to A, and C is already a root. So C's whole tree, with D in it, is hung under A. A is now the root of A, B, C and D.

@7-8
E and F are joined the same way, with E as the root, in a group of their own.

@9-10
The last edge joins A and D. Find from A gives A, and find from D gives A too. Same root: they are already connected. This edge adds nothing, and in a graph it would close a cycle, which is precisely how Redundant Connection finds its answer.

@11
Two groups remain, A, B, C and D under A, and E and F under E. With path compression and union by rank, each operation costs amortised inverse Ackermann of n, which is at most 4 for any input you will ever meet: effectively constant. That is why union-find beats re-running a traversal after every edge, which would cost the whole graph each time.
