We want the lowest common ancestor of 2 and 8 in this binary search tree, which has 6 at the root, 2 and 8 below it, and 0, 4, 7, 9, 3 and 5 further down. In a search tree you never need to search both sides. Walk down from the root: if both targets are smaller than the current node, go left; if both are larger, go right. The first node that is neither strictly above nor strictly below both of them splits them, and that node is the answer.

@1
Here the walk ends at the root. 2 is at most 6, and 8 is at least 6, so the targets lie in different subtrees, and 6 is the lowest common ancestor. The same rule covers the case where one target is the node itself. The cost is the height, with no recursion needed. In a general binary tree, with no ordering to steer by, you fall back to the bottom-up version: return the node if it is one of the targets, otherwise whichever child found something, or this node if both did.
