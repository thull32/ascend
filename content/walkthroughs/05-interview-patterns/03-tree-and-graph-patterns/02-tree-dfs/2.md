@0
Is this a valid binary search tree? It has 8 at the root, 3 and 10 below it, and 1, 6, 14, 4, 7 and 13 further down. Checking each node only against its parent is the classic mistake, because it misses a violation deeper down. Instead every node must sit strictly between a lower and an upper bound, and those bounds come from all its ancestors.

@1
The root has no ancestors, so its range is minus infinity to plus infinity, and 8 fits. Its left child inherits minus infinity to 8, and its right child inherits 8 to plus infinity.

@2
3 sits between minus infinity and 8. Its left child inherits minus infinity to 3, and its right child inherits 3 to 8. That 8 came from the root, not from 3.

@3
1 fits between minus infinity and 3. It is a leaf, so its children's ranges are never used.

@4
6 must sit between 3 and 8, and it does. Here two bounds from two different ancestors are in force at once: 3 from its parent, 8 from the root.

@5-6
4 must sit between 3 and 6, and 7 between 6 and 8. Both fit. A 9 in the place of 7 would have satisfied its parent 6, and failed only against the 8 inherited from the root.

@7-9
The right side works the same way. 10 sits between 8 and plus infinity. 14 sits between 10 and plus infinity. 13, as the left child of 14, must sit between 10 and 14, and it does.

@10
Every node respects its bounds, so this is a valid binary search tree. Two numbers carried down the recursion encode the constraint from every ancestor, in linear time. The equivalent check is an in-order walk that confirms the keys are strictly increasing. And before writing either, ask whether duplicates are allowed, and on which side.
