@0
Six nodes, 1 through 6, and the tail, 6, points back to 3. A plain traversal would go round 3, 4, 5, 6 forever. Floyd's algorithm uses two pointers from the head instead of a visited set: slow moves one node per step, fast moves two. The invariant: after t steps, slow is t steps from the head and fast is 2t.

@1
Step 1: slow is on 2, fast on 3.

@2
Step 2: slow is on 3, the entry of the loop, and fast is on 5. Both are now inside the loop, which has four nodes. Measured forward along the loop, fast is two nodes behind slow.

@3
Step 3: slow moves to 4, fast goes 6 and round to 3. Each step slow moves one and fast moves two, so the gap shrinks by exactly one. Fast is now one node behind.

@4
Step 4: slow moves to 5, and fast goes 4, 5. They land on the same node. A gap that falls by exactly one per step cannot jump over zero, so once both are in a loop they must meet, and they can only meet if there is a loop. Cycle detected, after 4 steps.

@5
Phase two finds where the loop starts. Slow goes back to the head, 1, fast stays on 5, and from now on both move one node per step. The head is two steps from the entry, and the meeting point, 5, is two steps past the entry. In the lesson's letters, a is 2, b is 2, and a plus b is 4, one whole lap of the loop.

@6
One step: slow on 2, fast on 6.

@7
Two steps: slow reaches 3, and fast, from 6, wraps round to 3 as well. They meet on the entry, exactly as the proof says: after a steps, the pointer from the head is on the entry, and the other has gone the rest of the way round the lap. Linear time, constant space. And the pointers are compared by identity, never by value: on a list with repeated values, equal values are not the same node.
