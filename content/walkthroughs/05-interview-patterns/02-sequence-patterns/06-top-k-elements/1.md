@0
We want the 2 largest of 3, 2, 1, 5, 6 and 4, and we keep them in a min-heap of size 2. That sounds backwards, so here is the idea that makes it work: the root is the smallest of the best 2 seen so far, which makes it the second largest so far. It is the only value a newcomer has to beat.

@1-3
The heap is not full yet, so the first two values go straight in. 3 lands at the root. 2 is appended as its child, and because 2 is smaller than its parent 3, it sifts up and the two swap. The root is now 2.

@4
1 arrives. The heap is full, so 1 is compared with the root and nothing else. It is not larger than 2, the smallest of the current top 2, so it is rejected in one comparison. That is the cheap path, and on random input it is the common one.

@5-7
5 arrives and beats the root 2. So 2 leaves the top 2 for good: 5 overwrites the root and sifts down. Its only child, 3, is smaller, so they swap, and 5 settles at the leaf. The root is now 3.

@8-10
6 beats the root 3. Same move: 6 overwrites the root, sifts down past 5, and settles. The root rises again, to 5.

@11
4 is compared with the root 5 and rejected.

@12
The heap holds 6 and 5, the two largest. Notice that the root only ever rose: 2, then 3, then 5. The kth largest of a growing set never decreases, so a value that falls below the threshold can be forgotten forever. That is why the pattern works on a stream. Each of n values costs one comparison, and an entry costs one sift of about log k levels, so the bound is n log k time with only k values of memory.
