@0
Every box on screen is one page of a database index. The root page holds two separator keys, 20 and 40, and they do nothing but route. The three leaves below hold the actual keys in sorted order: 5, 10, 15; then 20, 25, 30; then 40, 45, 50. Each leaf can hold four keys. The idea to hold onto: every leaf sits at the same depth, so every lookup reads the same number of pages.

@1
Look up 45. Read the root and binary-search its separators: 45 is at least 20 and at least 40, so follow the third pointer. That is one page read.

@2
Read leaf 3, binary-search it, and 45 is there. Two page reads in total, and the root is always in memory, so only one of them touches the disk. Real pages hold hundreds of keys, not four, and with a fan-out of around 300 a tree only four levels deep indexes billions of rows.

@3
Now a range scan, everything from 25 to 45. Descend once, to leaf 2, read forward, then follow the sibling pointer straight across to leaf 3. The leaves are linked, so a range or an ordered read is a sequential walk, and it never climbs back to the root.

@4
Insert 42. The root routes it to leaf 3, which has room, so 42 slides into place between 40 and 45, and the leaf is now full at four keys. Nothing else changes.

@5
Insert 47. It also belongs in leaf 3, which would now hold five keys, one over its capacity. The page must split.

@6
The split: 40, 42 and 45 stay in leaf 3, and 47 and 50 move to a new leaf 4. A copy of the new leaf's smallest key, 47, goes up into the root as a third separator. If the root had overflowed too, it would have split, and the tree would grow one level taller from the top. That is why all the leaves always stay at the same depth.

@7
Insert 12. Routed to leaf 1, which has room: 5, 10, 12, 15. Most inserts look like this one, a single page change.

@8
Three things go wrong in practice. A split writes several pages, so a crash halfway through needs the write-ahead log to keep the tree consistent. Random keys split pages all over the tree and leave them about 70 percent full. And ever-increasing keys, like auto-increment IDs, pile every insert onto the rightmost leaf, where they queue for that one page's latch.

@9
The trade: logarithmic reads with very few page fetches, cheap in-order scans, and updates in place, paid for with a whole page written per change and random writes when keys arrive in random order. LSM trees make the opposite bet: sequential writes, more work on every read.
