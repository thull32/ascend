@0
This is a plain binary search tree with no balancing at all, but the keys arrive in a shuffled order: 50, 20, 70, 10, 30, 60, 80, 25 and 65. Keep in mind why it is here. A treap is exactly the search tree you get by inserting its keys in order of their random priorities, so this is the shape a treap guarantees, whatever order the keys really arrive in.

@1-3
50 is the root. 20 is smaller, so it becomes the left child; 70 is larger, so it becomes the right child. Two keys, and both sides of the root are already in use.

@4-7
10 goes left at 50 and left again at 20, landing at depth 2. 30 goes left at 50, then right at 20, also at depth 2. The left side is now a full little tree.

@8-11
The right side fills the same way: 60 lands as 70's left child, 80 as its right child. Seven keys, three full levels, and not one rotation was needed. A random order happened to split the keys evenly at every node.

@12-14
25 walks left at 50, right at 20, and attaches under 30 at depth 3.

@15-17
65 walks right at 50, left at 70, and attaches under 60, also at depth 3.

@18
Nine keys in four levels, with the deepest keys only three links below the root. Compare the sorted input from the red-black lesson, which built a chain as tall as the number of keys. Each insert costs the height of the tree, and random order gives an expected depth of about 1.39 times log n. The weakness is that real input is rarely random. A treap removes that weakness by drawing a random priority for every key and rotating to keep the priorities in heap order, which simulates this random insertion order no matter what order the keys actually arrive in.
