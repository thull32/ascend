@0
H3 tiles the map with hexagons, and every hexagon has six neighbours, all sharing an edge, all the same distance away. On screen, each hexagon is a node: the centre cell c, its inner ring a1 to a6, and an outer ring b1 to b12. The question is H3's grid disk: which cells are within k steps of c? The answer is a breadth-first search, which explores cells in order of distance.

@1-7
Dequeue c, at distance 0. Its six neighbours are discovered, a6, a5, a1, a4, a2 and a3, each at distance 1, and each joins the queue.

@8-11
Dequeue a6. Its unseen neighbours, b12, b11 and b1, are discovered at distance 2. Neighbours already discovered are not added twice.

@12-25
The rest of the inner ring does the same. a5 adds b10 and b9. a1 adds b2 and b3. a4 adds b8 and b7. a2 adds b4 and b5. And a3 finds only one new cell, b6, because its other neighbours were already claimed. Twelve cells at distance 2.

@26-37
Now the queue drains the outer ring, b12 through b6, each at distance 2. They have nothing new to discover here.

@38
Every cell was dequeued once and every edge examined once, so the cost is linear in cells plus edges, and each label is the true number of steps from c. Grid disk of 1 is the first 7 cells, and grid disk of 2 is all 19. Because all six neighbours are equidistant, those rings are nearly circles, with no diagonal neighbours at a different distance as a square grid has. That is the main reason Uber gives for using hexagons in surge pricing and dispatch.
