@0
A sparse table answers any range maximum, in any order. But when the ranges are windows of a fixed size that only ever slide right, you do not need it. Here are eight values, 5, 2, 4, 7, 1, 3, 6 and 8, and a window of three. The tool is a deque, kept in decreasing order from front to back. Hold onto that rule, because it means the front is always the maximum of the current window.

@1-2
5 goes in at the back. Then 2, which is smaller, so the order is still decreasing: 5, then 2.

@3-5
Now 4 arrives, and it is bigger than the 2 at the back. As long as 4 is in the window, 2 can never be the maximum, and 2 will leave the window first. So 2 is evicted from the back, and 4 joins behind 5. The first window, positions 0 to 2, is complete, and its maximum is the front: 5.

@6-9
7 arrives and sweeps the deque clean. It beats 4, so 4 goes, and it beats 5, so 5 goes too. 7 sits alone, and the window from 1 to 3 records a maximum of 7.

@10-14
1 is smaller, so it queues behind 7, and window 2 to 4 records 7. Then 3 arrives, evicts the 1, and queues behind 7. Window 3 to 5 records 7 again. The front never changed, so each answer was a single look.

@15-18
6 arrives and evicts the 3. But now the window is positions 4 to 6, and the 7 at the front sits at position 3, outside it. So 7 expires from the front, 6 is the new front, and the window records 6.

@19-21
Finally 8 evicts the 6, stands alone, and the last window, 5 to 7, records 8.

@22
The answers are 5, 7, 7, 7, 6 and 8. Every index entered the deque once and left at most once, so the whole pass is linear in the length of the array, with no table to build. The catch is that this exploits the order of the queries, not any property of maximum. Give it ranges in arbitrary order and the trick is gone; then you need a sparse table, which relies on maximum being idempotent, or a segment tree.
