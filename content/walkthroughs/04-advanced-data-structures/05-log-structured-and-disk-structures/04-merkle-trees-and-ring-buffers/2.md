@0
Two threads on two cores, each incrementing its own counter, a and b. In a ring buffer, think of them as the producer's tail and the consumer's head. The threads never touch each other's data. But a and b sit 8 bytes apart, inside the same 64-byte cache line, and the hardware tracks ownership per line, not per byte. That one fact is the whole bug.

@1
Core 0 writes a. To write, it must own the whole line, so it takes the line in the modified state. Core 1, wanting to write b, has to wait for it.

@2-6
Core 1 writes b: it invalidates core 0's copy and pulls the line across. Core 0 stalls. Then core 0 wants a again, invalidates core 1's copy, and pulls it back, about 35 nanoseconds per transfer between cores. And again, and again. The line ping-pongs between the cores, although the two counters have nothing to do with each other.

@7
Count it: in 6 ticks the two cores completed 6 writes and forced 5 invalidations. Each core spends most of its time waiting for the line. This is false sharing: sharing by address, not by intent.

@8-11
The fix is padding. Give a its own 64-byte line, with an alignment attribute or a padding array. Now each core holds its own line in the modified state and writes at full speed, two writes per tick, with no coherence traffic at all. Tick after tick, both cores write locally, and nothing is invalidated.

@12
Shared line: 6 writes in 6 ticks. Padded: 8 writes in 4 ticks. Watch for it whenever per-thread counters, locks or flags live side by side in one array or struct. It is invisible in the code. You find it with a profiler such as perf c2c, and you fix it with padding or with truly thread-local data, which is exactly why the Disruptor pads its sequence counters.
