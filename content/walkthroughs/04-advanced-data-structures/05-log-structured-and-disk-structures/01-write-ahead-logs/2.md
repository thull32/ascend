@0
Once you have a durable, ordered log of every change, replication comes almost free. A leader accepts all the writes, and two followers receive its log and apply it in order, with the same redo path a database uses for crash recovery. Right now all three agree: x is 1, at log position 7, and the followers' lag is zero.

@1-2
The client writes x equals 2. The leader appends it to its log as position 8 and applies it. In asynchronous mode, it acknowledges the client as soon as its own log is durable, before any follower has the change.

@3
Then it streams position 8 to both followers. On a good day that takes a few milliseconds; under load, or across regions, it can be seconds.

@4-5
Reads are spread across the followers, and the client reads x from follower 1, which has not applied position 8 yet. It gets back 1. The client just wrote 2 and reads 1: its own write seems to have vanished. That is replication lag, breaking read-your-writes.

@6-7
A few milliseconds later both followers apply position 8 and catch up. The system is eventually consistent; the window is real but usually short. Two fixes close it: send a client that just wrote to the leader for its reads, or have the follower wait until it has applied at least the client's last write position.

@8-10
Now switch to synchronous mode for follower 1. Usually only one follower is synchronous, so one slow follower cannot stall every write. The client writes x equals 3, the leader logs position 9, ships it to both followers, and waits.

@11-12
Follower 1 confirms that position 9 is on its disk, and only then does the client hear OK. The write now exists on two machines, and the price is one extra network round trip on every commit.

@13-14
Then the leader dies. Something, a coordinator or the nodes themselves through consensus, must pick the most up-to-date follower, promote it, and point clients at it. Follower 1 is promoted. With asynchronous replication, any writes the old leader acknowledged but had not shipped are gone; with synchronous replication they are safe. And if the old leader comes back still believing it leads, two nodes accept writes, so fence it off first.

@15
The trade: asynchronous gives low write latency and tolerates slow followers, but can lose acknowledged writes. Synchronous guarantees a second copy, at the cost of latency, and of availability whenever that follower is slow. Reads scale out; writes do not.
