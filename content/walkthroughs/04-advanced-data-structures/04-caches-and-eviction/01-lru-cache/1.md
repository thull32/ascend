@0
An app, a cache, and a database behind it. This cache holds three keys, and the table lists them most recently used first. The rule, which is the whole policy: every access moves a key to the front, and when the cache is full, the key at the back, the one untouched for longest, is evicted. The requests will be A, B, C, A, D.

@1-3
A is not cached, a miss, so it is loaded from the database and placed at the front. Then B, also a miss, goes in front of A. Then C goes in front of both. The cache is full: C, B, A, with A at the back.

@4
Now A again, and this time it is a hit. A jumps from the back to the front. Reads update recency, not just writes, so the order is now A, C, B, and B is the least recently used.

@5
D is a miss, and the cache is full. The key at the back is B, last used at step 2, so B is evicted and D is loaded and placed at the front. Notice what saved A: one read at step 4. Without it, A would have been the victim.

@6
Here is where recency alone fails. A one-off scan, a report or a crawler reading a handful of keys once each, marches every key through the front of the list and pushes every genuinely hot key out the back, so the next real requests all miss. Variants like LRU-K, ARC, or a small probationary segment as in W-TinyLFU admit a key to the main cache only on its second touch.

@7
The trade: with a hash map and a doubly linked list, every operation is constant time, and LRU adapts instantly when the working set shifts, which is why it is the default almost everywhere. But it tracks recency only, so it cannot tell a key read a thousand times from one read once a moment ago. And the exact list costs memory per entry, which is why Redis approximates LRU by sampling five keys and evicting the oldest of those.
