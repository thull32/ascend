@0
MinHash measures how much two sets overlap. For vectors, the matching measure is the angle between them. Here are five words as tiny vectors of four numbers each. Real models use hundreds to thousands of unnamed dimensions; these four are labelled royal, male, female and food so you can see the geometry. King is high on royal and male, queen high on royal and female, man and woman are mostly male and mostly female, and apple is almost all food.

@1
First, lengths. King and queen have length 1.21, man and woman 0.93, apple 0.92. Length is not meaning, so similarity should ignore it and compare direction only. That is cosine similarity: the dot product of two vectors, divided by both their lengths.

@2-3
King against queen: a dot product of 0.98, divided by 1.21 times 1.21, gives 0.67. They share royalty but differ on gender. King's nearest neighbour is actually man, at 0.81, because the male dimension is so strong. By the same arithmetic, queen's nearest neighbour is woman, also 0.81.

@4-6
Man against woman is only 0.26, since they differ on the dimension that dominates both. And apple is far from everything: its best match, man, scores just 0.25. The food direction points somewhere else entirely.

@7
Directions carry relations, not just closeness. Take king, subtract man and add woman, and you get 0.90 royal, zero male, 0.90 female and 0.10 food. The nearest word to that vector is queen, with a cosine of 0.99.

@8
That is the whole of semantic search and retrieval-augmented generation: embed the query, compare it with the stored vectors, and return the closest few by cosine. Normalise every vector once, and cosine becomes a plain dot product. Random-hyperplane LSH turns the angle between two vectors into a collision probability; at scale, graph indexes like HNSW have mostly replaced it.
