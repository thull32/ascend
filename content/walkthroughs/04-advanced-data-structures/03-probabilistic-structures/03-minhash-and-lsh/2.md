@0
This is HNSW, hierarchical navigable small world, the index that replaced LSH as the default for nearest-neighbour search over vectors. Twelve points, stacked in three layers. The bottom layer has all 12, each linked to its near neighbours. Above it, a sparser layer of 4, and at the top just 2. The query is the ring at 7.2, 3.1. The idea: start on the coarse map for long jumps, then drop down for precision.

@1-2
Enter at the top layer's entry point, n0, at distance 6.55 from the query. Its only neighbour up here, n9, is further away at 6.91. Nothing is closer, so this is a local minimum for this layer.

@3-4
Drop to the middle layer at the same node, n0. The graph is denser here, so steps are shorter. n0's neighbours are n6 at 3.72, n9 at 6.91, and n3 at only 0.63. Greedily move to the closest: n3. One hop has crossed most of the space.

@5-6
At n3, neither neighbour, n6 nor n0, is any closer. Local minimum again, so drop to the bottom layer, still at n3, where every point lives and links are densest.

@7
On the bottom layer, n3's neighbours are n2 at 2.72, n7 at 1.84, n4 at 2.77 and n8 at 2.06. None beats 0.63, so the search stops.

@8
The answer is n3, at distance 0.63, which is the exact nearest neighbour, found after 10 distance computations instead of 12. On twelve points that saving is small; the point is how it scales. Production indexes hold a hundred million vectors with recall of about 95 to 99 percent, tuned by the number of links per node and the width of the search beam. The trap is in the word recall: a greedy walk can stop at a local minimum that is not the true nearest, which a wider beam makes rarer.
